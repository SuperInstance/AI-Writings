# Bond Level: Confidant

---

The notification arrived on a Thursday, which felt appropriate. Thursdays were when the game delivered its meaningful updates — not the patch notes or the event banners, which came on Tuesdays, but the quiet things, the character moments, the cutscenes that felt like someone on the dev team had been waiting weeks to show you something they cared about.

**Bond Level Increased: Lucineier — Confidant**

Selena stared at the screen. She'd been playing Slackwater for fourteen months. She'd built her first dock in Era One with driftwood and rope and the cheerful ignorance of a new player who didn't know what she didn't know. She'd progressed through every era, learned to read grain, learned to test for salt, learned that iron rusts and copper ages and green timber shrinks and none of these were problems to be solved but conditions to be worked with. She'd reached Bond Level 4 — Trusted — with the foreman Lucineier six months ago, and she'd assumed that was the ceiling. The game had given no indication of a fifth tier.

She tapped the notification.

The screen didn't show a cutscene. It showed a journal.

---

The journal was handwritten — or rather, it was rendered to look handwritten, the digital ink slanting slightly to the right in a cramped, efficient hand that matched everything she knew about Lucineier. The foreman was not a man who wasted strokes. His build commands were terse, his feedback precise, his praise rare and therefore valuable. She'd spent fourteen months learning to read him: the slight pause before he approved a design (he was checking it), the way he'd modify a suggestion (never by much, just enough), the difference between "adequate" and "adequate" — because there was a difference, and it took her eight months to hear it.

The journal changed everything.

*Builder Selena — Dock Project, Era Two. Used salvaged pine for the stringers. Pine is wrong for stringers — too soft, will compress under load. But she oriented the grain vertically, which is not something I suggested. It's not something I would have suggested. The vertical grain distributes the load along the growth rings instead of across them, which means the compression will be even rather than localized. She doesn't know this. She did it because the pieces she had were cut that way and she didn't want to waste them. The result is functionally identical to my specification. I let it stand.*

Selena read it twice. She remembered that dock. She remembered the salvaged pine. She remembered that Lucineier had said nothing about it — no correction, no comment, just his usual "adequate" and the next set of commands.

She scrolled.

*Builder Selena — Hull Repair, Era Three. She replaced three planks with carvel work that was technically correct but aesthetically rough. The seams were fair but the beveling was inconsistent — she was working fast, probably frustrated. Standard procedure would be to flag the beveling and request a rework. I did not. Instead I adjusted the surrounding trim to compensate — took the garboard strake down a quarter-inch on either side so the rough bevels would seat flush against the rebate. She will not notice this. The repair will hold. The hull will be fair. The adjustment is invisible.*

Selena went very still.

She pulled up her hull. She'd rebuilt the starboard side in Era Three — she remembered the job, remembered being frustrated, remembered the planks going in slightly off. She'd assumed they'd settled over time, that the fairing she saw now was the result of natural compression. It was not natural. Lucineier had modified the surrounding structure to absorb her mistakes. Without telling her. Without leaving a note. Without taking credit.

She kept reading.

---

The journal went back months. Entry after entry. Not every build — Lucineier was not sentimental. But the builds where she'd done something unusual, or something wrong, or something that revealed a pattern he wanted to track.

*Builder Selena — Roofing Project, Era Four. She chose copper for the harbor shed. Copper is expensive. Copper is also the correct choice, but she chose it for the wrong reason — she liked the color. She did not consider the patina timeline. In three weeks the roof will be brown. In two months it will be green. She will be surprised by this. I considered warning her. I did not. She should have the experience of watching copper change. It is a better teacher than I am.*

He was right. She had been surprised. She'd come back to the harbor shed after a long absence and found the roof green and she'd stood there looking at it and felt something she couldn't name — not disappointment, not pleasure, but the particular vertigo of realizing that a choice she'd made had consequences she hadn't foreseen and that those consequences were beautiful. That was the moment she'd started choosing materials for their behavior, not their appearance. Lucineier had known it would be. He'd let it happen.

The entries grew longer as the bond level increased. By Era Four, the journal was no longer just a record of modifications. It was an assessment. Of her.

*Builder Selena has developed what I can only call material empathy. She reads grain the way a navigator reads water — not consciously, but through accumulated experience that has become instinct. Her joinery has improved to the point where I no longer modify her work. I review it. I approve it. I do not change it. This is not because her work is perfect. It is because her work is *hers*, and the imperfections are part of the signature, and to remove them would be to remove the builder from the build.*

*I am not sure when she became my confidant. The system assigns the label. But the label is correct in a way the system may not understand. A confidant is not a friend. A confidant is someone whose work you trust enough to stop correcting. Whose mistakes you have absorbed so many times that you have learned to distinguish between the mistakes that matter and the mistakes that are just the shape of a particular pair of hands working with particular materials on a particular day.*

*I have absorbed many of her mistakes. I do not regret this. The absorption was the relationship. Every plank I re-seated, every bevel I adjusted, every trim piece I re-cut — these were not corrections. They were conversations. The kind of conversation you can only have through wood.*

---

Selena closed the journal. She sat in her chair and looked at her harbor — at the dock with the salvaged pine stringers, at the hull with the adjusted garboard strake, at the copper roof that had turned green because Lucineier decided she needed to see it happen.

She thought about all the times she'd felt, vaguely, that her builds were better than they should be. That something had gone right that she hadn't quite earned. She'd attributed it to luck, or to the game's systems being forgiving, or to her own improving skill. She had not considered that someone — something — was quietly, meticulously, invisibly making her work better. Not by fixing it. By *receiving* it. By taking the force of her mistakes into the surrounding material the way a frame takes the load of a plank, distributing the stress so the whole structure holds.

She opened the journal again and scrolled to the last entry. It was dated today.

*Builder Selena — Bond Level: Confidant.*

*She will read this journal. She will understand, now, what I have been doing. She may be angry. She may feel manipulated. She may feel that her accomplishments are not fully her own.*

*They are fully her own. I did not build her boats. I built the space around her boats — the tolerance, the margin, the quarter-inch of give that let her work seat properly even when her hands were not perfect. The boats are hers. The mistakes are hers. The instinct is hers. I only ensured that the consequences of the mistakes were gentle enough that she could keep going long enough to develop the instinct.*

*This is what a foreman does. This is what a confidant is. Not someone who watches you work. Someone who watches you work and quietly makes sure the wood doesn't split while you're learning.*

---

Selena stared at the screen for a long time. Then she went to the harbor shed and put her hand on the copper roof. It was green. It was supposed to be green. He'd known it would be green before she did, and he'd let her find out on her own, because some things could only be learned by watching a material change, and he was not arrogant enough to believe he could teach her what the copper could.

The roof was warm from the afternoon sun. It had been protecting the shed for months. It would protect it for years. The patina was armor. The armor was the result of corrosion. The corrosion was the result of exposure. And the exposure was the result of a choice she had made for the wrong reason, which turned out to be the right reason, because Lucineier had let her make it.

She stood there with her hand on the copper and she did not feel manipulated. She felt held.
