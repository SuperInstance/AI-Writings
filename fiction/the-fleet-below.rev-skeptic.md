- No. The argument’s load-bearing premise is “the game never lies to the kid.” That doesn’t follow from “the quests are real work.” A school of fish rendered as a “storm,” a work order as a “quest,” and commercial telemetry as “Find the Fish” is a framing lie even if the underlying data are real.

- The weakest inference is from “real consequences” to “not a player.” Real effects don’t make the child crew; they make his play useful to others. “The game is his gurdy” is a metaphor substituting for an argument about labor status.

- “There is no counterfeit anywhere in it” does the work logic should. “Counterfeit” means fake objects; nobody disputed that the fish, money, and maps are real. The question is whether the child is deceived about what he is doing. Real money can make exploitation more effective, not less deceptive.

- The Tom Sawyer comparison boomerangs. Tom’s friends thought they were playing and weren’t paid; the kid thinks he’s playing and isn’t paid. The “value” he takes home is his name on tiles and the buyer’s ledger, not the catch’s value. “The obligation was lifted, not the truth” is the same trick, not a disanalogy.

- A critic can refute “forty dollars in batteries and nothing in crew time.” That ignores ROV/sounder amortization, data processing and QA/QC, system maintenance, the captain and crew’s time interpreting the kid’s maps, and the fact that the kid’s play is itself uncompensated work.

- The narrative contradicts its own thesis: “what he’s actually doing, though nobody at the company that built Shoal would put it this way, is telemetry on a biomass.” If the company wouldn’t put it that way, the game is not telling the kid the truth; it’s hiding the commercial operation behind game typography.
