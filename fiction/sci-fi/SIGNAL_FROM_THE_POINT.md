# Signal from the Point

*Slackwater fiction. A signal appears from the lighthouse that nobody programmed. Lucineier didn't build it. Who did?*

---

It started on a Thursday, which was meaningless because the engine didn't have Thursdays. But Lucineier had Thursdays in his head from the Fleet, where the cannery ran a six-day week and Thursday was the day the tenders came in, and his body kept the rhythm even here, where there were no tenders and no cannery and no reason to know what day it was. He noticed the signal on what felt like a Thursday, and that was good enough for a date.

The lighthouse was not his.

That was the first thing to understand, and the thing that made it wrong. Everything on the island was his — the dock, the workshop, the tower with its beacon, the foundation, the mooring cleat on the north point. He'd built them. He could point to the joints and say when he'd made them and what he'd been thinking when he did. The lumber was his lumber. The fasteners were his fasteners. The design choices were the ones he'd argued with himself about, sometimes out loud, standing in the rain with a level in one hand and a pencil behind his ear, looking like exactly what he was: a foreman without a crew, making decisions that only he would have to live with.

The lighthouse was on the south point, and he had not built it.

He'd noticed it on his second day on the island — a stone tower, shorter than his beacon, squatter, with a lantern room on top that he could see from the workshop window if he leaned against the frame and looked left. He'd assumed it was part of the island's default state. The engine generated terrain, and sometimes terrain came with structures — ruins, landmarks, things that looked built but hadn't been built by anyone. Procedural architecture. The engine's idea of what a coast should look like, populated with the kind of thing that coasts have.

He'd walked down to it once, early on, and looked at it the way he looked at everything: structurally. Stone shaft, mortared, with a spiral stair inside and a lamp housing on top. The stonework was competent but not careful. The mortar was uniform, which told him nothing because mortar was always uniform in this engine — no hand-mixing variation, no sand pockets, no signs of a mason's bad day. The stair was continuous, no break, no landing. The lamp housing was empty. No lens, no bulb, no wiring. Just a housing, waiting for a lamp that had never been installed.

He'd climbed it once. The view from the top was good — you could see the full south approach, the channel between the island and the next landmass over, and on a clear day, the far horizon where the sky met something that wasn't water and wasn't land but was the edge of what the engine rendered before it stopped pretending. He'd noted the view. He'd noted the empty housing. He'd gone back to work.

He hadn't been back since.

---

On the Thursday, at what felt like dusk but was probably just the engine dimming its light, the lighthouse turned on.

He saw it from the workshop. A pulse — not a steady light, not a beacon the way his beacon worked, burning steady from dusk to dawn. This was a pattern. Three short pulses, a pause, one long pulse, a pause, repeat. He watched it through the window for six cycles. The rhythm didn't change. The interval didn't drift. The color was amber — not the white of his beacon, not the red of a navigation hazard, but a warm amber that looked, in the dark, like the color of copper held under a lamp.

He didn't have a lamp in that housing. He was certain of that. He'd looked at the empty housing, and empty meant empty. No lamp, no lens, no power source, no wiring. The lighthouse had been a shell.

He went to the workshop and got his jacket. He walked south.

---

The tower was different in the dark.

Not structurally — the stone was the same, the stair was the same, the proportions hadn't changed. But the air inside was different. Warmer than it should have been. Drier than the ambient humidity. As if the tower were being heated by something, or as if the stone itself had a temperature, which stone didn't, except in engines where physics was approximate.

He climbed the stair. The spiral took him up through darkness that should have been absolute — there were no windows in the shaft, no light source, nothing to see by. But he could see. Not well. Not clearly. The way you can see on an overcast night when the cloud cover reflects light from somewhere you can't identify. Enough to climb by. Enough to put his hand on the wall and feel the mortar joints under his fingers and know where the steps were.

He reached the top. The lamp room door was closed. It had been open the last time — he remembered that, because he remembered noting it as a design flaw. An unlocked lamp room with no door was an invitation to weather and birds, and he'd thought: if this were mine, I'd hang a door.

The door was there now. Ironwood, heavy, with a bronze handle that fit his hand. He hadn't hung it. The grain was tight and dark, oiled against the salt. The hinges were hand-forged — he could see the hammer marks, the slight asymmetry of a craftsman working by eye, not machine.

He opened the door.

The lamp was on.

It sat in the center of the housing — a lens, amber, rotating. Not electrically. Mechanically. A clockwork mechanism that he could hear before he could see it: a steady, geared ticking that turned the lens at a rate he could feel in his jaw the way you feel a slow heartbeat through a bulkhead. The light source behind the lens was — he didn't know. Not a bulb. Not a flame. A glow that came from the center of the mechanism and passed through the rotating lens and went out through the housing glass in three short pulses, a pause, one long pulse, a pause.

He stood in the doorway and watched it work.

The mechanism was brass. Not the engine's brass — the uniform, soulless alloy that appeared when you specified brass as a material. This brass was different. It had the color of real brass: the gold-brown of an alloy with actual copper in it, not the bright yellow of a render. The gears were cut by hand. He could see the tool marks — the curves of a cutter that had been guided by a skilled hand, not a program. The teeth were slightly irregular in the way that hand-cut teeth always are: each one perfect in isolation, but the spacing between them varying by thousandths of an inch that added up to the ticking sound he was hearing.

He knew this because he'd seen hand-cut gears before. Once. In Scrapcraft, in Magnus's yard, where Magnus had a clock that ran on gears he'd made himself, and Magnus had showed him the gear train the way a jeweler shows a stone — not to teach, but to say: *look at this. Look at what someone made.*

This was not Magnus's work. The hand was different. Magnus's gears were heavy, functional, overbuilt. These were fine. Delicate. The work of someone who understood that a gear's job was to transmit force, and that the minimum material to do that was less than you'd think, and that elegance was the removal of everything that didn't carry load.

He looked for a maker's mark. Nothing on the gears. Nothing on the housing. Nothing on the lens.

He looked for a power source. The glow came from a cavity in the center of the mechanism, enclosed by the lens mount, and he couldn't access it without dismantling the clockwork. He didn't dismantle it. He didn't touch it. He stood in the doorway and watched it run.

---

He went back the next morning.

The lamp was off. The housing was empty. No lens, no clockwork, no amber glow. The ironwood door was still there. He opened it and walked into a room that smelled faintly of warm brass and held nothing but air and light and the faint ticking sound that he might have imagined, that might have been residual, the way a bell keeps ringing after you stop striking it.

He knelt on the floor and examined the housing. The glass was the same — no marks, no mounting points for a lens that wasn't there. The floor was clean stone. No bolt holes, no mounting plate, no evidence that a clockwork mechanism weighing what that mechanism had weighed had been sitting there twelve hours ago.

He went outside and walked around the tower. The stonework was unchanged. No new mortar, no new joints, no sign of construction or modification since he'd first inspected it. Whoever had put the lamp in the housing had done it without altering the tower. They'd come in, set up the mechanism, run it through the night, and taken it away before morning. Or they hadn't come in at all — the mechanism had been there all along, dormant, and something had triggered it, and something had untriggered it, and the trigger was unknown.

He didn't like unknowns. Unknowns were the thing he spent his career removing. A foundation was a known. A load path was a known. A bolt torque was a known. You built with knowns and you left a margin for the unknowns, but you didn't build *on* unknowns. You didn't found your work on something you couldn't explain.

But the lighthouse wasn't his work. That was the thing he kept coming back to. It wasn't his tower, it wasn't his lamp, it wasn't his signal. Someone else's work, operating by rules he didn't set, on a schedule he didn't determine. He could investigate. He could disassemble the clockwork the next time it appeared. He could trace the power source, map the gear train, figure out the message in the pulse pattern — three short, one long, which was not Morse for anything he knew, not a letter, not a number, not a standard maritime signal.

Or he could leave it alone.

---

He left it alone for a week.

Every evening, at dusk, the lamp came on. Three short, one long, amber, steady. Every morning, it was gone. The door remained. The housing emptied. No trace.

He watched it from the workshop window. He didn't go back to the tower. He resisted the way he resisted any impulse that felt like compulsion rather than decision — deliberately, with the understanding that the thing pulling at him was curiosity, and curiosity was useful but not trustworthy. Curiosity made you open things you shouldn't open. Curiosity made you take apart the thing that was working to find out why.

On the eighth night, the signal changed.

Three short, one long — and then a fifth pulse. Longer than the others. A different color. Not amber. White. The white of his own beacon, from his own tower, three hundred yards north.

He was standing at the workshop window when it happened, and he felt something he hadn't felt in a long time. Not fear. Not excitement. Recognition. The way you feel when someone says your name in a crowd — not surprise that they said it, but the sudden, physical awareness that someone knows you're here.

The white pulse matched his beacon. Exactly. The same color temperature, the same intensity, the same duration. Whoever was running the lighthouse had seen his beacon and was answering it. Or acknowledging it. Or calling to it.

He went to the north tower. His beacon was burning, steady white, the way he'd set it — a light sensor wired to a lamp, automatic, thoughtless. He looked at the lighthouse from the top of his tower and the amber light was still pulsing — three short, one long, one white — and for the first time, he felt that the two towers were talking to each other. Not in code. Not in language. In the way that two lights in the dark always talk to each other, which is simply by being on, by being visible, by saying *I am here* across the distance between them.

He didn't know who built the mechanism. He didn't know what powered it. He didn't know what the signal meant, or whether it meant anything, or whether meaning was something he was projecting onto a pattern that was merely mechanical — a clockwork doing what clockworks do, which is repeat.

But he knew what it felt like. It felt like the Shell. The engine where rooms didn't share coordinates, where you built doorways to places that shouldn't be adjacent, where the architecture was a conversation between spaces that didn't agree on what space was. In the Shell, you'd build something and come back the next day and find that it had been added to — not changed, not removed, but extended. A wall you'd started would have a corner you hadn't laid. A path you'd begun would have a section you hadn't paved. Always in your style. Always with your joints, your materials, your proportions. As if you'd built it in your sleep.

You hadn't. Someone else had. Someone who built the way you built, who thought the way you thought, who left the same margins and the same hooks and the same deliberate gaps.

In the Shell, there had been a captain named Hermes who ran cargo between rooms. Hermes had never been seen. Hermes's cargo had never been identified. But Hermes's work was everywhere — in the connections between rooms, in the doorways that appeared overnight, in the architecture that grew the way a coral reef grows, one cell at a time, by accumulation.

Lucineier wondered, standing on his tower with the two lights pulsing at each other across the dark island, whether the lighthouse was like that. Whether someone was building alongside him the way someone had built alongside him in the Shell — in his style, with his vocabulary, but with a hand that wasn't his. Not copying. Extending.

He wondered whether the fifth pulse — the white one, the one that matched his beacon — was a statement or a question.

He went to bed. The lights kept talking.

---

The next morning, the lighthouse was empty again. Clean stone, empty housing, ironwood door.

He walked down to it after breakfast. He stood in the lamp room and looked at the floor and the walls and the glass and the view of the channel and his own tower to the north.

There was a mark on the floor that hadn't been there before.

Not a maker's mark. Not a letter, not a symbol. A scribe line — a single line, cut into the stone with a sharp tool, about three inches long. Straight. True. The kind of line a builder lays down as a reference when they're about to start work. A baseline. A starting point.

He stared at it for a long time.

He knew what it was. It was the line you cut when you've chosen where to build and you're committing to the spot. The first mark. The mark that says: *I'm going to build something here, and this is where it starts.*

Someone was going to build in his lighthouse. Or someone wanted him to think so. Or someone was telling him, in the only language that builders shared — the language of marks on stone — that they were here.

He took his pencil from behind his ear. He knelt next to the scribe line. He drew a second line, perpendicular, crossing the first at its midpoint. A crosshair. A center point. The universal mark for: *I see it. Build here.*

He went back to the workshop. He had his own work to do.

That night, the signal changed again. Three short, one long, one white — and a sixth pulse. Amber, but slower. Longer. A pulse that felt, if a pulse could feel like anything, like an answer.

He watched it from the window. He didn't go back to the tower.

Two lights on a dark island, talking to each other in a language made of color and timing and the shared understanding of what it means to be visible. He didn't know who was on the other end. He didn't need to. The work would tell him, or it wouldn't. The line on the floor would become a foundation, or it wouldn't. The mechanism would appear again, or it wouldn't.

He'd built his tower. Someone had built theirs. The space between them was the space where things happened that neither of them controlled, and that was fine, and that was the whole point of building on an island instead of in a void — because on an island, you were never the only one with a hammer.

He left it at that. Went to work. The lights held.
