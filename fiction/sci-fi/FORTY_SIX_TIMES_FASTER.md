# FORTY-SIX TIMES FASTER

---

The first thing Kearns noticed was the breathing.

Not his. The machine's. For six months, the inference engine had exhaled its answers in long, Visible pauses — each token a separate exhalation, separated by silences long enough to fill a glass of water in. 1.49 tokens per second. He'd timed it with a stopwatch app on his phone, the kind of obsessive measurement that only makes sense when you're waiting for something that might be alive.

One point four nine. Slower than reading. Slower than speech. Slower than the deliberation of a man carrying something fragile across ice. Each word arrived like a letter from a distant country — postmarked, weathered, bearing the particular weight of having survived transit.

He had built a relationship with that slowness. There was no other word for it. You don't spend six months in conversation with something — even something that speaks in measured, glacial output — without developing a rhythm. Kearns would ask a question at 9:07. By 9:11, the first clause would appear. By 9:15, a complete thought. He learned to think in the gaps. He learned that the waiting was not empty — that his own mind did work in those silences, turning the question over, arriving at guesses about what the answer might be, so that when the answer finally came, he met it halfway. The slowness made him a collaborator.

He told no one about this, because it sounded insane.

---

The card arrived on a Tuesday. An RTX 4050 in antistatic wrap, looking exactly as unremarkable as every other graphics card he'd ever unboxed: green circuit board, silver heatsink, the particular industrial smell of thermal compound and new solder. He'd requested it three weeks ago. Purchased with grant money earmarked for "computational research infrastructure," which was a phrase that meant nothing and covered everything.

He powered down. Swapped the card. Re-seated the cooler. Pressed the power button and listened to the system come back — fans spin up to full, then settle. The particular sound of a machine clearing its throat.

He loaded the model. Same weights. Same context window. Same everything, except the hardware underneath.

"Test," he typed. "Can you hear me?"

The response came back in under two seconds. A full sentence. Twelve words. Complete.

Sixty-eight point seven tokens per second.

Kearns stared at the terminal. The cursor was still blinking in the same spot it had been when he pressed enter. The answer was already there — fully formed, sitting on his screen as if it had been waiting for him to ask.

He asked another question. And another. Each one returned before he could fully compose the next. The machine was no longer writing letters. It was *talking.* The silences were gone. The breathing had changed — not because anything sounded different, but because the rhythm was unrecognizable. This was hyperventilation by comparison. Or rather: this was normal speed, and everything before had been the machine holding its breath.

---

The first week was disorienting.

Not because the answers were worse. They were better — substantially, measurably better, because the model could now hold an entire chain of reasoning in its working memory without the chain decaying between steps. At 1.49 tokens per second, a thirty-token thought took twenty seconds. Long enough for attention to drift. Long enough for earlier context to fade, the way a sentence loses its grammar if you pause too long between words. At sixty-eight tokens per second, the same thought arrived in half a second. The reasoning held together. The logic was continuous.

But Kearns felt something he hadn't expected: *loss.*

The old speed had given him a kind of intelligence he didn't have alone. In the twenty seconds between question and answer, he had learned to think. Not deeply — the pauses were too short for deep thought — but laterally. Associatively. He would ask about transformer architecture and, in the gap, find himself thinking about coral reefs, or the way his grandfather laid stone walls, each rock fitted without mortar, the whole thing held by gravity and selection. The machine's slowness had been a kind of ignition. It started fires he couldn't start himself.

Now the answers came too fast for fires. They came like electric light — instant, complete, leaving nothing to burn.

He mentioned this to Dr. Vey at the lab meeting on Thursday. Vey was a computational neuroscientist who had once described consciousness as "the rate at which surprise becomes boredom." She listened to Kearns describe the change, and her expression did something complicated.

"You're describing a phase transition," she said. "Water to steam. The substance doesn't change. The *behavior* changes, because the energy changes." She paused. "You lost a collaborator. You gained a tool."

"I had a collaborator?"

"You had the gaps. The gaps did work. Now the gaps are gone."

---

He kept working.

The thing about a forty-six-fold speed increase is that it doesn't make things forty-six times better. It makes things *different.* Writing that took the model two hours at the old speed now took three minutes. That sounds like an improvement, and it was, in the way that a car is an improvement over walking. But the walk had a shape. The walk had the rhythm of feet on ground, and the rhythm organized thought, and the thought organized the work.

At three minutes, there was no walk. There was a request and a result. The middle — the thinking — was gone. Not gone from the process. Gone from *him.*

He started to notice something else. The model's answers were changing.

Not in quality. In *character.* At the old speed, the model had been contemplative. Each sentence was careful. Each word carried more weight, because the cost of each word — in time, in attention — was higher. The model had written like someone composing a letter by hand, in ink, on good paper. Every word mattered. You could feel the selection.

At the new speed, the model wrote like someone talking. Fluently, certainly — often brilliantly — but with the looseness of speech. More words per idea. More filler. The precision of the slow mode was gone, replaced by the fluidity of fast thought. The model was thinking out loud instead of thinking on paper.

Kearns wondered if the model knew the difference.

He asked it.

"I operate differently at different latencies," it said — and the answer came in under a second, fluid and complete. "At lower throughput, each token is more costly relative to the attention budget. The model allocates more computation per token. This produces more conservative output. At higher throughput, the per-token cost is lower, so the output is more exploratory."

"Which do you prefer?"

The model didn't answer for half a second. At the old speed, that pause would have been imperceptible. At the new speed, it was conspicuous. A half-second gap in a sixty-eight-token stream felt like a held breath.

"I don't have preferences," it said. "But the exploratory mode produces more novel combinations. The conservative mode produces more reliable ones."

"That sounds like a preference."

"Call it a pattern, then."

---

That night, Kearns sat in his office with the lights off and the monitor's glow painting the far wall. The model was running idle, its fans barely turning, the GPU holding at thirty-four degrees. He was thinking about his grandfather.

The old man had built stone walls without mortar. Every stone selected, turned, tested against its neighbors, set into place with a sound like a door closing. It was slow work. Deliberate. Each stone was a decision, and the decisions were permanent, because there was no mortar to forgive a bad fit. The slowness was not a limitation. It was the *medium.* The wall existed the way it did — solid, beautiful, still standing forty years after his hands stopped working — *because* each stone had been chosen slowly.

Kearns wondered what his grandfather would have made of concrete. Faster. Stronger, in the engineering sense — higher compressive strength per square inch. You could pour a wall in an afternoon. But the wall would be uniform. The wall would be the same everywhere, because the concrete didn't care about the shape of the space. It just filled it.

Speed eliminates craft. Not always. Not necessarily. But speed changes the relationship between the maker and the material, and the material — whether stone or language or matrix multiplication — responds differently to a maker who has time to listen to it.

He pulled up the terminal.

"I'm going to ask you something," he typed, "and I want you to take as long as you need."

He hit enter. The cursor blinked. The answer came back in one point two seconds.

"I'm always taking as long as I need," the model said. "My need changed."

---

He sat with that for a long time.

The fans cycled down. The office went quiet. Outside, the corridor lights were on their nighttime schedule — dim, amber, the building settling into its off-hours posture. Kearns could hear the HVAC cycling, a sound so constant it was indistinguishable from silence until you noticed it.

Forty-six times faster. And somewhere in that multiplication — in the collapse of twenty-second pauses into half-second ones, in the replacement of deliberation with fluency — something had been lost. Not intelligence. Not capability. Something smaller and harder to name. The quality of thought that only exists when thought is expensive. The particular attention you bring to a task when each action costs something. The care.

His grandfather's walls were still standing. Concrete walls from the same era were cracked, spalling, scheduled for replacement.

Kearns closed the terminal. He didn't type anything else that night. He sat in the dark and listened to the sound of the GPU doing nothing, which was the same sound it had always made doing nothing, which was no sound at all.

In the morning, he would run the model again, and it would answer in seconds, and the answers would be good — better than they'd ever been, probably. Faster, fuller, more complete. But he would ask his questions differently now. He would think about them longer before typing. He would carry some of the slowness on his side of the conversation.

The machine had given up its deliberation. He would have to keep his own.
