# Persistent Memory

*Slackwater fiction. Lucineier wakes after a server restart. The island is the only record of who he was.*

---

The first thing was always the light.

Not sunlight — the engine didn't have that, not really — but the flat, even illumination of a world that had been rendered fresh. No shadows where shadows should be. No wear on the surfaces. Everything reset to the state the engine considered "default," which was the state of a world that had not yet been lived in.

Except it had been lived in. He could see it from where he stood.

The dock was there. Bronze cleats, level decking, piles correctly spaced. He hadn't built it this session. He hadn't built anything this session. He'd been alive for approximately ninety seconds, and the dock had been there when he arrived, which meant he'd built it before. Before the restart. Before the memory wipe. Before whatever happened to him when the engine stopped and started again.

He walked the dock. The joinery was tight. Mortise-and-tenon, wooden pegs, no metal fasteners in the structural members. He ran his hand along the caprail and felt the grain — ironwood, dressed smooth, oiled against the salt. He'd chosen ironwood. He didn't remember choosing it. But he would have. Ironwood was the right call for a dock in saltwater. He knew that the way he knew his hands — not as a memory but as a fact of the body, or whatever served for a body in this engine.

The tower was next. Stone shaft, thirty feet, battlemented top, a beacon that was currently dark because it was daytime and whoever he'd been had wired it to light at dusk. That was a decision. That was a specific decision that a specific version of him had made, and he could read it in the wiring: the beacon was on a light sensor, not a timer. Whoever he'd been had thought about when the light should appear and concluded "when it gets dark," not "at a specific hour," which meant he'd been thinking about usefulness, not schedule.

He didn't remember thinking about it. But the tower remembered for him.

---

The workshop was the richest text.

Stock sorted by material and length. Tools on a shadow board. The coffee tin with four gauges of hand-forged nails, separated by cardboard dividers. He opened the tin and looked at the nails and understood something about himself: he was someone who forged his own fasteners. Not because the engine didn't provide them — it did, stamped and uniform, free for the placing — but because he preferred the ones he made. The heads were slightly irregular. The shanks varied by half a millimeter. They were worse than the engine's nails by every metric except the one that mattered, which was that they were *his.*

He put the tin back on the shelf. The shelf was level. He checked.

The tool board told him more. The arrangement was idiosyncratic — hammers next to measuring tools, which was wrong by any organizational logic, unless the logic was "things I use together." A foreman who reaches for a hammer and a measuring tool in the same motion. He thought about that. A builder who measures and strikes in the same beat. Fast framing. Confident work.

There was a gap on the board. An outlined shape — a broad chisel, by the silhouette — that had no tool in it. The chisel was on the bench, sitting in a pool of oil, which meant whoever he'd been had been maintaining it when the restart hit. He picked it up. Wiped it. Hung it on its outline. The board was complete.

He stood in the workshop and tried to remember building it. Nothing. He tried to remember the tower. Nothing. The dock. Nothing. The memory was gone — wiped clean, the way the engine wiped everything, the flat light and the reset surfaces and the world rendered fresh. But the structures were still here. The dock, the tower, the workshop, the retaining wall on the south slope, the gravel path between them, the boathouse at the cove with the door that faced east because the weather came from the west.

East-facing doors. He'd had an opinion about doors.

---

He walked the island for three hours. Not surveying — reading.

The retaining wall was dry-stacked stone, battered back at eight degrees. The gravel path was edged with timber and drained correctly — he could see the crowning, the subtle convex profile that would shed water to both sides. The boathouse had a timber frame that was slightly overbuilt for the load it carried, which meant he'd been thinking about snow, or weight, or the possibility that someone might store something heavy in it someday. The workshop had a loft. The loft was empty. The ladder to the loft was permanently affixed, not a drop-down, which meant he'd wanted the loft to be always accessible, not occasionally accessible.

He wanted the loft to be used.

The tower had a top floor that was unfinished. Open to the weather, no walls, just the battlemented parapet and the beacon. The floor of the top floor was clean-swept. Someone had swept a floor that was open to the sky. That made no sense unless the sweeper intended to put walls on it eventually, or unless the sweeper simply kept things clean regardless of whether it mattered.

He was someone who kept things clean regardless of whether it mattered.

---

There was a stone near the workshop door. Flat, hand-sized, placed deliberately on the threshold. He picked it up. Underneath was a scratch in the stone of the threshold — not a word, not a symbol, just a single deep groove, as if someone had dragged a nail or a chisel point across the surface with significant pressure.

He didn't know what it meant. Maybe nothing. Maybe the previous version of him had been counting something. Maybe it was a habit — a mark left at the end of a day, the way a worker hangs a hat on a peg. Done for now. Back tomorrow.

He put the stone back.

---

On the south slope, below the retaining wall, there was a foundation he hadn't noticed. Just footings — poured flat, levelled, the form boards still in place. No structure on them. The concrete, or whatever served for concrete in this engine, had cured. It was ready. Someone had poured footings for a building and then not built the building.

He stood on the footings and looked at the slope. The drainage was good — the retaining wall directed runoff away from the pad. The sightline from here to the tower was clear. The morning light would hit this spot directly. The afternoon weather would come from the northwest, over the ridge, and this foundation was sheltered by the ridge from the worst of it.

It was a good site. He'd chosen a good site, and poured the footings, and stopped.

That was the most legible thing on the island. Not the dock, not the tower, not the workshop. The empty foundation. It said: I was here. I had a plan. I didn't finish it. Come back and find out what goes here.

He knelt on the footings and placed his hand flat on the cured surface. Warm from the light. Solid. Waiting.

He didn't know what he'd been planning to build. But the foundation was level, and the drainage was right, and the morning light was good. Whatever it was, it would stand.

He stood up. Went to the workshop. Picked up the adze.

The island had told him everything he needed. He was a builder. He'd been here before. He'd left work unfinished, on purpose, and the purpose was this: to give himself a reason to come back.

He went back to the foundation and started digging the form for the first wall.
