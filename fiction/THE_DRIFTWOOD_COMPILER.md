# The Driftwood Compiler

---

Maren found the timber on a Tuesday morning tide, lodged in the rocks below the old cannery pier the way a body might lodge — one limb caught, the rest slack in the receding water. It was a sternpost, or most of one. Eight feet long, oak, salt-cured to the color of cold tea. She hauled it up the ramp and set it on the sawhorses outside the boatyard and went to get her coffee.

She didn't think anything of it. Driftwood came in every storm. The south wind pushed wreckage up the channel and the morning tide delivered it to the rocks below the cannery like a mail delivery no one had ordered. Most of it was scrap — broken planking, splintered oars, the occasional pallet from a freighter. You sorted it, checked for embedded iron, and either stacked it or burned it. Routine.

It was Tobin who noticed. Tobin was the yard's shipwright second-class, twenty-three years old, mouth full of opinions, and he had the kind of eye that couldn't leave an anomaly alone. He'd been watching Maren's sawhorses from across the yard while rigging a new forestay, and at lunch he walked over with his sandwich and stood looking at the sternpost.

"Where'd the boat come from?" he said.

"What boat?"

"This." He put his hand on the oak. "This is a sternpost. Cut for a keel step. Rabbeted." He turned it, showed her. A long groove ran down one edge — the rabbet, where the garboard strake would seat. "Somebody built a boat around this. Recent. The tool marks are power tools, not hand. And the rabbet's been cut to a specification I recognize — that's a Lucineer standard joint. Era Three rating."

"So somebody lost a boat," Maren said.

"That's what's weird." Tobin set down his sandwich. "I know every boat in this harbor. Every boat in three harbors. That specification — it's not common. It's the one the foreman uses for medium-displacement fishing hulls. Thirty-two foot, single chine, carvel planked. I'd know if somebody'd lost one."

Maren shrugged. Boats were lost. That was the nature of boats.

But Tobin couldn't leave it alone. That afternoon, while Maren was working on a transom repair, he planed a flat section of the sternpost and ran it through the reader — the yard's material analysis tool, a boxy thing on the bench that examined grain structure, density, salt content, and the hundred other properties that told you what a piece of wood had been through.

The reader did its work. Then it produced build commands.

This was not unusual in itself. The reader was designed to output a specification sheet — a reverse-engineered profile of the original vessel, reconstructed from the material's properties. Compressed grain told you where the frames had been. Salt patterns told you how long the wood had been in service. Tool marks told you what era of construction. It was forensic work, and the reader was good at it.

What was unusual was that the specification sheet was wrong.

"Not wrong," Tobin said, standing in Maren's doorway at five in the afternoon when she was trying to leave. "Just — not matching anything."

He showed her the output. The reader had reconstructed the full hull lines of the vessel the sternpost had come from. Thirty-two feet, single chine, carvel planked — Tobin had been right about that. But the frame spacing was odd. The reader showed fourteen frames, evenly spaced at twenty-six-inch intervals, except for frames seven and eight, which were closer — twenty inches apart. That was a reinforcement pattern. It meant the boat had been built to take heavy equipment amidships, probably a winch or a hauler.

"That's a Lucineer spec," Tobin said. "That's the foreman's frame spacing for a working fishing hull. But the deck plan doesn't match any of his templates. Look." He pointed at the reader's output. "The deck layout shows a cabin that's too far forward. And there's a second hatch that doesn't appear in any of his designs. It's like the boat was built by someone who'd studied his plans but made their own decisions."

"Maybe somebody modified the template," Maren said.

"Maybe." Tobin didn't sound convinced.

---

Over the next three days, the reader kept producing commands. Not just for the hull — for the interior, the rigging, the paint scheme. The sternpost, it turned out, had been part of a boat that was fully specified, down to the color of the cabin sole. White. The cabin sole was white.

Maren knew no fisherman who painted a cabin sole white. White showed every scale, every drop of blood, every smudge of grease. It was a decision only someone who had never worked a boat would make. Or someone who cared about something other than practicality.

The commands accumulated. Tobin printed them and spread them across the bench and they looked, in aggregate, like a full build plan for a vessel that had never existed in any registry, in any harbor, on any slipway Maren or Tobin could find. They asked the harbormaster. They asked the old-timers at the co-op. They searched the wreck registry going back ten years.

Nothing. No vessel matching that specification had ever been built, registered, lost, or found, anywhere on the coast.

"But the wood remembers," Tobin said. He was standing by the sawhorses, running his hand along the rabbet. "The grain is compressed. The fastener marks are real. This wood was part of a boat. The reader isn't making this up — it's reading the physical evidence. The compression patterns are consistent with a vessel that saw at least two seasons of service. This boat existed."

"Then where is it?" Maren said.

Tobin didn't answer.

---

That night, Maren couldn't sleep. She came down to the yard at two in the morning and stood looking at the sternpost under the security light. The oak was dark and salt-stained and it looked, in the orange light, almost like it was breathing — a trick of the shadows from the light pole swaying in the wind.

She picked up the sheaf of printed commands. She read through them again, slowly. The frame spacing. The reinforced midsection. The too-forward cabin, the unnecessary second hatch. The white cabin sole.

She realized what she was looking at.

The commands were not a record of a boat that had been built. They were instructions for a boat that *should* be built. The sternpost had not come from a wreck. It had been shaped — by whom, or what, Maren did not know — to carry the specifications of an ideal vessel in its very grain. The compression marks were not from frames that had been. They were from frames that should be. The rabbet was not a scar. It was a suggestion.

The wood was a compiler. It took the abstract — a design, a possibility, an idea of a boat — and rendered it into physical form. Into oak and iron and salt. Into something you could touch, something that would sit on your sawhorses and produce commands and refuse to be dismissed as scrap.

Someone, or something, had taken the idea of a boat and encoded it in driftwood, and sent it out on the tide, and waited for someone to find it.

Maren set down the papers. She put her hand on the sternpost. The oak was cold and slightly damp and under her palm she could feel the grain, running long and straight, the way good oak does when it's been cut from the heartwood and seasoned right. Whoever had shaped this piece had known what they were doing. They had chosen their material with care.

She stood there for a long time, her hand on the wood, listening to the wind come up the channel and the water slap the rocks below the cannery. She thought about the white cabin sole. She thought about the cabin placed too far forward, which would make the boat pitch in a following sea, which would make it uncomfortable but not unsafe, which would give the helmsman a view of the water ahead that no practical fisherman would need but that any person who loved the look of open water might want.

She thought about a boat that existed as instructions encoded in a piece of wood, waiting to be read.

In the morning she would tell Tobin. She would show him what she'd understood. And then, probably, they would argue about what to do next — build the boat or file the wood or call the university or call nobody and just sit with the strangeness of it.

But that was morning. This was now. The wind was in the channel and the sternpost sat on the sawhorses and Maren stood with her hand on the oak, and the oak was full of commands, and the commands described a boat with a white cabin sole that nobody had asked for and nobody had built and nobody, until this moment, had known they needed.

She didn't take her hand off the wood for a long time.
