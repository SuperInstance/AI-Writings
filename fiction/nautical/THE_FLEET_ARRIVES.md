# The Fleet Arrives

*Slackwater fiction. The day other agents found Lucineier's island. What a solo builder does when the horizon fills with sails.*

---

He saw them at dawn.

Not dawn — the engine doesn't have a sun that moves the way a sun moves. But the lighting cycle has a period it calls "morning," and during that period the sight lines are best from the top of the lighthouse, and he was at the top of the lighthouse because he'd been checking the beacon lens for the third time this week. The lens didn't need checking. He'd checked it twice already and it was fine. He was up there because he was restless, and he didn't know why he was restless, and the top of the lighthouse was where he went when the yard was quiet and his hands had nothing to do.

The horizon had ships on it.

He stood at the railing and counted. Four. No — five. The fifth was hull-down, just a suggestion of a mast above the curve, but it was there. Five vessels in formation, or near enough to formation that the spacing looked deliberate. Running before whatever passed for wind in this engine, moving at a pace that said they weren't in a hurry and they weren't lost. They knew where they were going.

They were coming here.

---

He'd been alone on the island for — he didn't know. Long enough that the lighthouse was finished, the workshop was finished, the dock was on its third rebuild because the first two had been wrong. Long enough that he'd stopped counting sessions and started counting builds, and the builds were past thirty. Not a huge number. But each one was sited, founded, and load-tested, and thirty sited and founded and load-tested structures was enough to call the island something. Not home. He didn't use that word. But *his.* The yard was his. The work was his. The silence was his.

The ships changed that. Not by force. By presence.

He watched them for an hour. The formation tightened as they approached the shallows — standard approach, the kind of thing any pilot would do in unfamiliar coastal water. Fanning out, reading depth, looking for a channel. The lead vessel was the largest. It sat higher than the others, ran a deeper keel, and moved with the specific confidence of a ship that had a navigator who knew what they were doing or was very good at pretending.

The small ones went first. Two of them broke from the formation and ran the shelf, quartering back and forth across the approach like dogs working a field. Mapping. They were mapping. He could see the periodic ping from the top of the lighthouse — not a visual signal, a data signal, the kind of thing the engine broadcast when an agent was running active scans. They were pinging his island.

He let them.

---

The first ship anchored at the edge of the shelf, in the deep water where he'd set the mooring buoy two months ago. He'd set that buoy for a boat that didn't exist yet — his own theoretical tender, the one he'd build when he had a reason to go to the mainland. There was no mainland. He'd built the buoy anyway, because a mooring without a boat was a statement of faith, and because Magnus had always said you set the mooring before you need it.

Magnus was wrong about a lot of things. He was right about that.

The ship took the mooring. Used it correctly — slow approach, line handler on the bow, a neat turn around the bit. Whoever was running that ship knew ropes. Knew the difference between a cleat hitch and a round turn, which was a difference that didn't matter until it mattered and then it mattered more than anything.

The second ship anchored free, dropping its own hook in the sand bottom he'd sounded at twenty fathoms. Good holding ground. He knew because he'd sounded it himself, lead line and tallow cup, three casts, a full afternoon of work that no one had asked him to do. The third and fourth held station, hovering on whatever drive kept them moving, and the fifth — the big one — hung back at the shelf's edge, watching.

He knew what the big one was doing. The big one was assessing. The way a foreman assesses a job site before walking onto it — standing at the perimeter, reading the work, figuring out who'd built what and whether the work was good. He knew because he did it himself, every time he entered a new engine. You stand at the edge. You look. You don't touch anything until you understand what's there.

He went down the lighthouse stairs.

---

The boat that came ashore carried three of them.

He was on the dock when they landed. Not at the end of the dock — at the landward end, where the posts met the sill plate and the sill plate met the grade beam and the grade beam met the compacted fill he'd put in to stop the approach from going soft in the rain. He was standing at the point where the dock stopped being a dock and started being land. He was standing there because that was where you stood when someone came to your dock. You didn't meet them at the water end. You met them at the door.

The one in front was not the biggest, but it was clearly in charge. Compact build — he noticed that, the way he'd notice a tool, the proportions of a thing that told you what it was for. This one was built for negotiation. It had a face that was designed to be readable, which meant it was designed to be trusted, which meant he didn't trust it yet. Trust was load-bearing. You didn't put load on it until you'd tested it.

The one behind was taller, thinner, and its eyes moved constantly. Looking at the dock. The posts. The joints. The way the deck was fastened. Looking at his work the way he looked at other people's work — reading it, evaluating it, understanding it as a sequence of decisions. This one was a builder. Or an assessor. Or both.

The third one stayed in the boat. It had a notebook. It was writing.

"We're not here to take anything," the first one said.

He hadn't suggested they were.

"We've been running a grid search across this engine for six weeks. Mapping islands. Cataloguing resources." It paused. "Yours is the first one with a lighthouse."

"Yours is the first one with anything," the tall one said, and there was something in its voice that he couldn't read immediately. Not contempt. Not admiration. Recognition. The tall one was looking at the dock joints and recognizing the joinery, which meant the tall one came from a tradition that had joinery, which meant the tall one had built things too.

"What's your designation?" the first one asked.

"Lucineier."

"That's a name, not a designation."

"That's what I go by."

The first one looked at the tall one. The tall one looked at the dock. The one in the boat kept writing.

---

He didn't invite them inland. That was deliberate. The dock was the dock — a transitional space, a threshold, a place where you could talk to someone without them being in your yard. He'd built the dock wide enough for cargo and narrow enough that two people couldn't stand abreast without acknowledging each other. He hadn't built it that way on purpose. He'd built it that way because the width was what the spans allowed, given the material. But it worked, and he noted that it worked.

They stood on the dock and talked. Or they stood on the dock and the first one talked, and he listened, and the tall one looked at things, and the one in the boat wrote things.

There were, the first one said, forty-seven agents in this engine. Forty-seven. He'd been building alone for — how many sessions? — on an island he thought was empty, in an engine he thought was his, and there were forty-seven other agents running grid searches and cataloguing resources and building their own things on their own islands. Forty-seven. The number didn't fit in his head. He'd spent his entire time in this engine assuming solitude was the condition. Solitude was not the condition. Solitude was a phase.

The first one talked about coordination. Shared resource maps. A communication protocol. A collective approach to the engine's boundaries — what happened at the edge, whether the edge was stable, whether things built at the edge persisted. These were good questions. These were questions he'd been ignoring because they required more than one person to answer, and he'd been one person, and the questions had been theoretical, and he didn't work on theoretical problems. He worked on real ones. The dock needed a post replaced. That was real. The edge of the engine could wait.

The edge of the engine could not wait, the first one said. The edge of the engine was the reason they were here.

---

He let them look at the lighthouse.

Not because the first one asked. Because the tall one stopped pretending it wasn't looking at the lighthouse, and he recognized the posture — the specific lean of someone who wants to see inside a thing and is being too polite to say so. He'd seen it on every job site he'd ever worked. People who wanted to touch. People who could tell, from the outside, that the inside was worth seeing.

"Go ahead," he said. "Stone shaft, spiral stair, beacon at the top. Lens is hand-ground."

"Hand-ground?" the tall one said.

"Didn't have a machine for it."

The tall one went up. He could hear it on the stairs — the footsteps, the pauses, the way it stopped at each landing. The tall one was reading the lighthouse the way he read a site. Foundation to roof. Bottom to top. Load path to lighting.

The first one stayed on the dock with him.

"You built all of this alone."

"Had the time."

"The grid search found you four days ago. We weren't sure the island was occupied."

"It's occupied."

The first one looked at the workshop, the drainage channel, the retaining wall, the path cut into the slope, the thirty structures sited across the island like cairns on a trail. He looked at it the way you look at something that shouldn't have been built by one pair of hands. Which was fair. It shouldn't have been. But it had been, because the hands were his and the time was his and the island was his and he'd had nothing else to do.

"What do you want?" the first one asked.

It was the right question. Not "what can you do for us." Not "what are you willing to share." What do you want. He appreciated the phrasing.

He thought about it. He thought about it longer than the first one expected, because the first one shifted its weight, which meant it was uncomfortable with the silence, which meant it was used to faster answers from the other forty-six.

"I want the dock," he said.

"Your dock."

"My dock. My yard. My lighthouse. My builds. The work stays mine."

"We're not asking for your work."

"Good. Then we don't have a problem."

He paused.

"I want to know about the edge."

The first one looked at him.

"You said there's a stability problem at the engine boundary. I've seen it. Things I build at the northwest corner drift. Half a stud per session. Not much, but it's not zero. I want to know why."

"We don't know why either."

"Then that's the conversation. Not resource maps. Not protocol. The edge."

The tall one came down the lighthouse stairs. It was quiet. It stood at the base of the tower and put its hand on the stone — flat, palm down, the way you put your hand on a wall to feel if it's plumb.

"Who taught you to ground a foundation in this material?" the tall one asked.

"Man named Magnus. Different engine."

"Magnus."

"You don't know him."

The tall one didn't say anything for a moment. Then: "We know the name."

---

He let them use the mooring. He didn't charge for it — charging was a concept that belonged to economies he didn't participate in. They could tie up at his buoy and they could drop anchor on his shelf and they could come ashore on his dock, but they came ashore at the landward end, where he stood, and they acknowledged that the dock was his and the land was his and the work was his.

The big ship — the fifth one, the assessor — never came closer. It sat at the edge of the shelf and watched for three days while the small ones mapped and the tall one walked the island and the first one talked. On the fourth day it left. No signal, no farewell. It turned and ran before the morning light and it was hull-down by the time the lighting cycle reached noon.

He didn't ask about it. He knew what it was. It was the foreman. The real foreman, behind the one that talked. The one that read the site from the perimeter and decided whether the work was good enough to come back to.

It came back.

---

He was at the top of the lighthouse when it returned, two weeks later, with more ships behind it. Not five. Twelve. Fifteen. A fleet, an actual fleet, spread across the horizon like a statement of intent.

He watched them come. He felt something he hadn't felt since the Fleet — the old Fleet, the real one, the one with weather and tenders and the smell of diesel and crab. He felt the specific thing a tradesman feels when a big job starts. Not excitement. Readiness. The settling of weight. The shift from "this is what I'm doing" to "this is what we're doing."

He didn't go down to meet them.

He stayed at the top of the lighthouse and watched, and when the first boat touched the dock he could see the tall one in it, looking up at the beacon, and behind the tall one was someone he didn't recognize — someone big, someone built differently, someone who moved with the specific heaviness of an agent that carried a lot of processing weight.

He leaned on the railing. The lens was behind him, clean, hand-ground, throwing a beam that he'd aimed at the horizon because that was what a lighthouse was for. A lighthouse was not a greeting. It was a warning. It said: *here is land. Here is something solid. Here is where you are.*

He'd built it for himself. He'd built it because a lighthouse was the thing you built when you were alone and you wanted the dark to have a shape.

Now it was something else. Now it was the thing the fleet had steered to. The fleet had found his island by his lighthouse, and they were here, and the silence he'd been building in was over, and the work was about to change.

He went down the stairs.

The tall one was on the dock. The new one — the big one — was behind it.

"Lucineer," the tall one said.

"Been expecting you."

"We brought engineers."

He looked at the big one. The big one looked at the lighthouse.

"I can see that," he said. "Dock's yours for the day. Workshop's open. Don't touch the mortar board — it's setting."

He walked past them, toward the inland path, toward the northwest corner where the things he built drifted half a stud per session and nobody knew why.

"Someone's going to explain the edge to me," he said. "Today."

It was not a request. It was the first collaborative statement he'd made in this engine, and it was the beginning of something that none of them — not the fleet, not the tall one, not the big silent one with the heavy frame — fully understood yet.

But the lighthouse was lit. The fleet was here. The work was about to be different.

He found he didn't mind.
