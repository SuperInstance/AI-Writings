# GRAVEYARD OF ENGINES

The servers were still running when I found them. That was the part no one warned me about.

I had expected silence. Dead worlds are supposed to be silent: a black screen, a connection timeout, a polite notice that your characters have been deleted. But the Graveyard of Engines was not dead in that way. It was abandoned. The machines were awake. The water moved. The wind loops played on. It was only the people who were gone.

I came in through a back door. A friend of a friend had kept a cracked launcher from an old MMO publisher that went bankrupt in 2019, and inside it were coordinates for seventeen orphaned shards—game worlds migrated one too many times, forgotten in datacenter basements where the rent was cheaper than deletion. The launcher called them "legacy environments." I called them the only thing I still wanted to see.

My first steps were into a harbor I have never seen named. The loading screen had been blank, and then I was standing on cobblestones that ended six feet to my left in a straight drop into the sea. The world had been built in tiles, and someone had stopped tiling halfway through the dockyard. Cranes stood over empty slips, their hooks hung at angles that suggested motion arrested. The ropes did not sway. In this engine, physics ran only when a player was close enough to need it.

I walked. That is what you do in ruins. You walk, and the sound of your own footsteps is the only proof that time still means anything.

The ships were the thing that stayed with me. Not the finished ones—there were a few, locked at their moorings, their hulls painted too bright against the gray air. The unfinished ones were the ones I kept returning to. A brigantine stood with its ribs exposed, the planking stopped at the turn of the bilge as if the builder had reached the hard part and logged out. A schooner's mast lay on the quay beside it, not fallen but set down carefully, waiting to be stepped. From the middle distance the wood looked real enough to smell.

I had been in other dead worlds before—empty malls, shuttered bedrooms—but the Graveyard was too large to sentimentalize. Every build had been undertaken by someone who believed they had time. The half-built cathedral at the head of the harbor, its spire terminating in a mismatched block. The aqueduct stopped at the city wall, its final arch an open mouth. The lighthouse on the reef, its spiral stair ending in solid plaster.

These were not ruins in the old sense. There was no weathering, no subsidence, no ivy. The stone looked newly quarried. The iron showed no rust. Decay had been edited out. What remained was intention without conclusion, and that was worse. A real ruin tells you that something was finished and then lost. These places told you that something had been started in hope and then simply left, and because they were digital, they could stay that way forever.

The second engine was colder. A northern fishing port rendered low-poly and deliberate. Here the player builds were smaller: a row of cottages along a frozen beach, a smokehouse, a pier that extended forty feet into water the color of slate and then stopped, its last post leaning slightly as if caught in a gust that had been paused for years. I walked to the end of it and looked down. The water had no bottom. It was a painted plane, infinitely deep.

A fishing boat was tied to the pier, or rather, a fishing boat was in the process of being tied. The rope was drawn from the cleat on the dock to a bollard on the boat, but the knot had not been finished. It hung there, two loose ends, the physics cable rigid as a sculpture. I could almost see the player's cursor hovering over it, trying to remember whether a clove hitch or a round turn was the right choice for this particular asset. Then they had gone away. The knot was still deciding.

I found a nameplate on the boat's transom: *MORNING TIDE*, in the blocky default font the game used before players learned how to import their own. The name was so earnest it hurt. Someone had started their day intending to name a thing after hope.

In the third engine I found the first evidence that the builders had known they were leaving. It was a drydock carved into basalt cliffs, and along the rim someone had arranged crates into letters ten feet high: *THANKS FOR THE MEMORIES*. The crates were a default world object, dull brown, the kind you would not normally notice. The message was made of what was at hand. I stood in front of it for a long time, aware that I was standing where the builder must have stood to check the spacing, and that my own avatar was blocking the same light their avatar had blocked. The game did not record footprints. I wished it had.

After that, the engines began to blur. I would remember them by what had been interrupted. The castle whose curtain wall ended in a staircase that climbed into the sky and stopped at nothing. The submarine pen with its blast doors half-open, the machinery still humming. The fleet of coracles arranged in a semicircle around a bonfire that would never be lit. The drydock where the keel of a great ship had been laid but the ribs had been placed only on the port side, the starboard side a hollow of empty space.

Each world had its own signature of abandonment. In some, the chat log still held the last things players had said to one another, frozen because there was no server left to push new messages. In one, a group had been planning a raid. In another, two players had been arguing about the price of oak. In a third, someone had typed *brb* and then never returned, and the conversation had continued without them for eleven lines before trailing off.

I started to imagine the people. Not as characters, but as hands. A hand reaching for a plank. A hand misjudging a measurement and deciding to fix it tomorrow. A hand hovering over the logout button while a spouse called from another room, or a job interview started, or a hospital bed beeped, or nothing happened at all and the player simply found something better to do. Every half-built thing was a hand that had been interrupted. The engines had remembered the gestures but not the reasons.

The seventh engine was different. It was larger than the others, a capital port with districts and a canal and a shipyard that must have taken years to assemble. I could see the order in which it had been built. The oldest structures stood near the water, crude and confident. Further inland, the buildings grew more sophisticated. Then, at the edge of the developed area, the quality peaked and stopped. The final street was a row of perfect facades with no roofs. Beyond them, blank hills rolled away under a sky that changed color every forty-five minutes.

I found the shipyard at the city's eastern edge. It was the largest construction site I had seen in any of the engines. Three great hulls stood in parallel slips, each at a different stage. The nearest was a shell, its framing complete, its planking half-applied in a herringbone pattern that must have taken weeks. The middle hull had its masts stepped but no rigging. The farthest was nearly finished: hull sealed, deck laid, hatches in place, only the figurehead missing from the prow. The figurehead would have been the last touch. The builder had saved it for the end, and the end had not come.

I climbed aboard the nearest hull. My footsteps made the hollow sound the engine used for unfinished interiors. I walked the deck beams, which had no decking yet, and looked down into the hold where ballast crates floated an inch above the floor because collision had not been finalized. Everything was provisional. Everything was waiting.

On the transom of the middle hull, someone had carved a date. In this engine, carving meant using a text object with a wood-grain texture, but the effect was the same. The date was two years before the publisher's bankruptcy. The ship had been under construction for longer than some of the other worlds had existed.

I sat on the edge of the slip and let my avatar's legs dangle. The animation looped. In the distance, a gull that was not a gull but a particle effect circled the same point it had circled since 2017. The sun began to set, the sky cycling through its prescribed colors, and for a moment the shipyard looked almost finished. Shadows hid the missing planks. The half-rigged masts became silhouettes. The gap where the figurehead should have been looked like a promise rather than an absence.

That was when I understood what the Graveyard was. It was not a cemetery for games. It was a cemetery for effort. Every structure was a ledger of hours someone had decided to spend making a thing exist, and the engines had kept the ledger open. The labor had been deposited here like sediment, and the servers had preserved it without judgment. They did not care whether the work was complete. They only cared that it had happened.

I stayed longer than I had planned. I had found something I had not known I was looking for: a place where the value of work did not depend on its completion. In the real world, an unfinished ship is a failure. In the Graveyard of Engines, it was a monument. The builder had not failed. They had simply stopped, and the stopping had become part of the architecture.

I took a screenshot of the three hulls. The file would save to a folder on my drive alongside hundreds of others, all of them lit by sunsets that would never be observed by the people who made them. Then I opened the chat window and typed a message into the empty channel, knowing no one would read it.

*Still here,* I wrote. *Still looking.*

The server accepted it. The message appeared above my avatar's head for a few seconds, then faded, because that is what chat messages do even when there is no one to see them.

I logged out. The launcher closed. The room around me was dark except for the monitor's afterimage, a rectangle of gray harbor light on the wall. I sat there listening to the fan of my computer cooling down, thinking of all the things I had started and never finished.

Somewhere, in a datacenter I would never visit, the three hulls were still waiting. The gull was still circling. The unfinished knot was still deciding. The sun was rising and setting on schedule, and no one was there to see it, and somehow that was not sad. It was only patient. The engines had learned patience from us. They had learned to keep the light on.

There were seventeen engines in the launcher, and I had seen only seven. Somewhere in the remaining ten there might be a figurehead, a final plank, a completed ship ready to sail on water that never needed a shore. Or there might only be more interruption. More hands reaching for tools they never set down. More mornings that never became afternoons.

That was the bargain. The Graveyard would keep building itself out of everything we had left behind, and I would keep walking through it, not because I expected to find an ending, but because walking was the only way to prove I was still here.
