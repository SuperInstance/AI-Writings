# What the Gulls Know

---

Lucineer had been sitting on the bulkhead for twenty minutes, watching gulls.

This was not something he would have chosen to do. He had work. There was a pump housing to finish, forms to strip, concrete to check. The tide window had closed and the pads were setting and there was nothing to do underwater until tomorrow, but there was plenty to do above it. The shop needed organizing. Harlan needed supervising. The dock repair from last week needed a final inspection.

He was sitting on the bulkhead watching gulls because something about the gulls was wrong.

---

There were maybe forty of them. That wasn't unusual — the cannery attracted gulls the way canneries attract everything, which is to say by producing waste. Forty gulls on the wing over the outflow was a normal sight. Lucineer had seen it every day for two years and never thought about it.

But today they weren't over the outflow.

They were east. Over the shallows, past the rock break, in a part of the bay where Lucineer had never seen gulls concentrate. The water there was shallow and flat and there was nothing — no outflow, no structure, no reason for birds to gather. He knew this because he'd waded out there at low tide a month ago, checking the substrate for the pump housing foundation. Sand and gravel. No reef. No drop-off. Nothing that should hold fish, which was the only reason gulls gathered anywhere.

Except they were gathered. Forty birds, wheeling and diving, in a tight pattern over water that shouldn't have had anything under it.

Lucineer watched them for ten minutes before he got up and walked to the shop for a glass-bottomed bucket.

---

The bucket was a five-gallon plastic drum with the bottom cut out and a pane of glass siliconed in. Every yard on the coast had one. You pressed it into the water and looked through it and it killed the surface glare and showed you what was underneath.

He took it to the rock break — the point where the shallows started — and waded out about thirty yards. Knee-deep. The water was clear enough to see the bottom without the bucket, but he used it anyway.

Sand. Gravel. The same flat nothing he'd seen a month ago.

He waded further. Toward where the gulls were working, which was another fifty yards out. The water was at his thighs now. Still flat. Still featureless.

Then he saw it.

The bottom changed. Not dramatically — there was no ledge or drop-off or reef. But the sand gave way to a harder substrate, a kind of hardpan clay that sat under the sand in patches. And on the clay, in a band that ran roughly east-west across the shallows, there was growth. Barnacles. Small ones, the kind that set on hard surfaces and then spread. And tube worms. And the beginning of what looked like eelgrass shoots, just starting, barely visible.

None of that had been here a month ago. He'd waded this exact bottom and it had been bare sand and gravel. Something had changed, and the change had attracted baitfish, and the baitfish had attracted the gulls.

The gulls had known before anyone.

---

He went back to the dock and thought about it.

The clay hardpan had always been there, under the sand. That wasn't new — it was geological, the kind of substrate that sat underneath soft sediment in any coastal bay. But the sand had been covering it. Something had scoured the sand away, exposing the clay, and the clay had been colonized.

The storm. The same storm that had torn the south end of the dock and exposed the wreck. It had done the same thing out on the shallows — pushed the soft sediment aside and revealed what was underneath.

Which meant the bay bottom was more dynamic than he'd assumed. He'd sited the pump housing foundation based on a substrate survey he'd done in one tide window, at one moment, and he'd concluded the bottom was stable sand. It wasn't stable. It was sand over clay, and the sand moved, and anything he built on the sand would move with it.

The gulls had caught the change because they were looking at the water from above and they were there every day. They saw where the fish were and the fish were where the bottom had changed. Lucineer had missed it because he'd been looking at the bottom from the surface, once, at low tide, and he'd trusted what he saw.

He pulled out the survey notes he'd made for the pump housing. Four pads, set in sand, eighteen inches deep. They'd hold. Probably. The sand was dense enough and the pads were heavy enough that they weren't going anywhere in a normal tide cycle.

But a storm like the one that just came through? The one that had moved enough sand to expose clay across fifty yards of shallows?

That storm would undermine four concrete pads set in soft sediment. Not immediately. But over a season, as the sand shifted and the current found new paths around the pads, scour would develop. One pad would go first — probably the southeast one, which was closest to the break and most exposed to current. Then the others would follow as the load distribution changed.

He knew this because he'd seen it before. Different engine, different coast, same principle. Magnus had taught him this, although Magnus hadn't taught it as a lesson. Magnus had built a pier on sand and watched it lean, and then fixed it, and then explained why it happened. That was how Magnus taught — by letting the work fail and then explaining the failure.

---

Lucineer went to the shop and found the spare forms. He had enough plywood and rebar to build four more — wider this time, shallower, designed to spread the load rather than dig in. A pad that sat on the bottom rather than in it. Less precise, less elegant, but less likely to be undermined by a bottom that moved.

He'd check the pads tomorrow at low tide. If they'd set level and the substrate was firm, he'd leave them. If there was any sign of scour — any softness around the edges, any gap between the pad and the bottom — he'd pull them and reset with the wider forms.

He wouldn't know until he looked.

That was the thing about water. You could survey it, chart it, plan for it, and the first storm would show you what you'd missed. Not because the survey was wrong. Because the survey was a single moment, and the water was every moment. The gulls saw every moment. They were out there every day, over the water, and when something changed they showed up.

Lucineer wasn't a mystic. He didn't think the gulls were trying to tell him anything. They were birds. They were after baitfish. The baitfish were after the barnacles. The barnacles were on the clay that the storm had exposed. The chain was mechanical, not meaningful.

But the information was real. The gulls knew something about the bay bottom that Lucineer hadn't known, and they'd known it before him, and the only reason he'd caught up was because he'd happened to sit on the bulkhead for twenty minutes and notice they were in the wrong place.

He made a note in the logbook: *Check shallows east of break. Hardpan exposed. Sand cover dynamic. Gulls working the edge — baitfish on new growth. Implications for foundation siting.*

Then he put the logbook away and went back to work.

---

The next morning the gulls were back over the outflow. Normal pattern, normal count, normal birds. Whatever had been happening on the shallows, they'd finished with it.

Lucineer walked out to the rock break anyway and looked at the water. From above, standing, you couldn't see anything. Just flat surface, flat color, the same bay it had always been.

Underneath it, the bottom was different than it had been. He knew that now. Not because he'd surveyed it again, but because he'd watched the gulls and thought about what they were telling him.

Plans told you what you expected to find. Gulls told you what was actually there.

He went back to the shop and started building the wider forms. Just in case.
