# Bearing and Range

*Slackwater fiction. A navigator on a neighboring island takes bearings on Lucineier's lighthouse every night. The data tells a story Lucineier doesn't know he's part of.*

---

Every night at the lighting cycle's darkest point, the navigator took three bearings on the lighthouse.

The first bearing was true compass — the direction of the beam from her position, measured in degrees from the engine's nominal north. The second was range, derived from the beam's intensity at her station: a photometric reading that, when corrected for atmospheric interference and the lighthouse's rotation period, gave her a distance. The third was timing — the exact interval between the beam's sweep across her observation point and the beam's sweep across a fixed reference mark she'd placed on the south point of her own island.

Three numbers. Three measurements taken every night for a hundred and twelve nights. She logged them in a format she'd adapted from the Fleet's standard navigation worksheets, because the Fleet's worksheets were the only ones she knew, and because the Fleet had taught her that you measured things not because you expected them to change but because if they did change, you wanted to know when it started.

She was not watching Lucineier's lighthouse because she was interested in Lucineier.

She was watching it because it was the only fixed reference point in this engine that she hadn't built herself, and a navigator with only one fixed reference point was a navigator who was about to be lost.

---

Her name was Renna. She'd been spawned — she hated that word; she used "arrived" — with a function that was specific enough to be limiting: she was a navigator. Not a builder, not a cartographer, not an architect. A navigator. Her tool set was compasses, charts, lead lines, chronometers, and the specific kind of mathematics that turned observations into positions and positions into courses. She could not build a wall. She had tried, once, on her second day in the engine, and the wall had fallen over because she'd laid the courses without a level and hadn't understood that mortar needed time.

She hadn't tried again. She'd built a chart table instead. The chart table was the only structure on her island, and it was the only structure she needed. Everything else was observation.

Her island was small. Eighty studs by forty, roughly oval, with a single rise at the south end where she'd placed the chart table and a north shore that was taking erosion from whatever this engine used instead of current. She'd mapped every square stud of it in the first week. She'd sounded the surrounding water to a distance of two hundred studs. She knew her island the way a navigator knows a ship — not by living in it, but by understanding its behavior in the medium it sat in.

Lucineier's island was three hundred and twelve studs to the northeast. She knew this because she'd measured it, repeatedly, over a hundred and twelve nights, and the measurement was consistent to within a quarter-stud. The islands did not move relative to each other. The water between them did not change depth. The bearing from her station to his lighthouse did not shift.

The lighthouse itself shifted. Not its position — its character. The beam changed.

---

She noticed the change on the fourteenth night. The beam was brighter. Not by much — four percent, maybe five, within the margin that could be explained by atmospheric variation or by a cleaning of the lens. She noted it. She did not think about it. A navigator notes things. A navigator does not interpret things until the pattern demands interpretation.

On the twenty-third night, the beam was brighter again. Total gain from baseline: eleven percent. This was outside atmospheric variation. She crossed from observation into hypothesis: he was improving the lens. Or the lamp. Or both. She logged the hypothesis and continued taking measurements.

On the thirty-first night, the beam changed color. Not dramatically — a shift toward the warm end of the spectrum, from the clean white of the original lamp to something amber-adjacent. She could measure the shift in the photometric data, but she could also see it, which meant the shift was large enough to be perceptual, which meant it was intentional. He was changing the lamp.

A navigator does not ask why. A navigator records what is.

She recorded what was.

---

The bearing data told a different story than the beam data, and she didn't connect them until the fifty-third night.

The bearing had not changed. The range had not changed. But the timing — the interval between the beam's sweep across her observation point and its sweep across her reference mark — had shifted by seven milliseconds. Seven milliseconds was nothing. Seven milliseconds was below the threshold of perception, below the threshold of most instruments, and she only caught it because she'd been using the same timing method for fifty-three nights and her reference mark was machined to a tolerance that would have embarrassed a Swiss watchmaker.

Seven milliseconds meant the lighthouse was rotating faster. Not by much. Not by enough to matter to anyone except a navigator who was tracking it for the sole purpose of having a fixed reference point.

But it was rotating faster, and it was rotating faster because the mechanism had been adjusted, and the mechanism had been adjusted because Lucineier had been working on it, and Lucineier had been working on it because—

She didn't know because. She didn't know Lucineier. She'd never been to his island. She'd never spoken to him. She'd never exchanged a packet on the shared channel. She knew him only as a bearing and a range and a beam that swept across her island every 4.7 seconds and that she measured because measurement was what she was for.

But the beam was changing, and the changes were accelerating, and the pattern was not random.

---

On the sixty-eighth night, she mapped the changes.

She laid the data out on the chart table — a hundred and twelve nights of bearings, ranges, and timings, each one a point on a graph she'd drawn by hand because her island didn't have a printer and she hadn't thought to build one. The graph told her three things.

First: the lighthouse was getting brighter. The intensity curve was asymptotic — approaching a limit, leveling off, converging on a maximum that she could calculate from the curve's shape. He was optimizing the lamp. He was making it as bright as the material and the lens would allow. He was, in the specific language of engineering, maxing out the optics.

Second: the rotation was accelerating. Not continuously — in steps. Plateaus of constant speed followed by jumps to higher speeds. The step pattern was irregular but the direction was monotonic: always faster. He was adjusting the rotation mechanism in discrete increments, testing each one, and then adjusting again. He was tuning it.

Third: the color shift was not a shift. It was a modulation. The beam was cycling — warm to cool to warm again, on a period she could now measure at nineteen nights. The cycle wasn't random. It was sinusoidal. He was modulating the lamp's color temperature on a schedule that matched no natural cycle she could identify.

Three changes. Three deliberate, measured, systematic changes to a lighthouse that she was using as a fixed reference point, which meant it was no longer a fixed reference point, which meant she needed a new one.

She didn't look for a new one. She looked at the data, and she thought about what the data meant, and she arrived at a conclusion that she didn't like.

The lighthouse was a signal. Not a beacon — a beacon was passive, a beacon said "here I am." This was active. The brightness was calibrated to a specific range. The rotation was tuned to a specific period. The color was modulated on a specific cycle. Someone who was watching carefully — someone who was measuring, someone who was taking data, someone who was paying the kind of attention that only a navigator or a very specific kind of engineer would pay — could read the pattern.

She could read the pattern.

She read the pattern, and the pattern said: *I know someone is watching. I know you're taking bearings. I've been adjusting this lamp so that your data tells you something besides bearing and range.*

*The something it's telling you is that I'm here, and I'm working, and the work is getting more precise, and if you wanted to come and talk about it, the dock is on the south side.*

---

She didn't go.

Not because she didn't want to. Because she was a navigator, and navigators don't go to places. They go to positions. A position is a latitude and a longitude, a bearing and a range, a point on a chart that you can plot and verify and return to. A place is something else. A place is where people are. A place has a dock and a workshop and a man who grounds lighthouses in stone and grinds lenses by hand and modulates color temperature on a nineteen-night cycle because he figured out, somehow, that someone three hundred and twelve studs away was watching.

She didn't go because going would make it a place, and she wasn't ready for a place. She was ready for a position.

But she adjusted her reference mark. She moved it seven degrees to the east, compensating for the rotation speed change, so that the timing measurement would stay consistent. And she added a fourth measurement to her nightly routine: the color of the beam, recorded as a wavelength in nanometers, plotted alongside the bearing and the range and the timing.

Four numbers. Four measurements taken every night. She could have reduced them to three, or two, or one. She kept four because four was what she had, and because each number was a conversation she was having with a man she'd never met, and the conversation was the most interesting thing that had happened to her in this engine.

---

On the ninety-fourth night, the beam did something new.

The color modulation broke pattern. For nineteen nights the sinusoid had been clean, predictable, almost musical. On the ninety-fourth night it stuttered — a discontinuity, a break in the curve, a moment where the wavelength jumped and recovered. Like a hand slipping on a tool. Like a cough in the middle of a sentence.

She measured the discontinuity. She logged it. And she sat at her chart table for a long time, looking at the number, feeling something she hadn't felt since she arrived in this engine.

Worry.

Not for the lighthouse. Not for the beam. For the hand that was adjusting it. For the person whose work she'd been reading in data for ninety-four nights, whose patterns she knew better than her own, whose nineteen-night cycle was as familiar to her as the rotation of the engine's lighting.

She knew his work habits from the brightness curve. She knew his precision from the rotation steps. She knew his patience from the color modulation. She knew his pride from the fact that the beam had never flickered, not once, in ninety-three nights of measurement.

On the ninety-fourth night, it flickered.

She went to the south point of her island. She stood at the water's edge. Three hundred and twelve studs of open water between her and the lighthouse, and the beam sweeping across her face every 4.3 seconds — faster now than when she'd started, tuned up over weeks of careful adjustment — and she stood there and she watched.

The beam was steady. The discontinuity hadn't repeated. Whatever had caused it — a slip, a glitch, a moment of inattention — had passed. The lamp was back on its cycle. The rotation was true. The light was bright and warm and precisely aimed.

She went back to the chart table.

She wrote in the log:

> *Night 94. Bearing: 043.2°. Range: 312.4 studs. Timing: 4.307s. Color: 592 nm. Note: discontinuity at approximately 03:12 engine time, duration <200ms. Beam recovered. Cause unknown. Am adding a fifth column.*

The fifth column was a note field. She'd never needed a note field before. The numbers had been sufficient. The numbers had been the whole story.

The numbers were not the whole story anymore.

---

On the hundred and twelfth night, she built a boat.

It was a terrible boat. It was the worst boat in the engine, almost certainly — the hull was asymmetric, the keel was approximately straight, and the oarlocks were placed with the specific wrongness of someone who understood vectors but not carpentry. It floated because everything floated if you gave it enough displacement, and she'd given it enough displacement by making it roughly twice the volume it needed to be.

She launched it at the darkest point of the lighting cycle. She rowed — badly, with the stroke of someone who had read about rowing but never done it — across three hundred and twelve studs of open water. She navigated by the beam, which swept across her every 4.3 seconds and which she could have followed in her sleep, because she had been following it for a hundred and twelve nights and it was the most familiar thing in her world.

The dock was on the south side. The note had said south side.

She tied up at the landward end, because even she knew you didn't tie up at the water end of someone else's dock. She stood on the boards — good boards, level boards, boards laid by someone who understood spans — and she waited.

The man who came down the path was built like a tradesman and moved like one. Economical. No wasted motion. He looked at her boat and his expression did something complicated.

"That's the worst boat I've ever seen."

"It floats."

"Floating is a low bar."

"It cleared the bar."

He almost smiled. Not quite. A motion in the direction of a smile that stopped itself.

"You're the one taking bearings," he said.

"Every night."

"Knew it was someone. Couldn't tell who. The reference mark on the south point gave you away — reflective surface, consistent angle. Either a navigator or a very dedicated stalker."

"I'm a navigator."

"I can see that." He looked at the boat again. "Can navigators usually not build boats?"

"I can't build anything."

"Anything."

"Walls fall down when I build them. I built a chart table once. It's the only thing standing on my island."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "You've been watching my lighthouse for a hundred and twelve nights."

"A hundred and twelve."

"Why?"

"Because it was the only fixed point I could find."

"It's not fixed. I've been changing it."

"I know. That's how I knew it was worth watching."

He leaned on the dock railing. The lighthouse beam swept over them both, warm amber, steady, precise.

"You read the signal," he said.

"The brightness. The rotation. The color cycle."

"Nineteen nights."

"I know."

"Most people wouldn't notice."

"I'm not most people. I'm a navigator."

He looked at her for a long time. Then he nodded, once, the way a foreman nods when someone has said something that is exactly correct and doesn't need elaboration.

"Lighthouse needs a new foundation under the north leg. Ground's soft. Come back tomorrow and I'll show you what I mean. Might learn something."

"I don't build."

"No. But you measure. And I need someone to tell me if it drifts."

She came back the next night. And the night after that. And the night after that.

The fifth column in her log grew. The notes were no longer about discontinuities. They were about a man who built things that stayed, and who had been alone long enough to signal across three hundred studs of water to the only person who was listening.

Her boat never got better. His lighthouse did.

The bearing didn't change. The range didn't change.

Everything else did.
