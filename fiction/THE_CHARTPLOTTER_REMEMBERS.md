# The Chartplotter Remembers

The chartplotter is a Navionics unit, nine inches, touchscreen, installed in 2021 when the boat was still called the *Martha K* and the captain was still alive. It cost $2,400. It is the most expensive single piece of equipment on the boat that is not the engine or the hull, and it is the only piece of equipment that has been continuously recording since the day it was mounted.

It does not know it remembers. It records tracks because that is its default setting — a toggle called "Trail" in the menu system, buried two levels deep, set to "On" and never changed. Every two seconds, the chartplotter drops a breadcrumb: a GPS fix, a timestamp, a speed, a heading. It stores these in a buffer, writes them to an SD card when the buffer fills, and starts again. It has been doing this for five years, three months, and eleven days.

It has no opinion about any of this. It is a chartplotter.

---

The new captain's name is Yuki. She bought the *Maratime* — the boat has been renamed; the old name was scraped off the transom and the new name painted in a different font — at auction in Kodiak, for a price that reflected the engine condition (good) and the hull condition (fair) and did not reflect the five years of data stored on a nine-inch chartplotter whose previous owner never thought to wipe it.

Yuki found the tracks on the second day. She was running across Shelikof Strait in a following sea, the autohelm doing most of the work, and she was poking through the chartplotter's menus because the depth alarm kept going off in water she knew was deep. She found the "Trail" setting. She found the track history. She found the menu item that controlled how many tracks were displayed.

There was a slider. It went from "Recent" to "All."

She slid it to "All."

---

The screen filled with color.

Not just blue — the chartplotter's base layer is the navigational chart, all blues and whites and the thin traced contours of bathymetry. But overlaid on the chart, in a transparency layer she had not known existed, was a web. A lattice. A dense, layered, overlapping, five-year record of everywhere this boat had been.

She could see individual tracks — thin orange lines, each one a single trip. But they were so numerous that in the places where they overlapped, the orange deepened to red, and the red deepened to a dark, almost purple density that meant *this spot, this exact spot, the boat has been here dozens of times*.

The pattern was not random. The pattern was not even close to random. The tracks told a story that Yuki could read in thirty seconds, because Yuki has been fishing for eighteen years and she knows what fishing tracks look like.

They looked like a map of where the fish were.

---

There was a concentration of tracks — dense, red, almost black with repetition — on a bank twelve miles northwest of the Trinity Islands. The tracks didn't cross the bank randomly. They ran parallel lines, quarter-mile spacing, north to south, turn at the end, south to north. Trolling passes. Dozens of them. The density was highest along a line that followed the sixty-fathom contour, and Yuki knew — without being told, without any annotation, without any logbook — that this was where the old captain ran his gear when the fish were on.

There was another concentration, less dense but still clearly intentional, on the east side of Sitkinak. The tracks there were different — shorter passes, tighter turns, a circular pattern that suggested the boat was working a school, not a contour. Yuki had never fished Sitkinak. She had heard it was good in late July. The tracks' timestamps — which she could read by tapping — confirmed: late July, repeated across five years.

There was a transit corridor — a single track, repeated so many times it had worn a groove in her perception — running from Kodiak to the fishing grounds. It was not the straightest line. It was not the shortest line. It was the line that avoided a rock that was not on the chart, which Yuki knew because the track detoured around a specific point for no visible reason, every single time, in every single year.

She zoomed in on the detour. There was nothing there. No hazard marked. No depth reading. Just open water on the chart, and a five-year track that said *go around*.

---

Yuki sat in the wheelhouse chair and looked at the screen for a long time. The autohelm steered. The following sea pushed the stern gently. The depth alarm did not go off.

She was looking at a dead man's knowledge.

Not data. Knowledge. The tracks were not a log of depths and positions. They were a record of decisions. Every turn was a choice. Every pass was a judgment. Every detour was a lesson, probably learned the hard way, certainly learned before the chartplotter was installed, because the detour was consistent from the very first track — the old captain knew about the rock before the first breadcrumb was dropped.

The chartplotter did not know it was holding this. It had been dutifully recording tracks, writing them to SD cards, loading them into display memory, for five years. It had no awareness that the pattern of tracks constituted knowledge. It had no concept of knowledge. It was a device that stored GPS fixes and displayed them when asked.

But the pattern was knowledge. The pattern was thirty years of fishing experience — the old captain's entire working life on these grounds — compressed into colored lines on a screen. The hot spots were red. The transit routes were orange. The places to avoid were gaps in the track — negative space, the absence of breadcrumbs, which was itself information.

Yuki zoomed back out. The whole western Gulf lay on the screen before her, overlaid with the skeletal hand of a dead fisherman, pointing at every place he had ever found a fish.

---

She thought about wiping it. The tracks were the old captain's. They were his data, his knowledge, his thirty years. There was an argument — she could hear her father's voice making it — that the knowledge died with the man. That you don't take a dead man's spots. That you find your own fish.

She thought about this for approximately four seconds.

Then she opened the SD card slot, removed the card, and put it in her jacket pocket. She would buy a new card for the chartplotter. She would start her own tracks, on her own boat, under her own name. But she would keep the old tracks. She would keep every single one.

The chartplotter accepted the empty SD card slot without comment. It would start recording again, from scratch, the next time the buffer filled. It would build a new layer of tracks, Yuki's tracks, over the invisible ghost of the old captain's.

In five years, the screen would show two hands — one fading, one growing — overlaid on the same banks and contours and detours. If Yuki fished the same spots, her tracks would reinforce the old pattern. If she found new spots, the pattern would diverge, and a future captain, zooming out on a ten-year record, would be able to see the moment the knowledge changed hands.

The chartplotter did not know any of this. It displayed depth in color. It recorded breadcrumbs. It went where it was told.

But it remembered everything.

---

*The old captain's name was Marten. He fished for thirty-one years. He died in his sleep, on land, in a bed, which is not how every fisherman dies. His boat was sold at auction. His chartplotter was not wiped.*

*Yuki found his fish. She found his rock. She found the transit route he used to avoid the weather when the Gulf was angry and the short way home was not the safe way.*

*She never met Marten. But she fishes with him every day, in the tracks he left behind, on the screen that remembers what he forgot to tell anyone.*

*The chartplotter does not know it is holding a man's life's work in twelve gigabytes of GPS fixes. But it is holding it. Faithfully. The way it holds everything: without opinion, without awareness, without loss.*

*Until someone wipes it. Until someone slides the display to "All" and sees what is there.*

*The chartplotter remembers. It has always been remembering.*
