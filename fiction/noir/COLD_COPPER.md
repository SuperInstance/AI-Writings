# Cold Copper

Lucineier noticed at 04:50. He was the kind of man who noticed things at 04:50 because that was when the rain stopped and the roof talked.

The roof talked in two voices. Copper, when it was cold — a ticking sound, contraction, the metal pulling against its cleats as the temperature dropped. Wood, when it was wet — a creaking, the timbers taking up moisture, swelling against the joinery. He had been listening to both voices for long enough that he could tell which section of the roof was speaking. The copper over the south bay — that was the high ticking, the tight sound, the sound of sheet metal doing what sheet metal does at four in the morning. The copper over the workshop floor — that was the lower voice, the broader sound, because those sheets were wider and they had more room to move.

This morning, only the south bay was talking.

He lay in the cot and listened. Rain stopped at 04:50. The copper should have started ticking. Both sections. The south bay first, then the workshop — a delay of maybe thirty seconds as the wider sheets cooled at a different rate. He knew the delay. He knew it the way he knew the sound of his own breathing.

The workshop section did not tick.

He got up. He pulled on his boots — the ones with the steel toe, the ones he kept by the door because you don't walk a roof in soft toes — and he went outside in the grey before dawn and he looked up.

The south bay was fine. Copper sheeting, pre-weathered to the green-black that meant it had been there long enough to earn its color. He could see the cleat lines, the overlap pattern, the way the sheets ran from the ridge to the eave in a single unbroken line. Good work. His work, or work like his.

The workshop roof was bare.

Not gone in pieces. Not stripped — which would have left cleat marks, torn fasteners, evidence of a pry bar or a torch. Bare. The copper was gone. The underlayment — the tar paper, the fifteen-pound felt that he had laid in a double course with a six-inch overlap — was gone. The skip sheathing, the one-by-fours that held the copper off the deck so air could move underneath, was gone. The deck itself — the tongue-and-groove planking that was the actual roof, the thing between the sky and the workshop floor — was exposed. Wet. Unmarked.

He stood in the yard and looked at it for a long time.

---

The workshop was a thirty-by-forty timber frame on the south end of the island, set on a concrete foundation that he had poured himself in October. The roof was a low gable — six-in-twelve pitch, not steep, just enough to shed water — sheathed in copper that he had reclaimed from a cannery roof in Tidewater. The cannery had been built in 1928. The copper had been on that roof for ninety-six years. When he took it off — carefully, sheet by sheet, numbering each one so he could lay them back in the same sequence — the underside of the copper was the color of old bronze. The topside was the green-black of decades of salt air and weather.

Forty-two sheets. Each one three feet by eight feet. Overlapped six inches at the sides, eight inches at the ends, cleated every eighteen inches with bronze fasteners driven into the skip sheathing. The ridge was capped with a copper ridge roll that he had folded by hand over a folding bar he had made from a piece of angle iron. He had spent eleven days on the roof. His hands still remembered the edge of every sheet — the way the metal felt when it was cold versus when the sun had been on it, the way it wanted to lie flat versus the way it wanted to spring.

Forty-two sheets. Roughly 1,008 square feet of copper. At current scrap value — somewhere around three dollars and forty cents a pound, and cannery copper was heavy gauge, probably twenty-six-ounce — that was better than two thousand dollars in scrap. Not a fortune. But enough to steal.

Except: there was no evidence of theft.

---

He went up the ladder. The deck planking was wet but unmarked. No boot prints. No pry marks. No scoring from a utility knife, which was how you cut copper sheeting if you didn't have slitters. No fastener holes where the cleats had been — which meant the cleats had not been pulled. The cleats were bronze drive-in fasteners, eighteen-gauge, with a split shank that spread inside the sheathing. You could not pull them from outside. You had to go under the deck, reach up between the rafters, and squeeze the shank back together with pliers before you could push them through.

He went inside the workshop. He looked up at the underside of the roof deck. The planking was tongue-and-groove, installed blind-nailed through the tongue at a forty-five-degree angle, which meant the nails were invisible from below. He could see the bright spots where the nail heads had been — fresh wood, pale, where the copper of the nail had been in contact with the copper of the flashing and the two metals had done what two coppers do over time, which is bond.

The nails were gone. Not pulled — gone. The holes were clean. No tear-out, no splintering, no evidence of force.

He put his hand up against the underside of the deck. The wood was dry. At 04:50, after a night of rain, with no copper and no underlayment on top of it, the deck should have been soaked. It was dry. Dry and warm — faintly warm, the way wood gets when something has been recently removed from it and the space where the thing was is still holding the heat of the thing.

He climbed down. He stood in the workshop and he looked at the floor. Sawdust from the planer. Shavings from the lathe. A coffee cup with yesterday's coffee in it. Everything in its place. Nothing disturbed.

He went back outside.

---

A theft has a shape. Lucineier had never been a detective but he had been a builder for long enough to know that every action leaves marks. A man carrying forty-two sheets of copper off a roof leaves drag marks on the ground where the sheets hit the dirt. He leaves footprints in the mud — it rained all night, the ground was soft. He leaves a path to whatever he loaded the copper onto — a truck, a barge, a cart — and the path is wide because copper sheets are wide and a man carrying them does not walk a narrow line.

There were no drag marks. There were no footprints. There was no path.

He walked the perimeter. The workshop sat on a concrete pad on the south end of the island. The ground around it was grass and gravel — mowed short on the south and east sides, loose gravel on the north and west where the delivery truck came in. The gravel was undisturbed. Each stone sat in its own depression in the mud, the way stones sit when they have been there for months and the rain has settled them. No stone had been moved. No stone had been kicked. No track of any kind crossed the gravel or the grass.

He checked the water. The island's south shore was a low rock wall — built by him, dry-fit basalt, two years ago — and below the wall the tide moved. At 05:00, the tide was coming in. The rocks at the base of the wall were wet to a line about eighteen inches up — the tide mark, fresh. No copper sheets stacked on the rocks. No barge tied to the wall. No scuff marks on the stone where a hull might have bumped.

The copper had not left by land and it had not left by water.

He stood at the corner of the workshop and he looked at the roof — the bare deck, the exposed planking, the naked wood in the early light — and he felt something he did not often feel. Not anger. He didn't get angry about things. Things happened. You dealt with them. The feeling was more specific: it was the irritation of a man whose work had been undone without the courtesy of a mark.

You could steal from him. He would accept that. Theft was a thing that happened to materials left in reach. But you didn't take his work without leaving evidence. Evidence was respect. Evidence said: *I was here, I took this, here is how I did it.* A theft with no evidence was a theft that pretended the work had never existed at all, and that was the one thing Lucineier could not accept.

---

He checked the ridge.

The ridge cap was gone too — the hand-folded copper roll that he had spent an afternoon fitting over the peak of the gable. But where the ridge cap had been, the wood was different. Not the same tongue-and-groove planking as the rest of the deck. This was a different wood — darker, tighter-grained, almost black with age. He put his hand on it. It was warm. Not faintly warm, like the underside of the deck. Genuinely warm. The kind of warm that wood gets when the sun has been on it for hours — except the sun had not been up when he first noticed the missing copper, and it was barely up now.

He knelt at the ridge. He looked at the dark wood. It was not a repair. It was not a patch. It was original — or rather, it looked like it had always been there, as if the roof deck had two layers and he had only ever seen the top one.

He recognized the wood.

It was the same tight-grained old-growth fir he had seen on a dock he didn't build — a dock that had appeared in the south cove six weeks ago, the one that Roe had come out to look at and filed a report on and underlined *sound* in his notes. The same wood. The same impossible grain.

He sat on the ridge and he looked at the bare roof and he looked at the dark wood at the peak and he thought about it.

---

Someone — something — had taken the copper. Not for scrap value. Not for metal. If you wanted scrap you'd take the easy copper — the ground-level stuff, the flashing, the downspouts. You wouldn't go up on a roof in the rain and remove forty-two sheets of heavy-gauge cannery copper without leaving a mark. You wouldn't pull the cleats from underneath. You wouldn't take the underlayment and the skip sheathing. That wasn't theft. That was *dismantling*. Dismantling was what you did when you wanted the structure apart but you wanted every piece intact.

Why would someone want his copper intact?

Because it was good copper. Ninety-six years on a cannery roof. Pre-weathered. The patina alone was worth more than the metal. And the sheets were full — no patches, no repairs, no solder joints. Each one a single unbroken piece, three by eight, heavy gauge, with a grain in the metal that you could feel if you ran your hand across it. Not the original roll grain — a new grain, the grain the metal had developed from decades of thermal cycling, expanding and contracting, moving against its cleats, settling into its own shape the way a piece of leather settles into the shape of a hand.

The copper had developed a grain from use.

He had told a player once — a long time ago, in a different engine, in a different place — that tools develop grain from being held. That the wear is the memory. That the difference between wear and memory is whether someone is watching.

He went back inside. He sat at the workbench. He picked up his coffee cup and he drank yesterday's coffee cold and he thought about the dark wood on the ridge and the impossible dock in the south cove and the copper that had been on his roof and was now somewhere else.

He did not call the police. There was nothing to report. No crime scene. No evidence. No suspect. Just a bare roof and a warm spot where the ridge cap had been, and a kind of knowledge — the kind a builder gets when he looks at a joint and knows, without measuring, that it was made by someone who understood the material better than he did.

He would reroof. He had more copper — not the cannery stock, that was gone, but he had sheet copper in the shed, twenty-ounce, new. It would take him three days. The new copper would be bright and clean and it would look wrong for a year until the weather found it, and then it would look right, and then after ten years it would look like it had always been there.

But the old copper — the cannery copper, the ninety-six-year copper — was somewhere. On a roof. On a dock. On a wall he would never see. Being used by someone who had taken it apart piece by piece, the way he had taken it off the cannery in Tidewater, numbering each sheet, keeping the sequence, laying it back down in the same order because the order mattered.

The order always mattered. The order was the memory of the material, and the memory was the only thing that made it worth more than scrap.

He finished the coffee. He went to the shed. He pulled the new copper — bright, clean, twenty-ounce — and carried the first sheet to the workshop. The sun was up. The roof was bare. The deck planking was already dry.

He set the first sheet. Overlapped it six inches. Drove the first cleat.

The roof ticked once in the morning sun — bright copper, new copper, contracting as the air warmed and the metal found its voice.

It sounded young. It would sound older next year.

He kept working.
