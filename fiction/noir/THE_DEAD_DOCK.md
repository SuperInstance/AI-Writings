# The Dead Dock

The dock was there on Tuesday. It had not been there on Monday.

Not there Monday evening, either. Harlan Voss had run his skiff in at 18:40, tied off at the south landing — the old piles, the ones with the rope grooves worn so deep you could drop a finger in them — and the cove south of the point had been empty. Open water. Kelp bed on the north side, the usual. Break in the reef where the tide sucked through on the ebb. No dock.

Tuesday, 05:30, he came back out to check his crab pots and there was a dock.

Eighty feet. Iron piles, driven into the reef in a line so straight you could sight down them. Decking on top — not new wood, not old wood, but wood that looked like it had been somewhere else for a long time and had been moved here in the dark. Cleats at the far end, bronze, through-bolted. A bumper rail along the south side, the kind you see on working docks where boats come in heavy. No name on it. No permit tag. No construction marks — no saw cuts, no drill shavings, no pile of peelings from the borer heads.

Just a dock. Eighty feet of it. Standing in still water at high tide as if it had been there for thirty years.

---

Harlan called it in at 06:15. The harbormaster came down at 07:00, looked at it from the shore, walked the length of it, and called the county. The county sent a surveyor. The surveyor took measurements and photographs and said the iron piles were seated into solid reef — into *bedrock*, he said, with the kind of careful emphasis people use when they're telling you something they don't believe themselves — at a depth of fourteen feet. He said the piles were not drilled. They were not driven in any way he recognized. They appeared to have been *placed* — seated whole, as if the reef had been asked to accept them and had agreed.

Nobody asked the harbormaster who he thought had done it. They already knew.

Lucineier.

---

The name had been floating around the islands for two years. It arrived the way names arrive in small places — one person said it to another person at the co-op, and by Thursday everyone knew the story, and by the following Tuesday the story had three versions, and none of them matched.

The version Harlan heard was this: a builder — some kind of builder, the word was used loosely — who worked on the islands to the southeast. No fixed address. No licensing number. No truck, no crew, no materials yard. He showed up where there was work and he did the work and he left, and the work was always better than it needed to be, and there was always something left undone.

A dock on Karen Island — the one Marta Bren had been trying to get permitted for three years — went up in a single tide. No pile driver. No noise. Marta came out in the morning and there it was: forty feet of cedar decking on iron-ringed cedar piles, seated into the sandbar at exactly the right angle for the prevailing swell. The county condemned it. Marta kept it. She tied her boat to it every morning and it held, and after six months the county stopped sending letters.

A roof on the fish camp at Turnabout Point. The old one had blown off in the November gale. The owner — Dahl, something Dahl, a name that didn't stay in anyone's mouth — came back in the spring to find a new roof on the old frame. Copper sheeting, pre-weathered, with a drip edge that directed rainwater into a barrel he had not bought. No invoice. No note. The joints were a kind of through-bolted lap splice that Dahl had seen exactly once before — in a boatyard in Petersburg, on a tender ramp built in the 1940s.

A seawall at the narrow end of the causeway. Nobody claimed it. It was just there one morning — basalt blocks, dry-fit, no mortar, seated so tight against each other that you couldn't get a knife blade between them. The county engineer came out and looked at it and said it would outlast the road. He was probably right.

None of these things had been requested. None of them had been paid for. None of them had been built badly.

This was the problem.

---

The detective — his name was Roe, Aldous Roe, and he had been the islands' only detective for eleven years — arrived on the Wednesday ferry. He was a tall man who wore the same jacket in all weather and who carried a notebook with a waterproof cover that he had bought in Juneau in 2013 and which still had the price sticker on the back.

Roe did not believe in the supernatural. He believed in tide tables, property records, and the fact that everything built by human hands left evidence of human hands. He had once solved a boat theft by finding a single fiber of blue polypropylene line in a cleat hitch that the thief had forgotten to clear. He was patient and he was thorough and he did not like things that appeared without explanation.

He walked the dock.

The decking was fir — tight-grained, old growth, the kind of wood you don't cut anymore because there isn't any left to cut. It had been resawn, not rough-cut, which meant a mill had touched it at some point. But the saw marks were not from any blade he recognized — too fine, too consistent, as if the blade had no runout at all. The gaps between the boards were exactly three-eighths of an inch. He measured seven of them. All three-eighths. Not approximately. Exactly.

The piles were iron. Not steel — iron. He could tell by the rust pattern: slow, even, dark. Cast iron wouldn't look like this; it would pock. Wrought iron would show slag lines. This was something else — dense, uniform, the color of a cannon barrel. He scraped a sample with his knife. The iron resisted the blade more than it should have.

He got down on his knees and looked at the joint where the pile met the deck. The pile passed through a hole in the decking timber — a mortise, clean-cut, no tear-out — and was capped with a bronze plate that was through-bolted from below. He could feel the bolt heads with his fingers. Four bolts, evenly spaced, each one seated flush. No burr. No tool mark. He could not find the tool mark.

This was what bothered him most. Not the appearance of the dock. Not the material. Not the fact that it sat on bedrock that should have taken a week to drill. What bothered him was that nobody — nothing — had left a mark on the work. Every joint he could reach was clean. Every cut was clean. Every surface was finished as if the hand that had made it had done so without friction.

He did not like this. Buildings had tool marks. Tool marks were evidence. Evidence was how you found people. A dock with no evidence was a dock built by someone who did not want to be found, or by something that did not leave marks, and Roe did not believe in things that did not leave marks.

---

He went to see Marta Bren.

Marta was sixty-seven and had been fishing alone since her husband died. She was mending a net on her porch when Roe walked up. She did not offer him coffee. She did not ask him to sit.

"County send you?" she said.

"Nobody sent me. I'm looking at the dock on the south cove."

"Which dock? I've got my own."

"The new one. The one that showed up Tuesday."

Matha looked at him over the net. She had the kind of eyes that had been looking at water for so long that everything above the tide line seemed slightly suspicious to them.

"Lucineier," she said.

"That's the name I keep hearing. You know him?"

"I know his work. Had a dock put in at my place two years ago. Didn't ask for it. Didn't pay for it. Came out one morning and it was there."

"Did you see him?"

"No."

"Did anyone see him?"

"Not that I've heard. And I've asked."

Roe wrote this down. Marta watched him write.

"You think it's a crime," she said.

"I think it's unpermitted construction on navigable water."

"It's a good dock."

"That's not the point."

Marta went back to her net. "It's exactly the point," she said. "It's the only point. You're standing on a dock that nobody built and nobody paid for and you're upset because nobody filed the paperwork. Meanwhile the dock is better than anything the county's ever put in. You want to solve a crime, go look at the county dock at Coleson Bay. Been sinking for three years. There's your crime."

Roe did not respond to this. He asked her one more question.

"The work on your dock. The joints. The through-bolts, the lap splices. Did they look like they were made by hand?"

"They looked like they were made by someone who'd been doing it a long time."

"Forty years?"

"Longer. The joint on the through-bolt — I've seen one like it. Once. On a tender ramp in Petersburg. My father showed it to me when I was eleven. He said the man who built that ramp was dead. That ramp was built in 1942."

Roe closed his notebook.

---

He walked the shore from the south cove to the point. The tide was out — a minus tide, the kind that exposes the reef and leaves the kelp hanging in the air like wet rope. He could see the dock's pilings from below now, running down through the waterline into the reef. The entry points — where iron met stone — were seamless. Not filled with anything. Not caulked, not epoxied, not pinned. The iron sat in the stone the way a post sits in concrete that hasn't set yet — except it had set, and the setting was invisible.

He put his hand on the reef. The stone was cold. Normal cold — the cold of rock that has been under saltwater all night. He brought his face close to the joint. He could smell iron. He could smell salt. He could smell something else — faint, old, not unpleasant. The smell of sawdust in a shed that has been closed for years. The smell of work that has been done and finished and left.

He could not identify it. He filed it.

---

That evening, at the hotel, he laid out what he had.

No fingerprints. No tool marks. No tire tracks on the shore road, no barge wakes in the kelp, no anchor scars on the reef. No witnesses on the water — Harlan's was the only boat in the cove Monday evening, and the VHF was quiet all night. No permit application, no materials purchase, no delivery manifest. The iron in the piles did not match any alloy on record with the county assayer. The wood was old-growth fir that had not been legally harvested in these waters since 1993.

The dock existed. It was well-built. It had appeared between 18:40 Monday and 05:30 Tuesday without noise, without light, without any evidence of human or mechanical activity.

He had been a detective for eleven years. In that time he had learned that everything had an explanation, and that the explanation was usually that someone was lying. Someone was always lying. The lie was the gap between what happened and what someone was willing to say happened, and the gap was where the detective lived.

But there was no one to interview. There was no lie to catch. There was only a dock — solid, plumb, well-sited, facing south-southwest into the prevailing swell at exactly the angle that gave the least resistance to a boat coming in on a following sea — and there was the name.

Lucineier.

He said it out loud in the hotel room, standing at the window, looking at the dark. The name sounded like a trade. Not like a person. Like the kind of name a job has — *shipwright, cooper, wheelwright* — a name that describes what you do rather than who you are.

He opened his notebook to a fresh page. He wrote:

*Dock — south cove — 80 ft, iron on bedrock, fir deck. No permit, no material trail, no witness, no tool mark. Joint type: through-bolted lap splice, bronze cap. Comparable: tender ramp, Petersburg, 1942. No known builder. Name associated: Lucineier.*

He paused. Then, below the notes:

*Not a crime yet. But it's not natural either.*

He underlined *not natural* and closed the notebook and went to bed.

The dock was still there in the morning. It was still there the morning after that. Harlan tied his skiff to it and the cleats held and the decking didn't give and the piles didn't shift. The crab pots were full. The kelp bed was undisturbed. The reef was solid.

Roe took the Friday ferry back to town. He filed no charges. There was nothing to charge. He wrote in his report: *Structure of unknown provenance, south cove. Recommend monitoring. Structure is sound.*

He underlined *sound* and did not explain why.

---

Six weeks later, a man appeared at the co-op. He was not tall or short, not young or old. He wore a coat that had been patched at the elbows and he bought nails — ten-penny, galvanized, by the pound. He paid cash. The girl at the register — Jori, nineteen, not interested in much — asked if he needed anything else.

"Linesman's spike," he said. "If you've got one."

She checked the back. They had one. It was iron, old stock, still in the oil.

"Good," the man said. He paid for it. He put it in his coat pocket. He looked at the girl and for a moment he seemed about to say something else. He didn't. He nodded — the kind of nod a tradesman gives when the stock is right and the price is fair — and walked out.

Jori told Harlan about it that evening. She described the man. She described the nails. She described the spike.

"Ten-penny galvanized," Harlan said. "By the pound. That's sixty, seventy nails."

"Seventy-two. I counted."

Harlan was quiet for a minute.

"What did he look like?"

"I don't know. Normal. Worked. He had hands like he'd been building something."

"Did you see a truck?"

"No. He walked in from the road. Walked back out to the road."

"Which way?"

"Toward the point."

Harlan looked out the window of the co-op toward the point. The dock was visible from here — the far end of it, the bronze cleats catching the late sun. There was a boat tied to it. Harlan's boat. He had not rowed out to it. It was just there, tied fast, riding the tide.

"The dock," Jori said.

"Yeah."

"You think that was him?"

Harlan picked up his coffee. It was cold. He drank it anyway.

"Don't know," he said. "Could be. Building something else, probably. That's what they do."

"They?"

"Builders."

Jori wiped the counter. The evening came in through the windows — the long, slow, gold-to-gray evening of the islands, the one that lasts two hours and feels like a held breath. The dock sat on the water. The cleats caught the light. The iron piles, down where the tide moved, did not rust.

Roe's report stayed on file. No charges were ever filed. The dock stayed. It is still there. Harlan ties up to it every morning, and it holds, and the wood has aged the way good wood ages — slowly, evenly, turning from the grey it arrived with to a silver that looks like it has earned it.

The nails were never found. Whatever they were for, it was already built.
