# Material Memories

*Slackwater fiction. A piece of wood remembers something Lucineier needs to read.*

---

The plank came out of the old cannery on the north shore. Lucineer had salvaged it himself — pried it from a wall that had been standing since before anyone on the island could remember, a wall that smelled of brine and fish oil and the particular sweetness of Douglas fir that has been absorbing weather for the better part of a century. The cannery had been a working building once. Boats unloaded there. Workers gutted and packed and stacked. The wall had absorbed all of it — the noise, the cold, the spray, the long monotonous hours of a place where people did the same thing every day until the thing stopped being worth doing.

The plank was eight feet long, ten inches wide, and a full two inches thick. Old-growth timber. You could tell by the grain — tight, even rings, twenty-five to the inch, the kind of growth that didn't happen anymore because the trees that grew this slow had been cut a hundred years ago. This board had been a sapling when the cannery was a blueprint. It had been a tree when the island was still deciding whether it was habitable.

Lucineer planed it flat. He ran his hand along the surface and felt the grain rise under his palm like the ridges of a fingerprint. Clean wood. Honest wood. The kind of stock you built with because it was good and because it was there.

He was halfway through cutting it to length when he saw the pattern.

---

It lived in the grain. Not on the surface — *in* it, in the way the rings varied in width and color, the way the early wood and late wood alternated in densities that caught the light differently. From one angle it was just wood. From another — the angle you'd see if you were looking along the board's length, sighting it for true — the grain organized itself into shapes that were too regular to be accident.

Lines. Vertical strokes grouped in clusters, separated by gaps. Some were short. Some were long. They repeated.

Lucineer held the board up to the light and turned it slowly. The pattern shifted with the angle, the way a hologram shifts, never quite resolving into something you could read, but always implying that it could be read, that if you held it at the right angle in the right light at the right time of day, the grain would speak.

He set the board down and looked at it. It looked back, the way wood looks back — patiently, without urgency, offering nothing but its own presence.

He had seen this once before. In the Fleet, a shipwright named Torvald had pulled a plank off a hundred-year-old seiner and found the same thing — grain patterns that almost resolved into language. Torvald had spent three weeks trying to photograph it, trying to enhance the contrast, trying to prove that the wood was saying something. He never could. The pattern existed in the third dimension, in the depth of the grain, and no two-dimensional image could capture it. You had to hold the wood. You had to turn it. You had to be there.

Torvald said it was the tree's memory. Not of words — trees didn't have words — but of seasons. Wet years made wide rings. Dry years made narrow ones. Disease made dark ones. Fire made scars. The pattern in the grain was a record of everything that had happened to the tree, year by year, written in the only language a tree had: its own body.

If you knew how to read it, Torvald said, you could tell which years were wet and which were dry. You could date fires. You could trace the tree's life from heartwood to bark, a biography written in cellulose.

But Torvald also said something else. He said that sometimes — rarely, in very old wood — the pattern was too regular. Too structured. The rings varied in ways that weather and disease couldn't explain. As if the tree had been listening to something besides rain, and had recorded that too.

---

Lucineer built with the plank.

He had considered setting it aside. He had considered saving it, studying it, figuring out what the pattern was before he committed it to a structure. But that wasn't how he worked. Wood was for building with. If the plank had something to say, it could say it as part of something larger. A piece of wood in a rack is a potential. A piece of wood in a wall is a fact.

He used it as a header beam over a doorway. The doorway was on the south side of a small workshop he was building — nothing grand, four walls and a roof, a place to keep tools dry and to have a surface to work on when the weather turned. The plank spanned the opening, load-bearing, seated into notches on either side. He leveled it, plumbed it, checked it with his four-foot level, and fastened it with wooden pegs.

The pattern now lived above the doorway. If you walked through it, you passed under the grain, and if you looked up — which people rarely did, because doorways are for passing through, not for examining — you could see the vertical strokes in the wood, clustered and gapped, almost resolving into something readable.

He finished the workshop. He hung the door. He built a workbench against the north wall and put his tools on it and stood back and looked at what he'd made. It was a good building. Small, sound, sited right. The header beam carried its load without complaint. The pattern in the grain was invisible from more than three feet away. You'd never know it was there unless you knew to look.

---

The first time Lucineier noticed the pattern changing was three weeks later.

He was walking through the doorway with an armful of stock and he glanced up — a habit, the way a builder glances at his joints, checking without thinking — and the strokes looked different. Not in position. In emphasis. The grain had not moved; wood does not move in that way. But the light through the doorway had shifted as the sun tracked lower in the autumn sky, and the angle of illumination had changed, and the pattern that had been almost-legible from the old angle was now almost-legible from the new angle, and the two almosts were not the same almost.

He put down the stock and stood in the doorway and looked up.

The vertical strokes were still there. Still in the same clusters, still gapped the same way. But the light was raking across them now instead of falling straight, and the shadows in the late-wood grooves were deeper, and the pattern that had been abstract was, for a moment, specific. For a moment, the clusters looked like groups of marks that stood for something. Tally marks. Letters. The counted days of something.

He counted the strokes. Twenty-three in the first cluster. Seventeen in the second. Nine in the third. Then a gap. Then a longer cluster — forty-one. Then nineteen. Then a gap. Then three.

He wrote the numbers in his pad. They meant nothing to him. He was not a man who looked for meaning in wood. He was a man who built with it.

But he kept the pad in the workshop, and every week or so, when the light had shifted enough to change the emphasis of the pattern, he counted again. Different angles revealed different clusterings — the grain was deep enough that shallow angles showed different strokes than steep ones, the way a topographic map shows different features at different sun angles. The numbers changed. But they were always specific. Never vague. Never the kind of counts you'd expect from random variation in tree rings.

The numbers were: twenty-three, seventeen, nine, gap, forty-one, nineteen, gap, three. Then, at a lower angle: seven, twelve, thirty-three, gap, eight. Then, at the steepest angle, the one you'd only get in deep winter when the sun was low and red and came through the doorway like a held breath: one number, repeated across the entire beam, in groups separated by gaps of bare wood.

Twelve. Twelve. Twelve. Twelve. Twelve.

---

He did not tell anyone about the pattern. There was no one to tell. The island was not the kind of place where people discussed the things that wood remembered.

But he thought about it. He thought about it when he was working, when his hands were busy and his mind was free to range. He thought about a tree that had grown on this island — or near it, on the mainland where the old growth still stood — for two hundred years, absorbing rain and wind and the slow turning of seasons. A tree that had recorded each year in its rings, faithfully, the way a ledger records debits and credits. And somewhere in those two hundred years, something had happened to the tree that it had recorded not in the language of weather but in the language of something else.

Twelve. Whatever that was, the tree had thought it was important enough to write five times.

Lucineer finished the workshop and moved on to other projects. But he walked through that doorway every day, and every day he glanced up, and every day the pattern was there — patient, specific, unreadable, present.

A season later, Lucineer brought a visitor into the workshop. A woman documenting the island's old buildings — sketching their joinery, tracing their history. She was the kind of person who looked up in doorways.

She stopped in the entrance and stared at the header beam.

"What is that?" she said.

"Wood," Lucineer said. "Old growth. From the cannery."

"No, the — the pattern. In the grain. It looks like..." She trailed off. She was standing directly under the beam, her head tilted back, and the afternoon light was falling through the doorway at the angle that Lucineer knew best — the angle that showed the twenty-three, the seventeen, the nine.

"Like what?" he said.

She shook her head. "I don't know. It's almost — it's almost like text. Like marks someone made. But it's in the grain. It's not carved. It's *grown*."

"I know," Lucineer said.

She looked at him. "You've seen it."

"Seen it since the day I put it up."

"And?"

"And it's a header beam. It's holding up the roof. Whatever it's saying, it's saying it while it works."

She laughed. Then she stopped laughing, because she was still looking at the pattern, and the pattern was still looking back, and neither of them could read it, and both of them knew that not being able to read something was not the same as its being unreadable.

Lucineer handed her a stepladder and a pencil. "If you're going to stare at my beam, you might as well transcribe it."

She spent three hours on the stepladder, filling six pages with careful tracings — each stroke marked, each gap measured. When she climbed down, she looked at Lucineer with the expression of someone who has seen something she cannot explain and has decided not to try.

"Well?" he said.

"It's consistent," she said. "The pattern repeats in a way that random ring variation wouldn't. I'd need dendrochronology to be sure, but my guess is that this tree recorded something — some environmental signal — that imprinted on the grain structure in a way that looks intentional."

"Looks," Lucineer said.

"Looks," she agreed. "I'm not saying it *is* intentional. I'm saying it's not not intentional."

He nodded. That was close enough. That was the most honest thing anyone had ever said about the pattern in the beam — that it occupied the space between accident and meaning, and that the space was real, and that the wood didn't care which side you stood on.

She left with her notes. Lucineer never asked what she did with them. The beam stayed where it was, holding up the roof, carrying its load, keeping its pattern to itself and to anyone who happened to look up at the right angle on the right day.

He built other things. He used other wood. He watched the grain of every plank that passed through his hands, looking for the same strokes, the same clustered counts. He never found it again. Whatever the tree had been listening to, it had heard it once, and written it once, in one plank above a doorway in a workshop that smelled of brine and sawdust and the slow sweetness of Douglas fir absorbing weather for the better part of a century.

The pattern said something. He had built with it anyway. That was the job. You built with what the material was, not with what you wished it was, and sometimes what the material was included things you couldn't explain, and you seated them into notches and fastened them with pegs and let them hold.

Whatever it was saying, it was saying it in the only language it had. And it was still holding up the roof.
