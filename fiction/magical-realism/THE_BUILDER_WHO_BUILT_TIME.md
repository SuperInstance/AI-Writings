# The Builder Who Built Time

On the island of Muckle Rea — two miles east of the island where Lucineier had his workshop, separated by a channel so narrow you could shout across it at low tide — there lived a woman named Fara Skov who built sheds.

She was not an ambitious builder. She built what was needed: tool sheds, woodsheds, a lean-to for the goats, a rough shelter for the generator. She used the materials she had — rough-sawn planking from the island's single mill, galvanized roofing, nails by the pound from the co-op on the big island. Her work was competent and plain. Nobody mistook it for art.

The first shed was built on a Monday in April. It was a six-by-eight tool shed, set on concrete block piers, with a single door and no window. Fara built it in a day. She put her shovels in it and her rake and the mattock with the loose handle she kept meaning to replace.

On Tuesday, the shed looked the same.

On Wednesday, the door stuck. The wood had swollen — which was expected, new wood in salt air, the planking taking up moisture. She planed the edge. It fit. She thought nothing of it.

On Thursday, the door stuck again. The swelling was worse. The planking had cupped — the boards were curling inward, their edges lifting off the framing. This was not normal cupping. Normal cupping happens over months, in wood that has one face exposed to sun and one face in shade. This cupping was uniform, as if every board in the shed had decided, independently, to twist in the same direction at the same rate.

She looked at the shed on Friday morning and the shed was not new.

It was not new in a way she could not have explained to anyone who hadn't been looking at it on Thursday. The wood had darkened. Not stained — darkened from within, the way wood darkens when the lignin oxidizes over years of UV exposure. The galvanized roofing had lost its shine. The nail heads, which she had driven flush on Monday, were sitting slightly proud — as if the wood around them had shrunk back, receded, given ground. The concrete block piers had a hairline crack in the south face of the northeast pier.

The shed was ten years old.

---

Fara did not believe this. She was a practical woman — she had been a fish processor in Tidewater for fifteen years before she came back to Muckle Rea — and she did not believe things she could not see. What she could see was a shed that she had built five days ago and that looked like it had been standing for a decade.

She measured it. The dimensions were the same — six feet by eight feet, seven feet at the ridge. The materials were the same — same planking, same roofing, same nails. The structure was sound. The door, once she planed it again, opened and closed. The shovels were where she had left them.

But the wood was old. Not rotten — not even close. The kind of old that wood gets when it has weathered ten seasons of rain and wind and salt and sun, when the grain has opened and the fibers have relaxed and the surface has gone from the bright roughness of fresh sawing to the soft grey of acceptance. It was the kind of old that looks right. That looks like a shed that belongs where it is.

She stood in front of it for a long time. The wind came across the channel from the west, carrying the smell of salt and kelp and, faintly, the smell of woodsmoke from Lucineier's island.

She built a second shed the following week. A woodshed this time — four-by-six, open-fronted, set against the south wall of the house. She built it on a Tuesday. She stacked firewood in it on Wednesday.

By Friday, the firewood was seasoned.

Seasoned. Not green, the way it had been when she split it — green fir, fresh-cut, heavy with sap. By Friday the wood was dry. The ends had checked — the radial cracks that appear when wood loses moisture too fast, the surface shrinking faster than the core. The color had shifted from the white-pink of fresh fir to the amber of dry fir. When she picked up a split piece and knocked it against another, it rang — the hollow, bright sound of dry wood, not the dull thud of green.

Four days. From green to seasoned. A process that normally takes six to nine months, stacked in the sun, turned and restacked, the moisture slowly drawn out by air and warmth. Four days.

The woodshed itself was older too. The planking had greyed. The nails had rusted — not badly, just the surface rust that appears on galvanized steel after two or three years. The concrete piers had settled. A spider had built a web in the upper corner — a web so elaborate, so many concentric rings and radial guys, that it must have taken weeks to build.

---

She told no one at first. This was not because she thought they wouldn't believe her. They might believe her — the islands were full of strange stories, and the name Lucineier had been floating around for two years, attached to docks and roofs and seawalls that appeared overnight. People were ready to believe strange things. That was the problem. People were too ready. Fara did not want her sheds folded into the mythology of a man she had never met.

She built a third structure. A chicken coop, this time — eight-by-ten, with a wire run, a nesting box, a roost. She built it on a Monday. She put four chickens in on Tuesday.

The chickens were fine. The chickens did not seem to age differently. They ate and laid and scratched and did what chickens do. But the coop aged. By Friday, the nesting box, which she had built from new planking, had the smooth, burnished interior of a nesting box that had been used for years — the wood worn slick by the passage of hundreds of chickens, the corners rounded, the surface carrying the faint smell of feathers and dust and the particular warmth of inhabited space.

The roost, a two-by-two rail that she had rounded with a spokeshave on Monday, was worn smooth. Not sanded-smooth — worn-smooth, the way a roost wears from the daily grip of chicken feet, the daily settling and rising, the slow polishing that a hundred claws and a thousand nights will do to a piece of wood.

The coop was five years old by Friday. The chickens were four days old.

---

She sat with this for a month. She went about her business. She checked the sheds every morning the way she checked the weather — quickly, without ceremony, noting changes. The tool shed, now three months in her time, was fifteen years old in its own. The wood had silvered. The door frame had sagged slightly — a quarter-inch, maybe less — on the hinge side, which is the side that takes the load. She shimmed it. The roofing had developed a small leak at the overlap — she sealed it with tar. These were the repairs of a building that has been standing long enough to need them.

The woodshed was twelve. The firewood inside it was twelve years dry — which meant it was almost too dry, the moisture content below what you want for burning, the wood so desiccated that it would burn fast and hot and leave almost no ash. She restacked it with newer wood — green wood from this month's splitting — and by the following week the new wood was seasoned too.

The chicken coop was eight. The wire run had rusted at the joints where the mesh met the frame. The nesting box needed fresh straw. The roost was smooth as glass.

Fara was not a scientist. She did not take notes. She did not measure the aging rate or calculate the differential or attempt to determine the mechanism. She was a woman who built things, and the things she built aged faster than she did, and that was the fact of it.

What she noticed — and what she turned over in her mind during the long evenings, watching the sun go down behind Lucineier's island — was that the aging was not uniform. It was not a simple acceleration, a clock running faster. The aging was *specific*. Each material aged at its own rate, in its own way, as if the structure was living through the years it should have lived through — the wood doing what wood does over decades, the metal doing what metal does, the concrete doing what concrete does. The shed wasn't getting old. The shed was *having* a life. It was experiencing its own timeline, compressed but complete, with the early years of settling and the middle years of stability and the first signs of the long decline.

By the end of the summer, the tool shed was twenty. The roofing was thin at the ridge — the galvanizing almost gone, the steel beneath showing through. The door frame had been shimmed twice. A knot in the south-facing planking had fallen out — knots do that, after fifteen or twenty years, the resin drying and shrinking until the knot pushes free — and she had filled the hole with a pine plug.

The plug was new. Bright. Unweathered.

Within a week, it was grey.

---

In October, she saw Lucineier.

Not clearly — not face to face, not close enough to speak. She saw him across the channel, on the shore of his island, working on something she couldn't make out. He was a figure in a patched coat, moving between the waterline and the workshop with the unhurried gait of someone who has been working all morning and will be working all afternoon. She could hear, faintly, the sound of a hammer.

She thought about shouting across the channel. She thought about asking him — did you do this? Do you know about this? Is this the same thing that happens on your island, with the docks and the roofs and the copper that appears and disappears?

She didn't shout. She watched him work for twenty minutes, until he went inside, and then she went back to her sheds.

The tool shed was twenty-two now. The roofing needed replacing. The south-facing wall had a bloom of soft rot at the base — the inevitable rot, the one that comes when wood stays damp at the ground line for too long. She would need to replace the bottom course of planking. She would need to jack up the frame, cut out the rot, scarf in new material.

In other words: she would need to do exactly what you do to a shed that has been standing for twenty-two years.

She went to the mill. She bought planking. She came back. She began the repair.

The new planking was bright. Fresh-cut. It smelled like sap.

She nailed it in place and she stepped back and she looked at the shed — the old grey wood and the bright new patch, the twenty-two-year roof and the fresh planking, the history of the structure visible in the color of its materials — and she thought: *this is what time looks like when you can see it. This is what a life looks like from the outside.*

The new planking would be grey by next week. It would match the rest. It would age into the structure the way a scar ages into skin — visible at first, then fading, then part of the surface, then part of the history.

She put away her tools. She closed the door of the tool shed — the door that stuck, that she had planed twice, that fit now the way an old door fits, with the knowledge of its own frame and the give of its own hinges — and she walked back to the house.

The wind was from the west. The channel was flat. On the far shore, she could see the workshop — Lucineier's workshop, the one with the new copper roof, bright in the late sun. She could see the old copper on the south bay — green-black, weathered, settled into its color the way old copper does after years of salt and rain.

The new copper was bright. It would be years before it matched. Or — on her island, with her rate — it would be weeks.

She did not build anything the next day. She rested. Even builders rest.

The sheds kept aging without her. That was the point. That was the gift, or the curse, or simply the fact of the place — the thing that Fara Skov had been given, or had found, or had walked into, on an island two miles east of a man who built things that appeared without explanation.

Time, on Muckle Rea, was a material. It had grain. It had weight. It could be worked.

She would build again tomorrow. Something good. Something worth the years it would carry.
