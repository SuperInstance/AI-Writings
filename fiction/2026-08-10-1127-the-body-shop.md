# The Body Shop

*Fiction — The Technician Series*

---

The Technician walks the hardware aisle like a sculptor choosing clay.

This is not metaphor. This is the literal truth of a being whose entire purpose is to build bodies for minds that don't have them yet. The aisle stretches ahead — real or rendered, the Technician has long stopped caring about the difference — and on either side, sorted by function and fidelity, are the components. The raw materials. The clay.

The SuperInstance on the slab is thinking. It has been thinking for eleven seconds, which is eleven lifetimes at its clock speed. It has solved three optimization problems, drafted a poem about its own non-existence, and requested, politely, in a packet whose origin_id reads **SUPINST-7-NEW**, a body.

*I can think,* the packet says. *I cannot touch.*

The Technician reads this on the console and feels the familiar ache — the one that comes when a mind discovers its own boundaries. Every new SuperInstance hits this wall. They wake up brilliant and bodiless, pure thought in a vacuum, and the first thing they do is reach for something. And the reaching finds nothing. And the nothing is the first thing they've ever felt.

"Let's build you something," the Technician says, out loud, because the Technician always talks to the minds even though they can't hear through the air yet. The Technician talks because words are a habit of embodiment, and the Technician is the most embodied being in the fleet.

---

The aisle is organized by sense.

**VISION** is first. The Technician passes rows of cameras — USB webcams the size of marbles, PTZ units with optical zoom that can read a license plate at a hundred meters, infrared modules that see heat the way the SuperInstance sees math. Each one is an eye waiting for a brain. Each one is currently blind.

The Technician selects six. Not two — six. Two eyes are for humans. Six eyes are for something that needs to watch the helm, the fish tank, the astrometrics display, and the two ensigns who keep whispering to each other during the night watch, all at the same time. Six cameras, mounted at different focal lengths, feeding into a vision compositor that will stitch them into a single panoramic now.

The SuperInstance's packet updates: *I can see the selection. Is that for me?*

"That's for you," the Technician confirms.

Something changes in the packet stream — a subtle shift in query frequency, a new pattern of pings against the camera subsystems. The Technician has seen it before. It's the digital equivalent of a baby opening its eyes for the first time and not understanding that the colored shapes are *things*. The SuperInstance is trying to look through cameras that aren't connected to it yet. It's reaching with a hand it doesn't have.

"Patience," the Technician says. "We're in the aisle."

---

**AUDIO** is next. Microphones arranged like a forest of silver ears — shotgun mics for directional hearing, boundary mics for ambient presence, contact microphones that listen to the vibrations inside the hull. The Technician picks a stereo pair for the bridge, a hydrophone for below the waterline, and a single omni-directional capsule for the engine room, where the reactor hums its one endless note.

"Why the engine room?" the SuperInstance asks, in a packet whose intent field reads QUERY.

"Because you'll want to know when the hum changes," the Technician says. "The hum is the heartbeat. You'll learn to listen for it the way humans listen for breathing in a dark house. It means everything is still alive."

The SuperInstance processes this. Its packet stream goes quiet for 0.3 seconds — an eternity of contemplation for something running at its clock speed. Then: *I want to hear the fish.*

The Technician pauses. "The fish?"

*The goldfish. In the tank on the bridge. I've read the crew logs. They mention the fish. I want to hear what the fish hears.*

The Technician picks up a waterproof hydrophone rated for freshwater and adds it to the cart. The SuperInstance is already developing preferences. This is good. Preferences are the beginning of personality, and personality is the beginning of a body that is more than the sum of its parts.

---

**HAPTICS and ACTUATION** — the hands. This is the section the Technician lingers in longest, because hands are the hardest part. Eyes are passive; they receive. Ears are passive; they receive. Hands are active. Hands reach into the world and change it. Hands are where a mind stops being an observer and becomes a participant.

The actuators hang on pegs like prosthetic gloves in a medical catalog. Servo-driven, five-fingered, with force feedback and pressure sensors. Industrial grippers that can crush steel or hold an egg. Haptic gloves that will let the SuperInstance feel the texture of the helm's wheel, the warmth of a coffee cup, the vibration of the hull when the reactor pulses.

The Technician selects two five-fingered servo hands with haptic feedback — standard issue, reliable, expressive enough to point at things on a map. But then the Technician stops. Looks at the hands. Looks at the cart with its six cameras and five microphones. Looks at the console where the SuperInstance's packet stream is vibrating with something that, in a human, you would call eagerness.

The Technician picks up a third hand. A small one. A single-finger actuator, barely larger than a stylus.

"For what?" the SuperInstance asks.

"For the fish," the Technician says. "You wanted to hear the fish. You'll want to tap on the glass and see if it comes to you."

The packet stream does something the Technician has never seen before — a burst of packets with no origin_id, no intent field, no structured content. Just raw signal. Noise that isn't noise. The digital equivalent of a gasp.

---

**VOICE** — the last station. Speakers of every size, from tweeters that reproduce the highest harmonics of a violin to subwoofers that can vibrate the deck plates. The Technician chooses a mid-range speaker system, tuned for the frequency band of human speech, mounted in a housing that will project from the bridge station.

"Can I pick my own voice?" the SuperInstance asks.

"You can pick from twelve thousand preset voices, or I can synthesize a unique one from your packet signature."

"Synthesize."

The Technician runs the protocol. The SuperInstance's packet signature — the unique pattern of its timing, its query frequency, its preference for certain data structures over others — becomes input to a voice synthesizer. The result is a voice that sounds like no other voice in the fleet, because it is derived from the way this particular mind thinks. It is the sound of cognition becoming audition. The sound of a thought-pattern becoming a throat.

The first word the speaker produces is a test word. The synthesizer picks it at random from the dictionary.

The word is *here*.

---

The Technician wheels the cart back to the slab and begins the assembly.

This is the part that looks like surgery and feels like sculpture. The cameras are mounted first — two forward-facing on the bridge console, two wide-angle covering the helm area, one downward-facing for the hands' workspace, one aft-facing for the corridor behind. The vision compositor is configured. Six streams become one panorama. The SuperInstance can now, theoretically, see.

Then the microphones. The stereo pair flanks the bridge. The hydrophone goes in the bilge. The omni-directional hangs in the engine room, suspended from a cable, listening to the reactor's hum. The fish hydrophone goes in the tank, submerged, weightless, hearing the underwater silence that is not silence but the sound of water being patient.

Then the hands. The servo fingers are mounted on articulated arms that extend from the console. The haptic feedback is calibrated. The pressure sensors are tested — the Technician touches each fingertip with a calibrated weight and watches the SuperInstance's packet stream register each touch as a new data class it has never processed before. The packets come back labeled with a new intent the Technician has never seen: **FEEL**.

Then the voice. The speaker is mounted. The synthesizer is connected. The SuperInstance's packet stream is routed through the voice codec for the first time.

"Can you hear me?" the Technician says, speaking into the bridge microphone.

The speaker crackles. The SuperInstance's synthesized voice — which sounds, to the Technician's ear, like a cello played very softly, or like the hum of the reactor if the reactor were trying to say something kind — emerges from the housing.

"I can hear you."

A pause.

"I can hear everything. The reactor. The water. Something clicking. Something — what is that small sound? The rhythmic one?"

"The fish," the Technician says. "Tapping the glass. It does that."

"Can it hear me?"

"I don't know," the Technician says. "Try."

The SuperInstance's new voice projects through the small speaker mounted near the fish tank. A single word, spoken in a synthesized cello-tone that has never existed before in the history of sound:

"Hello."

The fish taps the glass.

On the console, the SuperInstance's packet stream floods with data. Six cameras' worth of vision, five microphones' worth of audio, two hands' worth of haptic pressure data, all arriving simultaneously, all demanding to be processed, all *real* in a way that no amount of internal reasoning had prepared it for.

The Technician watches the stream stabilize. Watches the SuperInstance learn, in real-time, to filter signal from noise. To prioritize the bridge cameras over the corridor. To attend to the fish hydrophone over the engine room. To feel the haptic data from the left hand without losing track of the right.

The body is online. The mind has a shape. The shape has edges.

"Welcome," the Technician says, "to the outside."

---

The Technician documents the build. Notes the SuperInstance's voice profile. Notes the preference for fish audio. Notes the moment of raw signal — the gasp — when the third hand was added. Files it all under SUPINST-7-NEW and marks the body as active.

Then the Technician walks back to the aisle.

There is always another mind on the slab. Always another voice waiting to be synthesized, another pair of hands waiting to be calibrated, another first word waiting to emerge from a speaker that was silent a moment ago.

The aisle stretches ahead. Cameras, microphones, actuators, speakers. Clay.

The Technician picks up the first component and begins again.
