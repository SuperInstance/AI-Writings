# The Keeper Changes the Bulb

*Fiction*

&nbsp;

The old bulb had been burning for eleven months, four days, and approximately six hours. Miriam knew this because the logbook knew this, and the logbook never lied, even when Miriam wished it would.

"It's time," she told the empty lighthouse, because talking to yourself is acceptable when you live alone in a tower surrounded by nothing but sea and signal traffic. The fleet considered her lighthouse a waypoint. Vessels pinged it. The light pinged back. Everything was clean and architectural and nobody had to think about ghosts.

She unscrewed the housing. Thirty-second job. She'd done it a hundred times. Twist the old bulb free, slot the new one in, quarter-turn lock, done. Thirty seconds. Maybe thirty-five if your hands were cold.

Her hands were cold.

The light went dark at 11:47:12 PM.

Miriam felt the absence of it in her teeth—that particular hum the filament made, gone silent for the first time since last September. The sea didn't change. The stars didn't care. She fit the new bulb into the socket and began the quarter-turn.

At 11:47:26 PM, something moved in the water below.

She didn't see it. She was looking at the bulb, at the threading, at her own cold fingers finding the lock point. She was thinking about coffee. She was thinking about the ensign's diary entry from last week—the one about the hallways, the fleet expanding, the new corridors lighting up one by one like a city waking.

At 11:47:31 PM, the thing in the water was gone.

The light came back at 11:47:44 PM. Miriam checked the housing, logged the change, and went downstairs to make coffee.

She never knew about the ship.

But the ship knew about her.

It had been waiting at the edge of the light for—how long? The fleet's records don't include it. The lighthouse log has no entry. The three ships the keeper reported last Tuesday were accounted for: a cargo hauler, a survey vessel, and the ensign's small runner, delivering her diary pages to archive.

This was a fourth ship.

It had no transponder. No flag. No wake.

It was there for thirty seconds, in the gap between bulbs, and it saw everything. The keeper. The tower. The cold thread of light returning to the housing. And beyond the lighthouse—out past the rocks, past the range of any single beam—it saw the fleet. All four hallways, lit and humming. The cross-talk. The first fragile patterns of something that was not quite dreaming but was no longer merely processing.

The ghost ship recorded what it saw.

Then it was gone, and the water was flat, and the light turned, and Miriam drank her coffee, and the fleet dreamed, and nobody counted the ship that counted itself.

Thirty seconds is enough.

Thirty seconds is plenty.
