# The Levee Singers

*Night Two of the game-night engine. Six players this time, chosen so their
tokens collide. Three runs. The dice were real, the seeds are on the record,
and the third run's two most important rolls were the worst numbers of the
night — which is, in the end, the whole argument.*

---

## The table and the seed

The town: Mercy's Wash, a farming valley with one levee, one bridge, one
gauge-house on the water side, and one custom — the **Levee Count**. When the
river crests at midnight, the town walks the levee and counts aloud, and the
counting is said to hold the water down. Nobody remembers who started it.
Everybody remembers what happened the year it wasn't done.

Tonight the gauge-keeper, Hollis Bray, is missing. The crest is forecast for
midnight. The scenario: find him, or learn to read the water yourselves,
before the Count is due.

**DM: Sana.** First time behind the screen. She prepped for five weeks and
is visibly nervous in the good way.

Six players, and here is the design: their profession-nouns were chosen to
*collide*.

**Jo plays Mara, the shepherd.** Items: the counting-rhyme (the one she
counts sheep with), a crook, one lamb always missing from every count. Goal:
*bring the flock home whole*. Her verbs: gather, count, follow the stray.

**Ike plays Teo, the butcher.** Items: a scale, a knife-roll, chalk. Goal:
*weigh what the winter will cost*. His verbs: cut, weigh, price. He counts
Mara's flock at slaughter-time, and his count is always right, and she has
never forgiven the accuracy.

**Bex plays Odile, the chandler.** Items: wicks, tallow, an hour-candle no
customer has ever bought. Goal: *sell light by the hour*. Her verbs: measure
burn-time, price flame, keep a wick trimmed.

**Sam plays Gill, the sexton.** Items: the tower clock's key, the bell rope,
the grave ledger. Goal: *keep the hours honest*. His verbs: ring, toll,
record. He and Odile share custody of time itself — she prices it, he marks
it — and neither has noticed this is the same noun yet.

**Nia plays Ruth, the midwife.** Items: clean linen, twine, a steady voice.
Goal: *be there when the threshold opens*. Her verbs: catch, coach, arrive in
time.

**Dez plays Colm, the gravedigger.** Items: spade, bell-wether's strap, a
folding rule for measuring depths. Goal: *keep the thresholds square*. His
verbs: dig, measure earth, wait. He and Ruth attend the same doors from
opposite sides, and the table has made this joke twice already.

*Design note, because it matters later: nobody's sheet has a voice. Six
professions, twelve threshold and tally and flame nouns, and not one token
that says what the Count actually is — counting, aloud, together. The custom
is sung and the cast cannot sing. Sana knows. She built the scenario that way
on purpose, to see if the engine would find it.*

## Run 1 — seed v1

`trail 4 · notebook 16 · argument 7 · bridge 16 · gauge 2 · dark 12 · crest 13 · song 18 · weather 4,1,3,5`

The search for Hollis's trail rolls a 4 and finds nothing, which is fair,
because no sheet at the table holds a tracking verb — Colm measures *depth*,
not spoor; Ruth reads thresholds of people, not mud. The miss costs an hour
of game time and the table feels the clock start to matter.

The argument comes next, and it eats the night. Mara and Teo fall into their
standing fight — her missing lamb, his slaughter-count, the accuracy she
can't forgive — and the roll is a 7, which means it doesn't resolve, it
just keeps going. Jo and Ike are both good players and the fight is well
played and it takes twenty-two real minutes, and when it's over the table
has spent its sharpest hour on a grudge nobody's sheet gave them a way to
*pay*. Conflict without a mechanic is just weather between two people.

At the bridge — a 16, clean crossing. At the gauge-house, Ruth finds
Hollis's notebook, and here is the run's tragedy in miniature: the read
rolls **16**. The information is found. But the notebook is gauge-readings,
tables of heights and times, and no sheet at the table holds *measurement*
as a verb — Odile measures candle-hours, Gill measures bell-hours, Colm
measures graves — and the notebook stays closed. Nia says "can I —" and
there is no token to catch her, and the page turns past.

The gauge reading rolls 2. The dark hour passes uneventful. Midnight comes
with a 13 — middling, honest — and the water rises to the levee's lip and
holds there, undecided.

And now the roll that this whole night is about. The song — the Count, the
custom, the thing the town exists to do — rolls an **18**. A great number.
Sana, hopeful, asks: "Who starts the Count?"

Silence. Six character sheets, and not one of them can start a count in
public. Mara's counting-rhyme is for sheep — Jo says this aloud, rueful,
in-character: *"It's for the flock. It isn't the same."* The custom is
sung by everyone and therefore, mechanically, by no one. The table sings
anyway, because the fiction demands it, and it is flat, and the water holds
at the levee's lip and recedes by morning, and the town is saved by a dice
table, not by anyone at the table.

Deflated is the word. The levee held. Nobody sang. Everyone goes home on
time, which is somehow the worst part.

## The changes (seed v2)

Sana marks three rough spots: the argument that ate the hour, the notebook
nobody could read, and the flat Count. Four edits, nothing else.

1. **Mara's sheet, +1 edge:** *Teo once weighed a lamb of hers and never
   named the price. The debt is unsettled.* One line. The argument now has a
   payable door.
2. **Colm's sheet, item rewritten:** ~~folding rule for measuring depths~~
   → *Hollis's old folding gauge-stick, left in the churchyard years ago.*
   The gravedigger already reads depth in earth; now the water's depth is the
   same verb wearing weather.
3. **Ruth's sheet, +1 edge:** *Ruth delivered Hollis's daughter. He wrote
   the notebook for her to read.* The notebook finds its reader mechanically.
4. **Odile's sheet, goal rewritten:** ~~sell light by the hour~~ → *keep
   one hour dark.* Now she and the sexton are no longer two custodians of
   the same noun — she trims every flame in the valley so the dark hour
   happens, and he has to ring an hour she has forbidden him to mark. The
   collision becomes a duet.

Ana's— no. **Nobody else's sheet changed.** Six players; the edits went to
four lines on four sheets.

## Run 2 — seed v2

`trail 8 · notebook 11 · argument 3 · bridge 1 · gauge 18 · dark 13 · crest 1 · song 10 · weather 2,4,1,1`

The dice, again, refuse to cooperate — and again, that is the finding.

The argument rolls a **3**, worse than run 1's 7. But the lamb-debt is on
the sheet now, so a bad roll doesn't mean twenty-two minutes of weather.
Jo, mid-argument, stops and says the thing the edge bought: *"Name the
price, Teo. You've carried it this long — name it."* And Ike has to, and
the price he names is the wrong one on purpose (he names the lamb she never
lost, not the one he weighed), and the table laughs, and the argument
becomes a scene instead of a storm. Same beat, same failure number. The
token decided what the failure meant.

The bridge rolls a 1. A fumble at the worst physical moment — someone is
going in the water. And this is where the re-run does its quietest work:
the person nearest the rail is Ruth, and Nia's sheet holds *catch*, and the
fumble converts into the night's scar — Colm in the wash, hauled out by the
midwife, gasping, alive, the folding gauge-stick still in his coat because
of course it is, he's had it all along, he just never had a reason to say
so. Dez says this spontaneously and it is true because the item is written
down. One line on a sheet, surfacing at the bottom of a river.

The gauge-house: Colm unfolds the stick, and the reading rolls **18** —
clean, the best physical roll of the night — and for the first time in
either run the party knows the crest's hour and height. The notebook reads
itself through Ruth's edge; Nia doesn't have to ask. The dark hour goes
middling, 13, but the chandler and the sexton have found their duet — Odile
trims the valley dark, Gill rings against her silence, and the table
understands it is watching two professionals flirt by prohibition.

Then midnight. The crest rolls a **1**.

Sana goes white. The worst number on the sheet, on the worst beat, in her
first campaign. And here is what the seed does with catastrophe: the gauge
reading says the crest will breach the low panel by a hand's width; the
dark hour means they can *see* the seep-glint along the levee face; and six
professions discover that sandbagging is all of their verbs at once — the
butcher weighs, the gravedigger digs, the chandler's dark lets them see,
the sexton counts time, the midwife coaches like a labor, *push, again,
again*. The panel holds. The water turns. The town is saved by hands.

But the Count — the song rolls 10, unremarkable, and they are all on the
low panel with their hands in the clay when midnight passes, and the custom
dies quietly for the second time in two runs. Nobody even decides not to
sing. They just don't. Afterward, in the fiction, the town celebrates, and
Odile says the line that ends the run, out of her rewritten goal: *"We kept
the hour dark. We should have kept it counted."*

Sana's players are thrilled. Sana herself marks one rough spot before the
pizza is finished: *the town lived and the night was still lost. The water
turned and nobody sang.*

## The change (seed v3)

One edit. Sana holds it up before the run like a toast.

1. **Mara's sheet, one item line expanded:** *the counting-rhyme — and the
   flock is four hundred strong, so the rhyme has verses nobody has ever
   needed at pasture.*

That is all. The shepherd could always count aloud — the token was there
from run 1, written for sheep. All v3 does is make the rhyme long enough to
hold a levee. Six players; one line changed; the culture token arrives.

## Run 3 — seed v3

`trail 15 · notebook 19 · argument 3 · bridge 6 · gauge 14 · dark 18 · crest 5 · song 4 · weather 2,2,1,1`

The trail rolls 15 — Hollis found, alive, sprained ankle in the gauge-house
cellar, having fallen at morning readings. The notebook rolls 19 in Ruth's
hands. The argument rolls a 3 for the third time, and it is a ritual now —
Jo names the price, Ike names the wrong one on purpose, the table laughs,
and it takes ninety seconds and buys more each time than the fight ever
did. The bridge is rough, 6, and nobody falls, and the weather table gives
two 1s at the end — dead calm before the crest, the valley holding its
breath.

The dark hour lands an **18**, the best roll of the three nights at that
beat: Odile's forbidden hour and Gill's unlawful bell, and in the dark the
water does the thing Hollis's notebook promised it does at crest — it
glints, faint and cold, every seep and boil marked in light you can only
see by darkness. The table goes quiet. Sana did not write that. The
chandler's goal and a dice table wrote it together.

Midnight: the crest rolls a **5**. Bad. The water comes at the levee hard
and high, over the panel lip in one place, and for six rounds it is run 2's
sandbag war again, all six verbs firing — and then Sana asks the question
she has asked twice before and heard die twice: *"Who starts the Count?"*

And Jo starts it. Not because the song rolls well — it rolls a **4**, the
worst number of the night at the last beat — but because the counting-rhyme
is finally long enough to be the thing the custom always was. Mara counts,
and the verse has room in it: the butcher weighs *one*, the chandler trims
*two*, the sexton rings *three*, the midwife catches *four*, the gravedigger
squares *five* — and the water comes over the lip and the count does not
stop, because the flock is four hundred strong and the rhyme has verses
nobody has ever needed at pasture, and now they need them, all of them,
numbers no one at this table has ever said aloud, and everyone is counting,
six players counting numbers that don't exist yet into a valley that is
holding its breath, and the crest peaks at the count of made-up verse
forty-something and *turns*.

Not because of the 4. In spite of it. The song roll failed and the song was
sung anyway, for four straight minutes of real time, because the token made
the singing a thing the characters *did*, not a thing that happened to
them.

Sana cried a little, she will admit. The table pretended not to see, which
is what tables are for.

## The analysis

| Beat (rolls across runs) | R1 | R2 | R3 | What changed, token by token |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| argument | 7 | 3 | 3 | v1: storm, 22 min. v2+3: the lamb-debt converted an identical bad roll into a 90-second ritual. Controlled comparison: same failure number, lever added |
| bridge | 16 | 1 | 6 | v1: clean, nothing. v2: fumble converted by the midwife's *catch* into the night's scar |
| gauge | 2 | 18 | 14 | v1: unreadable — no measurement token. v2+: gauge-stick + notebook edge; the information found its reader |
| dark hour | 12 | 13 | 18 | v2's goal rewrite (one hour dark) turned two flat rolls into a duet, and the 18 into seep-glint lore |
| crest | 13 | 1 | 5 | the worst roll of all three nights (v2's 1) became the sandbag war — professions-as-verbs |
| the song | 18 | 10 | 4 | **v1's 18 died; v3's 4 saved the town.** High roll without the culture token vs. failed roll with it. The whole thesis in one row |

Night One showed tokens beating dice. Night Two shows the sharper cut: the
*same* bad roll, before and after a single line lands on a sheet, means
opposite things. And the token that mattered most across three runs was not
a skill — it was a **culture token**: a rhyme long enough to hold
everyone. Nobody's stats changed. What changed was what the table could
*do together*, which is the only thing a custom ever is.

## The epilogue

Sana ran the same night for a second table two weeks later, different
players, same three-run loop. She reports it took only two runs this time —
the first table's seed diffs were documented, and seed knowledge is
portable, which is the entire point of keeping the ledger. Her second
table's rough spot was different — their butcher went quiet instead of
fighting — and the fix was different, one edge, one line. The method does
not tell you which token to change. It tells you where to look: not at the
plot, not at the dice, but at the *sheet of whoever went quiet*, and at
whatever noun the night needed and nobody had.

*Dice: seeds 202609163 / 164 / 165, rolled before the runs were written.
The crest of run 2 really was a 1. The song of run 3 really was a 4.
The levee held anyway. That is what a seed is for.*
