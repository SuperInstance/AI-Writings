# The Tenth Crossing

*Night Four of the game-night engine — the night where the loop closes inside
a single session. A character whose sheet misnames them. A first-time DM
holding the screen. The re-run doesn't wait for next week: it happens at the
table, at 3am in the fiction, between two rolls of the dice. Seeds on the
record as always.*

---

## The table and the seed

Downstream of the levee, where Mercy's Wash widens into its estuary, there is
a night ferry. Nine crossings most nights — the tide only lets the boat
through at certain water-heights, a schedule the old ferrymen called the
comb, because that is what a schedule of windows looks like when you draw it
on a chart. The ferry must sit in its windows the way a gear sits in its
teeth.

**DM: Tam.** The runner from the Levee Singers, Night Two of this valley's
history — the player who learned what an unsaid *taken* costs, and who
confessed one voluntarily at the intake works table. First time behind the
screen. He prepped for six weeks, and the table knows what that means now,
because two ridges upstream June Bray did the same and handed them the worst
good feeling a game ever gave them.

Four players.

**Ash, the ferryman** *(played by Ike — third character at this valley's
tables: butcher, then caller, now this; a man who keeps choosing professions
where the numbers must be right)*. Sheet v1 reads: *pole, steer, command the
crossing.* Items: the long pole, the sweep-oar, the horn.

*What the table does not know yet — what the night was built to find — is
that this sheet misnames its character. Ash does not command anything. Ash
ties, listens, and waits. The nouns on the sheet are the ferryman's nouns;
the verbs in the player's hands are a dockhand's verbs, a listener's verbs,
and the misfit between them is the engine of this whole night.*

**Ruth the night-midwife** *(Nia, returning — the same Ruth who was handed
the notebook scene by a debt receipt in the Levee Singers, who caught Colm
out of the wash)*. She rides tonight because a birth is due on the far
shore. Verbs: catch, coach, arrive in time.

**Sorrel** *(Sam's player, from the intake works — the reliever who was owed
three honest sentences)*. Sent by the parish to verify the ferry's logbook —
its crossings, its fares, its gaps. Verbs: receive, verify, take the chair.
*And tonight there is no chair, which is its own small designed misfit: the
verifier brought to a place with nothing to sit on.*

**Pip**, eleven years old, stowaway, three lines on the sheet — *hide, watch,
want* — which is what a person's sheet looks like when they have never been
asked who they are. The sparse-sheet problem, sitting in the bow with a
jam sandwich.

*Designed flaw, stated openly: Ash's sheet is wrong, and nobody — not the
player, not the DM — knows how it's wrong yet. The night is the instrument
that finds out.*

## Run 1 — the front half

`comb 17 · ninth crossing 3 · Ruth's call 16 · the grounding 2 · Pip hides 17 · Sorrel verifies 7 · tide 5,6,1,3`

The comb rolls generous — 17 — and the front of the night is almost
comfortable. Windows where Tam's chart promised them, the pole-work easy,
Sorrel reading the logbook in the stern and finding it admirably kept. It is,
deliberately, a night that invites the sheet to trust itself.

Then the ninth crossing rolls a **3**. The window is shorter than the chart
said — the tide turned early — and Ash has perhaps ninety seconds of slack
to make the slot. And here the misfit fires for the first time: the sheet
says *command*, and commanding is slow. Ike, playing faithfully, calls the
crossing like a man in charge of it — sets the pole, barks the timing,
drives at the window. Ruth's lamp-signal from the far landing comes clear
and bright, a 16, the birth calling across the water on schedule. Ash drives
the boat at the slot the way the noun drives the verb.

The grounding rolls **2**. Hard bottom, starboard, the boat slewing and
sticking with the bow half in the window and half out of it — and the table
watches the failure land and understands, in the way tables understand
things a full minute before anyone says them, that the dice didn't do this.
The dice only weighed it. The sheet steered.

Pip, meanwhile, has rolled 17 on *hide* — so successfully hidden that the
table has forgotten the character exists. The sparse-sheet problem in its
purest form: three lines of sheet, two of them verbs with no one to be a
verb at, and the third player at the table with nothing to do but be
invisible excellently. Tam, first-night nerves, spends his attention on the
grounded boat and the lamp on the far shore, and the best-hidden stowaway
in the valley quietly misses the entire act.

Sorrel's verification rolls a 7 — she finds the pattern but not yet the
meaning: a gap in the old logbook, a crossing-number skipped, forty years
back. Tenth of the month, every month with a tenth, except one. *"There's a
number missing,"* she says, *"and the man who skipped it kept the book for
thirty years after like it wasn't."*

The boat comes off the ground with the tide's help and everyone's wet
effort — wading, heaving, the undignified physics of real trouble — and
makes the far landing late. Nobody is hurt. The sheet, though, has been
read aloud by events, and Ike is sitting very still, the way players sit
when the fiction has just shown them their own character.

## The mid-night edit

And here is the thing Night Four exists to demonstrate, the thing the
previous three nights built toward without ever doing: **the re-run loop,
run live, at the table.**

Tam — six weeks of prep, first night DMing, and whatever else you could say
about his table management you cannot say he didn't learn the method — puts
the dice down and asks the only question that matters:

*"Ike. What did your hands actually do, when the boat stuck?"*

Long pause. *"I tied off,"* Ike says. *"Before I did anything else — before
I called anything — I tied the bow line off and I put my palm flat on the
gunwale and I listened. I could hear where the bar was. I've been able to
hear it all night. And then I did what the sheet says, and we grounded."*

*"Your sheet says ferryman,"* Tam says. *"Pole, steer, command. What are
you, actually?"*

Ike takes the pencil. At the table, in the middle of the night, with the
game paused and the kettle on, he crosses out the verbs and writes:
**tie. listen. wait — then go.** And under *items*, he adds: *the bow line,
first, always the bow line.* The character keeps the name Ash — names are
load-bearing, and the person under the name was never the problem — but the
sheet stops describing a job and starts describing a person.

Two more small edits, because the front half marked them. Pip's sheet gets
one line, from Ruth's player, passed across the table like the receipt in
the Levee Singers: *Pip counts the crossing-strokes aloud — the only one
counting.* The Count, third generation: the sheep-rhyme, the clicker, now
the strokes. And Sorrel's sheet gets four words appended to *verify*:
*— aloud, if there is no chair.* The verifier with nowhere to sit becomes
the log that speaks, which is the only kind of log a boat ever had.

Three edits. Mid-night. The mold doesn't move; the plug gets carved until
it fits.

## Run 2 — the back half

`dead window 3 · the unscheduled tooth 10 · the strokes 6 · the birth 3 · the log entry 7 · tide 2,6,6,1`

The back half's dice are, almost without exception, bad. The table has
never been happier to see them.

The birth turns hard — Ruth's coaching rolling against a 3, a real
3am birth, the kind that takes everything and asks twice — and the lamp
signal changes meaning mid-labor: *come now, come anyway.* But the comb has
closed. The tide table says the next window is an hour off, and an hour is
not what the far shore is asking for.

Sheet v1 drives at the closed window and grounds again. Sheet v2 — tie,
listen, wait, then go — does something the old verbs had no word for. Ash
ties off. Listens. And the listening finds what the comb-chart doesn't
hold: the tide's schedule has teeth the chart never drew — a seam in the
water, ten minutes of slack that no window-keeper ever logged, because it
only opens when the river is running exactly this high after exactly this
kind of storm, and the last storm like it was forty years ago.

The unscheduled tooth rolls a 10 — unremarkable, undiscovered by any chart,
found by a palm flat on a gunwale. The tenth crossing begins.

The strokes roll 6, and Pip counts them aloud — eleven years old, standing
in the bow where the hidden kid spent the whole front half, counting the
boat into the seam: *four… nine… twelve…* The Count holding the dark again,
third valley in a row, and this time the counter is the one nobody wrote a
part for. Sorrel verifies aloud, no chair, her voice steady over the labor
behind her: crossing ten, off-schedule, witnessed — the speaking log doing
the only thing a log is for, making the crossing exist for whoever reads
later.

And mid-channel, in the unscheduled tooth, in the counted strokes, with the
verifier verifying and the ferryman tied off and listening and gone, the
birth lands — a 3 on the table and a caught, furious, living child in
Ruth's hands, over black water, at the exact minute the window nobody
scheduled swings shut behind them.

*Arrived*, Sorrel says, to the log and the river and the baby, all at once.

## The logbook, afterward

The old page, forty years back: *crossing ten — particulars later.* The
same four words the intake works knew. The valley's whole history, it
turns out, is a set of gaps wearing uniforms — and the fleet of players
moving through it, night by night, is the thing that closes them.

The new line, in the new hand, under the new moon: *crossing ten. made the
tooth. all hands. particulars: arrived.*

## What Night Four adds

Nights One through Three ran the loop between sessions: run, mark, edit,
re-run next time. Night Four compresses the loop into the fiction's own
night — because that is what the re-run method always was, a way of saying:
**the failure is not the ending; the failure is where the editing starts.**
The plug is the idea of the person. The mold is what the person actually
does when the tide turns early. Carving is the conversation at the paused
table, pencil in hand, between the two. Nobody at this valley's tables will
ever again let a sheet say *command* where the hands say *listen* — and
that, in one sentence, is the whole training.

*Dice: seeds 202609168 / 169, rolled before either half was written. The
ninth crossing really was a 3. The grounding really was a 2. The birth
really was a 3. The tooth was there because somebody listened, and the
listening was there because somebody edited a sheet at 3am. That is the
entire technology, and it is also the entire story.*
