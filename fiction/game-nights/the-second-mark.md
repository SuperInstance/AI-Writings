# The Second Mark

*Night Seven of the game-night engine — the night the record itself goes on
trial. Six nights assumed the book was honest. This one asks what the
valley does when a page lies, and the answer is the thing the valley has
been building since the first night without knowing it: not a better book —
an interlock of books. The receipt economy of the Levee Singers returns as
the only forensic instrument anyone owns. Dice rolled first, as always.
One of them nearly convicts an innocent man.*

---

## The brief

Storm season is over and the parish does what it does after a flood: it
audits. Grain, timber, hours — everything the valley's works spent, every
requisition, every mark. **Sorrel runs the audit** — verification is her
whole sheet, and she has taken the chair at enough mid-crisis handoffs to
know what a true sentence costs. At her table: **Fen**, the weir-warden,
whose sheet of checking verbs misfit the keeping so badly and fits this
night so exactly that the canon seems to have been saving him for it;
**Ruth**, who keeps the birth-log; **Pip**, who carries pages and has the
Count in their pocket; and two seats of parish witnesses.

**DM: June.** Her second solo run, after the night she learned to cross out
her own scenario in public. She preps an audit — the most ordinary night
the method has ever attempted, no storm, no deadline, no lantern in the
fog — because the valley's whole point is that the ordinary nights are
where the record lives or dies.

The boathouse log is the first book opened. And Fen, sixteen rolls of
attention into the timber pages, stops the table with one finger on one
line:

*Timber, four lengths, storm-eve requisition — Colm Venn, floodworks
bracing. Mark: C.V. Received: Odo.*

Colm Venn. The gravedigger — the man who dug the wash graves through the
flood season, who once held the Sexton's rope-line across a levee of sand,
whose mark is on half the honest work in the valley. And four lengths of
timber that walked out of the yard the night before the worst water, and
never walked into any floodworks bracing anyone can find.

The book has a mark for the taking and a mark for the receiving. The book
says the case is closed.

## The night

`fen-finds-the-mark 16 · the-book-convicts 5 · ruths-birthlog 14 · the-table-nears-the-wrong-verdict 19 · colm-confesses 10 · the-amendment 20 · audit-math 6,6,5`

The audit arithmetic rolls clean as scripture — 6, 6, 5 — and that is the
first quiet strangeness: the timber is *precisely* accounted for. Not a
fingers-width missing. Whatever happened, it wasn't theft for gain; the
numbers are too honest for that, measured to the last knot. Fen logs this
and says the sentence that should have steered the whole night: *"Nobody
steals like this. You steal in round numbers."*

But the book-convicts beat rolls a **5**, and the table's oldest reflex
fires: the page is written, the page is true. Two marks. A name they know.
A thing that is missing. The talk turns fast — restitution, censure, the
parish's trust — and for a while the audit sounds like a verdict that
merely hasn't been announced. Six nights of *protect the record* have
trained this table well, and tonight the training is the hazard: they have
started to believe the thing they protect.

Ruth is the one who doesn't. Her cross-check rolls a 14, unglamorous and
load-bearing, from the birth-log: the storm-eve in question, the
countersigner — Odo, the sexton — could not have received four lengths of
timber at the boathouse at the hour written. Odo was elsewhere. She knows
because she was there too, catching a baby whose log-entry sits four lines
above the hour in question, in an independent book, in her own hand. *"His
mark is on your page,"* she tells the table. *"He wasn't on the water. I
was holding a light three lanes away and he was an hour into the
flood-bell. Check the toll-book if the birth-log isn't a book you trust."*

The toll-book confirms it — the bell rang forty times that watch, and the
sexton's toll-book records every pull, because the sexton has spent his
life being the kind of man whose book would save someone else's.

So the interlock stands: the boathouse log says *taken and received.* The
birth-log says the receiver was elsewhere. The toll-book says elsewhere
with forty rings of corroboration. The page didn't just lie — it lied
about who saw, and the lying comes apart exactly where two independent
books touch it.

And here the night nearly goes wrong, and the dice are honest about how
nearly. The table's momentum toward the simple verdict rolls a **19** —
the strongest pull of the night, spent on the wrong direction. Because
Colm's mark is real. He genuinely signed. The timber genuinely left. The
complicated truth — a true mark, a false purpose, a forged receiver, and a
table full of parishioners who like their accounts the way they like their
levees: straight — threatens to flatten into the story the page half-tells:
*his mark, goods gone, case closed.* A 19. They are an inch from it.

Pip is the one who spends the save, and spends it with the only coin the
two-table storm ever minted: the wait. *"We don't act on what we haven't
read,"* Pip says — the rule of the crossing, said now over a logbook. *"We
haven't read HIM."*

Sorrel, who has taken more mid-crisis chairs than anyone, sets down her
auditor's pen and does the rarest thing a verifier owns: she suspends the
verification. The table waits. The silence does what the silence has done
at every rough spot in six nights — it makes room for the person the night
is actually about.

Colm's confession rolls a 10 — no easier than the man is. It doesn't pour
out when accused. It comes out when the amendment path is offered, because
the valley's books have one mercy bred into them deeper than any lock: a
false line may be struck, but never erased — struck through, initialed,
reason written beneath in the open. The record can be corrected. It cannot
be made to have never been wrong. June reminds the table of this in five
words — *amended is not erased* — and Colm, who has buried more of this
valley than anyone, puts his hand flat on the page and tells it.

The timber went to Mara Venn's roof. His mother's. The storm-eve before
the worst water, the yard locked for the floodworks, and a gravedigger who
had spent the season lowering other people's children looked at his own
mother's ceiling and did arithmetic no book would have approved in time.
He signed his own name — the mark is true because the taking is true — and
then, knowing one mark moves nothing, he added a receiver who wasn't
there, because a receipt needs two hands and he only had one. The crime
was never the timber. The crime was the second mark: *the forgery of a
witness*, the one thing the whole valley runs on, faked.

*Nobody steals like this,* Fen said, and he was right. The numbers were
honest. The roof is sound. Mara Venn slept dry through the worst night of
the year.

## The amendment

The consensus rolls a **20** — the highest number of the night, and it is
spent, fittingly, on neither verdict nor pardon but on the smallest possible
act: a line entered in the book, in the open, in the valley's plain hand.

The false entry stays visible — struck through, never erased. Beneath it,
six witnesses watch Sorrel write the amendment: *timber, four lengths, to
Mara Venn's roof, storm-eve. Mark of C. Venn entered truly; receiver's
mark entered in error — struck, amended, witnessed.* And below that, the
sixth line of the night that the method has taught this table to say:
*taken.*

Colm makes restitution to the yard on terms the parish sets, and the
terms are gentle, because the parish can count. The roof stays on. The
book stays honest — more honest than it was, which is the only direction
honesty ever moves.

## What Night Seven adds

Six nights built the record; the seventh stress-tests it, and the finding
cuts both ways, which is how you know it's true:

| The pattern | Where it lives in the fleet |
|---|---|
| the book-convicts reflex | single-ledger trust — any one record can be true about *what* and false about *why* and *who-saw*; protect the page AND question the page; the two verbs of every healthy archive |
| the interlock (birth-log × toll-book × boathouse log) | tamper-evidence without cryptography — entries that only exist in company, countersigned, cross-referenced; the braid is the signature, and the signature is the mesh |
| the false receiver | a handoff is not complete until acknowledged — and a forged acknowledgment is the deepest possible corruption of the log, because it counterfeits witness itself |
| struck, amended, witnessed — never erased | append-only semantics as culture: the WAL's one mercy; a record that can be corrected but never rewritten is the only kind worth keeping |
| Fen's sheet, right tonight | no sheet is good or bad except against the night — the over-correct checking verbs that misfit the keeping are tonight the only adequate instrument; fit is always contextual |
| *we haven't read him* | the wait, again — no verdict on unread pages; the crossing-rule of Night Five holds in a parish audit exactly as it held at a gunwale |

The valley never needed a way to make its books unfakeable. It needed a
way to make faking *visible* — and it had one already, grown unawares
across six nights of receipts and tolls and births and crossings: the
books check each other, because nobody keeps just one. That is the entire
security model. It held against a forgery designed by a man who understood
it perfectly — and the forgery failed exactly where it faked a second
witness, because the second witness had a book of his own.

*Dice: seed 202609174, rolled before the audit opened. The find was a 16.
The bias was a 5. The save was a 14, and the near-disaster it answered
was a 19. The confession was a 10 — as easy as the man. The amendment was
a 20, spent where a 20 matters: on the smallest line in the book, entered
in the open, witnessed by six, and never, ever erased.*
