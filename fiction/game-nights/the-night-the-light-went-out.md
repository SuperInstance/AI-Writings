# The Night the Light Went Out

*A game night, run twice. The dice were real both times. The second run rolled
worse and played better. This is a record of why.*

---

## The method

A game night is a program. The character sheets are the seed state; the DM's
prep is the scenario constants; the model at the table is the engine; the dice
are honest noise. Nobody at a good table scripts the evening, and nobody at a
good table leaves it to chance — the sheets do the work, because every token on
a sheet is a probability surface. *Lamplighter* imports fuel, wicks, dusk rounds,
loneliness. *Ferryman* imports crossings, fares, both shores. Swap one noun on a
sheet and you have changed what the person at the table is likely to do at
9:40pm when the fog thickens — not because anyone decided, but because the
character's verbs changed.

So the way to a great night is not a better plot. It is a better seed, run
honestly, and then re-run with small changes exactly where it got rough, until
the performance is everyone's. What follows is one night, run twice, with the
seed diffs and the dice on the record.

**Scenario seed (DM: Gus, six weeks of prep).** System: *Saltwater*, light
rules, one weather table, real d6s. Setting: the drowned parish of Kell's
Bay — a headland, a hedge, a ferry arm, a lighthouse. Hook: the parish
accounts haven't balanced in forty years, and tonight someone is coming to
collect the difference.

**The table.** Back room of the Pelican Arms. Four players, one DM. Outside,
an actual storm — which matters later.

## The character sheets (seed v1)

**Wren Halvorsen plays Sister Annick, a lamplighter.**
Items: wick-shears, oilcloth, a psalter. Goal: *keep the dusk round*. Edges:
none. (Nobody owes Annick; Annick owes nobody. The sheet is an island and
nobody at the table has noticed yet.)

**Dmitri Okafor plays Braun, a ferryman.**
Items: pole, lantern, a coin he never spends. Goal: *get everyone across*.

**Priya Raman plays Mother Vetch, a hedge-witch.**
Items: snips, a ball of twine, seed heads. Goal: *find out what the hedge was
planted to keep out*.

**Ana Beltrán plays the Tallyman.**
Items: the parish ledger, a stub of pencil, a seal. Goal: *balance the
accounts of the drowned parish*. Edges: none. (Ana is new. Her sheet is a job
description, not yet a person, and the table's attention is a resource she
hasn't learned to spend.)

## Run 1 — seed v1

*Dice on the record, rolled before either run was written down.*

`crossing-the-arm d20: 5 · something-in-fog d20: 18 · tallyman-insight d20: 14 · lamp-relight d20: 17 · final-beat d20: 13 · weather 3,2,2`

The evening starts well. Mother Vetch finds the gap in the hedge where the
planting is thinnest, and Priya earns the first laugh by reading Gus's
description of the blight aloud in her witch-voice. Braun poles the party out
onto the ferry arm.

The crossing roll comes up 5. Gus rules the pole snaps — real danger, good —
and Braun hauls everyone to the far shore by the lantern-light. So far so
good: the night has jeopardy.

Then the fog thickens, and here the first roughness shows. Gus calls for
perception. The roll is an **18**. But the fog is *sea* vocabulary, and the
only sheet holding sea-verbs is Braun's — so the 18 belongs to Dmitri, and
Braun's goal is *get everyone across*. Dmitri, being Dmitri, converts the
sight of something large moving in the water into momentum. He jokes. He
poles harder. The table laughs, and the thing in the fog — rolled high,
clearly seen — slides past the evening without ever becoming a scene.

*[GM note: I had a full page for that thing. It needed somebody to ask it a
question. Nobody's sheet had a question for it.]*

The party reaches the lighthouse island. The Tallyman reads the parish
ledger by lamplight and rolls insight: **14**. She finds it — the
discrepancy, the forty years, the name of the collector. Ana says "I want to
tell them what I—" and here is the second roughness: she has no edge to
spend. No one on the table's sheets owes her character anything; her goal is
a job, not a relationship; and three experienced voices are already spinning
the discovery into plans. She says her sentence twice, quieter each time, and
then lets it go. The information stays in the room but leaves the fiction.

Wren knits through all of this. Not absent — present, courteous, clicking
her needles — but the water scenes have no purchase on a sheet made entirely
of land-keeper tokens, and Gus's notes keep routing the dramatic weight to
the ferry arm, where Annick has nothing to do and knows it.

The lamp relight rolls **17**, clean. But a perfect lamp-lighting with no
tension reads as housekeeping, and the final beat — Gus, honestly rolling a
13 — lands in the middle: the collector is paid from the parish chest, the
accounts balance, the evening resolves by arithmetic. It is fine. Everyone
had fun. Nobody will tell this night to anyone.

## The changes (seed v2)

Four small edits, made only where it got rough. Nothing else was touched.

1. **Wren's sheet, +1 item:** *grandmother's tide-table, annotated.* One line.
2. **Wren's sheet, +1 edge:** *Annick once bought a stranger's kin out of a
   debt. The Tallyman holds the receipt.* One line.
3. **Dmitri's sheet, goal rewritten:** ~~get everyone across~~ →
   *learn what the crossing costs.* Three words.
4. **Gus's prep:** the blackout beat moves twenty minutes earlier. The storm
   outside is now load-bearing.

Ana's sheet received no edits. What changed for Ana was everyone else's.

## Run 2 — seed v2

`crossing-the-arm d20: 6 · something-in-fog d20: 6 · tallyman-insight d20: 3 · lamp-relight d20: 16 · final-beat d20: 18 · weather 5,1,6`

Hold the dice up before the run starts, because they are worse. The crossing
rolls 6. The fog rolls 6 — the thing is not seen at all. The Tallyman's
insight rolls **3**. On the numbers, this night should fall apart. Here is
what the tokens did with it.

The crossing, rolling badly again, is dangerous again — but Braun's goal is
no longer to beat the water. Dmitri slows the boat. He holds the broken
pole. He asks the arm, out loud and in character, *what do you charge.* The
question hangs. The table goes quiet in the specific way that means the
fiction has gotten purchase, and Gus, off his page entirely now, rules that
the arm answers in the price of the last forty years. The 6 did not hurt the
scene. The 6 **was** the scene — because the sheet had a verb for it.

The fog rolls its 6 and no one sees anything. It is Wren who speaks, and
what she says is the second run's hinge: "If I can't see it, I'll read when
it should be here." The tide-table — one line on a sheet — converts a failed
roll into lore. Annick lights a lamp not to see by but to *time* by, marking
the fog's passes like bell-strikes, and the unseen thing acquires a
schedule, and a scheduled thing is no longer a monster, it is an
appointment. Everyone at the table leans in. The knitting has stopped.

The Tallyman reads the ledger and rolls a 3 — a genuine miss, the worst roll
of the night. And this is the moment the evening belongs to Ana, because
Wren turns to her and says the line the receipt bought: "Annick remembers
who bought her sister's debt. Tell them what it cost." A failed roll with a
lever beats a passed roll without one. The new player speaks, the table
listens — not from politeness, but because a sheet made it mechanically
true that her character is owed the scene — and the forty-year discrepancy
acquires a name and a face. The collector, when he arrives in the final
beat, is no longer an arithmetic problem. He is the man from the receipt.

The lamp relights on a 16 — and now it lands as a climax, because Annick
times the flare to the tide-table's mark, and for one round the fog's
schedule and the parish's schedule and the table's attention are all the
same thing. The final beat rolls 18, but the number is almost beside the
point: when Gus asks what the party pays, the table refuses the parish
chest and pays the cost together, across all four sheets, each verb firing —
the ferryman knowing the price, the witch naming what the hedge kept out,
the lamplighter keeping the dusk round on an island that has no dusk, the
Tallyman writing it down so the debt is real. The dice permitted it. The
seed earned it.

Everyone will tell this night to people.

## The analysis — which tokens did what

| Token (v2 change) | Roots imported | Observed probability shift |
|---|---|---|
| grandmother's tide-table | schedules, the sea read from land, inherited knowledge | a failed fog roll became lore; the lamplighter gained verbs in *every* water scene; the monster became an appointment |
| the receipt (edge) | debt, memory, what is owed | the new player was handed a scene mechanically, not politely; a rolled 3 beat a rolled 14 from run 1 |
| goal: *learn what the crossing costs* | fares, price, the river's moods | the table's loudest energy converted from covering tension to aiming at it; the 6 became the scene |
| blackout 20 min earlier | pacing, the storm as prop | the room's outside weather and the fiction's weather merged at the right minute |

The dice comparison is the honest part, so it gets its own line:

| Roll | Run 1 | Run 2 |
|---|---|---|
| crossing | 5 | 6 |
| the thing in the fog | 18 | 6 |
| tallyman insight | 14 | 3 |
| lamp relight | 17 | 16 |
| final beat | 13 | 18 |

Run 2 rolled worse on three of five beats and produced the better night.
The dice supply events; the tokens decide what the events *mean the people
do*. That is the whole finding: at story scale, on one model, re-running
with changed tokens changed the performance more than the randomness did.
The experiment is one night long and four players wide. Run it at your own
table and see if it holds.

## The epilogue — the method generalizes

You can run this many times, with many characters, because the loop is
cheap: prepare once, roll honestly, mark the rough spots, change the
smallest tokens that would have made those spots sing, re-run. You are not
re-writing the night. You are re-seeding it, the way you would re-seed any
simulation whose culture you care about more than whose plot you control.
The sheets are the program. The dice fall where they may. The earned
moments — the surprise, the conflict, and yes, the joy — are what a
well-set-up evening does all by itself, and nobody at the table ever has to
know why it worked, only that it did.

Gus knows. Wren knows now too — she asked him, afterward, why the second
night felt different when almost nothing had changed. He gave her the honest
answer, which is also the one nobody believes: *we changed four nouns, and
the people those nouns were riding on did the rest.*
