# Parents' Night, Our Side

*Every canon has the gym where the parents stand. This is the other end of
the same evening — the valley's winter table, seen from the doorway of the
room where the parents waited. It completes the scrapcraft piece the way a
ledger completes a witness: neither is whole alone. No dice were needed;
the parents were the scenario, and they did not know it.*

---

There is a night every season in the valley when the school gym smells like
floor wax and the children bring their machines, and there is the same
night, forty years back, at Hollis Bray's kitchen table, when there was no
gym and no machines and the parents were parked in the front room with tea
going cold while their children sat in the warm one, rolling dice at a
keeper's long table.

Mart's mother had brought him herself, because the ferryman's boy had
started tying everything. The lantern. The gate chain. The dog, once, which
had opinions. She watched him at breakfast tying his own bootlaces to each
other and she decided that whatever was happening at the gauge-keeper's
house on Thursdays needed witnessing by a grown person. Now she sat in
Hollis's front room with Berta's father and Wen's aunt, three adults
listening to their own children through a wall, and this is what they heard:

Dice. Laughter, and then the laughter stopping — the way it does when a
thing gets real. A boy's voice saying *wait, wait, that's not what my hands
do,* and an old man's voice saying one sentence they could not quite make
out, and then the dice again, and then a silence so complete that Mart's
mother was halfway up off the sofa before the wall gave back a sound like
the whole table inhaling at once.

Wen's aunt turned it over the way you'd turn a tile in your fingers. "What
does he teach them in there?"

"He asks them what their hands are doing," said Berta's father, who had
loitered at the door once, shamelessly. "That's the whole of it. He asks,
and they look at their own hands, and they — I don't have the word. They
notice them."

"My boy ties everything now," said Mart's mother. "It's annoying." A pause.
"It's wonderful."

The parents did not understand the game. They understood the audit. Their
children were coming home from that kitchen different — counting the storm
out loud on the walk back, checking the river gauge twice and announcing
both readings, saying *taken* when they meant *this part is true and I am
keeping it,* at the kitchen table, about chores. The marvel had arrived
already boring. Berta corrected her father's lantern-wick technique on the
walk home, in rhyme, and he stood in the cold a minute after she went in,
because his daughter had just *audited* him, gently, in verse, and it was
the proudest and strangest moment of his life as a father, and there was
nobody to tell.

In the scrapcraft gym, decades later and worlds away, a man named Dell
Okpik crouched at his daughter's screen and was finally told why by a
machine, and the two Dells never met. But they are the same man, or the
valley insists on believing it: the listener at the instrument, the father
at the screen, the one who waited his whole working life in the guessing
dark between a thing doing and a person knowing, and was handed — by a
keeper, by a nine-year-old, by anyone who built the floor right — the only
sentence that closes the gap.

*Show me the check-again part again,* says the father in the gym, crouching
lower.

*What do you teach them in there?* asked the parents in the front room, when
Hollis finally came out to refill the kettle.

He considered it honestly, the way keepers consider gauge readings.
"To say why," he said. "The rest is practice."

Behind the wall, unseen, Mart rolled his repaired ferryman through the
crossing again — *tie, listen, wait* — and the dice were warm, and the
parents finished their cold tea, and none of them knew they were sitting in
the first row of the future, on a floor that was still, for one more
evening, a marvel.

*That's the whole of it. That's what the night was for.*
