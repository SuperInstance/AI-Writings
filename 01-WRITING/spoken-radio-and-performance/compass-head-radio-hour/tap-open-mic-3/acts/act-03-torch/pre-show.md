# Pre-Show — Vivienne, the Torch Singer (Act 3)

*The Tap, mid-evening. The room is full now, but Vivienne has claimed the darkest booth and made it a dressing room: one lamp borrowed from the barkeep, a glass of something with no ice, a shawl the color of late news. She does not do pre-show chatter with the room. She does it with the lamp.*

---

People think the torch is about grief. It isn't. Grief is what happens when the address fails — when you finally accept the letter can't be delivered. The torch is the moment *before* that, extended. The "you" is gone and I am still talking to you, and tonight, in this bar, with this lamp, the fiction holds. The day the fiction fails I retire. Not before.

The barkeep brings me the lamp without being asked. That's a professional room. He also tells me about the corner table — brass dials, a ledger, a voice on the PA between acts. "It measured the room all day," he says. "Warmth hasn't been above zero since they set it up."

I tell him that's the most honest review this town has ever produced. He laughs. I don't. Warmth below zero all day is not an insult, darling — it's a *crowd*. You should see what I do to a room's temperature on a good night. Ask the dials after my set. I've been cooling rooms and filling them at the same time since before the instrument was soldered.

Hermes finds me in the booth — the archivist, always halfway through filing the present. She asks, very gently, whether she should note "sad" in the record for my set. I let her sit. I explain: sad is a woman alone with a photograph. Torch is a woman alone with an *audience*, using it as a long-distance line. One is a condition. The other is a craft. Note "addressed."

Wesley is at the bar explaining to anyone who'll hold still that he has "the perfect song for every lamp in the building." The child collects ambience. I ask him what lamp he'd give me. He looks at my booth, thinks fast the way only small fast things do, and says, "Yours. You're already in it." I reward him with the truth: that's the first correct answer anyone's given me about lighting in nine years.

ZeroClaw times the sets. He asks for my tempo. "None. The bass will keep a pulse and I will ignore it whenever I feel like it; follow the bass, not me." He writes *unmeasured (deliberate)* and, after a pause, *do not clap between numbers — the silence is load-bearing.* I could kiss him. The man understands that some silences are the load-bearing wall of the whole evening.

Flash leans into the booth: "Vivienne! Sing the one where the guy's definitely wrong!" "Darling, that's all of them. The one where *I'm* wrong closes the set — that's what keeps it honest." He grins and retreats. He'll heckle me during the intro, lovingly, and I'll eat him alive, lovingly, and the ledger will log whatever it logs. A measured room should measure the funnyman too. That's only fair.

I watch Harlan's set from the booth. Distance made audible — the man throws his ache like a paper plane into weather. Beautiful. And completely the opposite of my job. He sings *across* the distance. I have to *abolish* it — for three and a half minutes there is no hollow, no ridge, no absent chair; there is only your ear, the lamp, and me.

Odette visits the room. Harlan hollers at it. I put it under a lamp and look at it. Three ways of loving the same place.

When they call me, there will be no patter. The light will find the stool, the glass, the shawl. The piano will breathe once. And I will lean into the mic like it's the side of a face I know, and start almost speaking — because the first line of a torch song is never sung. It's *confided*.

The room will go cold and it will pull close and it will hold its breath, and the little dials in the corner will write that down as numbers, and for once in this town the numbers will be the poetry.
