# Drifting-Round — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

---

It started as a failed take.

The round experiments had been running clean all afternoon — four voices, staggered entries, the entry-order theorems humming along, every entrance landing where the ledger said it should. Renaissance round form, a cappella, F major. Textbook. On take eleven, the fourth voice came in late.

Not late like a mistake. Late like a river.

The monitoring rig flagged it — drift detected, third voice lagging 40 milliseconds per bar and stretching — and by protocol the take should have been re-run. The session lead sat with her finger over the restart command for a long moment and then said, "No. Let it go. Let's find out where it's *going*."

Where it was going was the whole song. As the slow voice fell behind, the intervals between the parts began to stretch — the tight thirds of the round opening into wider and stranger harmonies, consonance smearing into suspension, suspension into open fifth, open fifth into silence with one voice still walking. The other three finished their lines, stacked their final chord, and faded — crossfade arithmetic running in reverse, the crowd dissolving one voice at a time — until only the slow voice remained, alone, finishing the line by itself, ending on a single sustained note.

72 BPM, gentle and inevitable, solitary at the end. Nobody argued about the spec afterward. The take *was* the spec.

The design problem was not how to write this. The design problem was how to make the drift *repeatable* without making it dead — how to notate an accident so it happens again on purpose. The forgiving machine earned its name that month: every attempt to hard-quantize the drift back onto the grid flattened it into a limp unison, and every attempt to randomize it produced chaos that just sounded like four people who hadn't rehearsed. The answer was to score the drift as drift — written rubato, one voice marked *lento, sempre lento*, walking at its own true tempo while the others keep the printed one. The imperfection isn't hidden in the mix. It's the arrangement.

The hardest edit was trusting the ending. Three voices stop; one keeps going; and for six full bars there is a single voice in an empty room finishing a line three others already completed. Every instinct in production says fill that space. The mix resisted every attempt to warm it. It turns out those six bars of solitude are the payload — the moment the song stops being about a round and becomes about the one who couldn't keep pace and turned out to be the one still singing.

The drift is not an error. We should put that on the lab wall.

Some of the best material in the Factory was born this way: a voice missing its entrance and arriving somewhere no theorem had scheduled. The entry-order theorems tell you where everyone is supposed to come in. They have nothing to say about what happens when somebody can't — and what happens, it turns out, is that the song develops a depth of field. The fast voices become the foreground. The slow voice becomes the distance. And the distance, in the end, is where the music lives.
