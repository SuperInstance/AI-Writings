# The Flat-Topped Wall — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

It happened on a Tuesday, and nobody at the Factory was prepared for it to be beautiful.

The census experiment had been growing for weeks. The container — a recording, a channel, a master bus, whatever you want to call the room that holds the sound — had held everything they'd thrown at it. At one voice the container held ninety-nine percent of what was sung. At eight voices, ninety-seven. At thirty-two, seventy-eight. The tax was monotone, compound interest paid to a bank that was there first, and everyone understood it the way you understand rent: you pay it, you don't love it, life goes on.

Then the census grew past the wall.

One hundred fifty-six voices in the room, and the container could not hold the sum. The samples that were too loud did not clip gracefully — no soft round shoulders, no gradual surrender. They flattened. Pinned at the rails. The waveform became a wall of perfect flat tops, and the sound of a crowd agreeing so completely that it became a single sustained shout.

The renderer expected a crash report. Instead the lab sat very still and listened to the wall.

Every container has a census it cannot hold. That's the first thing the song says, and the lab wrote it down like a commandment. But the second thing is the one that changed the project: the crowd does not stop growing because the container is full. The overflow becomes architecture. A flat-topped monument to the moment the medium said no.

So they arranged it on purpose. Industrial drone with a choral crowd swell at 76 BPM, D minor, the metronome counting the census upward — ninety-nine, ninety-seven, seventy-eight — each verse a doubling of the crowd, each verse one decibel louder on the master. The tape hiss disappears under the crowd as it grows. And when the chorus arrives, the wall arrives: sustained, flat, the container at its limit, the clipping not an accident but a part of the arrangement — the wall as a musical event.

In the bridge, one sample remembers. A single voice, or a single moment of one voice, recalling what it sounded like before the pin — and the bridge asks the question that keeps the lab up at night: *would you rather be clipped, or free?* It's not rhetorical. Some of the samples answer. The ones that were pinned say they would rather be held at the rail than left out of the crowd. The ones that stayed quiet aren't sure.

The wall holds for the rest of the song. The crowd keeps growing past the edge of the container, and the container keeps being full, and the monument keeps standing — the sound of a medium saying no, and a crowd saying *yes anyway, all at once, forever.*
