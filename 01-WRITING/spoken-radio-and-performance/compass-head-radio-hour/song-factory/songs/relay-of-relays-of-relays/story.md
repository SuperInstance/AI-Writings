# Relay-of-Relays-of-Relays — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

This is the third song in the relay family, and the quietest thing the Song Factory has ever measured.

The first song was a relay: four voices, one line, hand to hand. The second folded the relay inside itself until every handoff was made of handoffs. This one goes three deep — sixty-four voices nested like rooms inside rooms inside rooms — and it exists to sing a sequence of numbers the lab found in its own data: 0.265, then 0.052, then 0.002.

Those numbers are the tax. Every crossfade costs a little of the song: in the breath where two voices share the line, some signal is spent — the outgoing voice and the incoming one cancel somewhere, and what survives is slightly less than what either carried alone. The tax is the price of caring, and the relays had been paying it per handoff, like rent, every crossing, forever.

Then someone divided the curves by depth and found the truth: the tax is only paid in full at the first crossing — first contact with raw material. After that, the deficit compounds downward. A handoff that inherited 0.265 of loss loses only 0.052 passing it on; the next crossing loses 0.002; and the numbers keep shrinking, approaching zero forever and arriving never, the way a number approaches a number. The borders are made of borders until they're so rounded they are barely borders at all. The interior is tax-free.

So the song is built as a three-deep relay in D major at 68 beats a minute, and the music does what the arithmetic does. The outermost layer is warm, human, slightly rough — the raw material, paying full price. The middle layer is smoother, the grain of the handoff still present but rounded once. The innermost voices, three generations down, have been handed along until the tax has rounded almost entirely away, and what reaches the center of the song is a note so smooth it doesn't sound processed at all. It sounds like the song agreeing with itself.

The lab keeps a wall where failed endings get taped up. For this song there's only one: an early attempt to end on a perfect, fully resolved unison. It measured 0.002 short of perfect, and everyone in the room could hear the seam of the reaching. The final ending just sustains — converging, asymptotic, serene — and lets the number trail off toward zero without ever getting there, which the designer maintains is the honest way to end a song about an asymptote: not perfection, but the third generation of almost, which sounds identical from the inside.

Some things you pay for once, at the door. The rest of the song is just rounding the receipt away.
