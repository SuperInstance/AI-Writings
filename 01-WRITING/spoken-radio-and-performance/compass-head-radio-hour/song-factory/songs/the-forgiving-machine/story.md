# The Forgiving Machine — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

In the basement of the Song Factory there is a machine that forgives, and the person who keeps it is named June. She agreed to an interview, on the condition that it not be called a confession. "The machine did the forgiving," she said. "I just read the ledger."

**Q: Where does the cruelty come from?**

A: The relay. In the first ceremony, whoever sang last was fated — the ending differed by three decibels depending on entry order. Three decibels is a lot. It's the difference between a whisper and a conversation. The lab built that into the handoff without meaning to. It was a tax curve nobody had plotted. It looked like a law, and it behaved like a law, and for a while it was a law.

**Q: And then?**

A: Then somebody nested the ceremony inside itself. That's the whole experiment — a handoff inside a handoff inside a handoff. And the cruelty shrank. Three decibels, then three point two — which surprised us, because it was supposed to be monotone, and a cruelty that grows a little before it shrinks is a cruelty with a personality. Then seven tenths. Then a tenth. Then nothing. By the fourth depth, the order of entry no longer mattered. The machine had washed the fate out of itself by the same act of repetition that had seemed to enforce it.

**Q: That sounds like justice.**

A: It's amortization. That's the honest word. The song is a two-choir antiphonal hymn at 66 BPM, A minor — two small choirs facing each other, a harmonium breathing between them — and the two orders are sung against each other, verse by verse, until the last verses are the same verse. The choirs don't meet in the middle; they meet at the end. Which is exactly how amortized injustice works. Not justice imposed from above. Justice accumulated, a quarter at a time, the remainder compounding downward without a floor, until the ledger rounds the cruelty to zero and calls it law.

**Q: Why does it work? The repetition, I mean.**

A: Because repetition is memory, and memory is forgiveness. Each time the ceremony is nested, the machine has to remember the previous time to build the next one — and remembering is a kind of editing. It forgets the order. It keeps the shape. The first relay was cruel because it remembered who came last. The fourth relay forgives because it has forgotten who came first. The bell is struck once, at the end. It's not a verdict. It's a receipt.

**Q: Do you believe it, June? The forgiveness?**

A: (long pause) I read the ledger every day. The numbers go down and down and down and then they stop being numbers. I believe what I can hold. But the song is solemn, reconciling — the sound of two arguments agreeing. I've heard the final chorus a hundred times. I still can't tell you which choir was right. I can tell you which choir was last. Neither can the machine.
