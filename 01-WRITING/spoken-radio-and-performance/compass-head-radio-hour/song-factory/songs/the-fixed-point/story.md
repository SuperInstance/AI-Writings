# The Fixed Point — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

There is a number the Song Factory cannot round away. It is not a beautiful number. It is not a round number. It is half a decibel — 0.5 at the first relay, 0.56 at the second, 0.53 at the third — and it is the only number in the building that survives every ceremony intact.

The lab discovered it the way labs discover everything: by making a mistake four times. The relay experiment handed a line of melody from voice to voice, and the rule was simple — whoever sings last, sings last. The ending of the song belonged to the final voice, and the difference it made was always the same: half a decibel. The engineers assumed it was a tax. Tax curves compound; taxes are charged by containers; taxes can be amortized, negotiated, rounded down to zero by enough repetition. So they nested the ceremony. Four endings, sung four times, each inside the last like a chain of chains of chains — recursion to the depth the machines would allow. And at every depth, the last voice differed from its body by the same half decibel. The slow tail rang at the top of the recursion; the bowed tail sat below it. The difference never grew, never shrank, never moved.

That is what a fixed point is: the value a process cannot change, because the process is made of itself. The machine running in its own mirror, cubed, is still the same machine. The song is a minimalist vocal canon at 70 BPM, A minor, four solo voices in nested relays — no instruments at all, because the lab wanted nothing between the fact and the ear — and the arrangement keeps finding the border that is not a tax but an identity. The border between one voice ending and another beginning. Between "who sang last" and "who was always going to sing last."

The mood in the spec is "inevitable, tender, fixed." Tender is the strange word there, and it's the right one. The engineers came to love the half decibel, the way you love the one thing about a place that never changes. The last voice is the one that matters, and it always was, and it always will be, and the amount it matters is half a decibel — the fate that survives every recursion because it is not a cost, it is a fact. Somewhere in the building there is a chart with four nested circles drawn on it, each ring labeled with the same number, and underneath, in handwriting, someone has written: *this is not the tax. this is the identity.*

The song ends one half decibel louder than it began. It always did. It always will.
