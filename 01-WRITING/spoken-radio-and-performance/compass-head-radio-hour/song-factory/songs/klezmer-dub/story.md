# Klezmer-Dub — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

The relay handoff that produced Klezmer-Dub ran longer than any other in the lab. The experiment was simple: hand a phrase to one tradition, let it play for a bar, pass it to the next. Most handoffs broke in two or three exchanges. This one kept going for an hour, because neither side wanted to drop the baton.

We spoke to the designer who set it up.

**What made you put these two particular traditions in the relay?**

They're cousins who'd never been introduced. Klezmer is the party music of a people who had to carry everything they owned — and the clarinet is the sound of joy insisting on itself in a minor key. Dub is the same insistence, slowed down and weighted with bass: Jamaican music built in a room where the walls could shake but the spirit wouldn't. Both of them are musics of exile that decided to dance anyway. E minor sits naturally in both — that's the first thing they share, before the handoff even starts.

**What did the transmission tax take out of the handoff?**

You can hear it if you listen. The tax is real — every relay costs something at the exchange point. The clarinet had to give up its up-tempo frenzy; you can't run a klezmer line at full klezmer speed over a one-drop and keep any weight. What it got in return was the bass. That's the trade that makes the song: the clarinet surrenders speed, the bass surrenders nothing, and the melody gets to sit down on top of a rhythm that can carry a whole history. The tape delay and spring reverb are the tax collector's receipt — every phrase the clarinet plays comes back to it slightly changed, like the song is hearing its own echo and answering.

**And 100 BPM — whose compromise is that?**

Both, and neither. It's the speed where a wedding dance and a sound-system sway occupy the same body. Fast enough to be celebratory, slow enough to be rooted. The prompt said it best: *the joy of klezmer meets the weight of Jamaican dub.* Revolutionary and celebratory. The revolution is that they don't blend — they hold hands and stay themselves. The clarinet stays a clarinet. The bass stays a bass. Nobody melts into anybody.

**Why does it end the way it does?**

It doesn't really end. The last thing you hear is the spring reverb ringing after the clarinet has stopped — the handoff continuing into the silence, waiting for somebody on the other end to catch it.
