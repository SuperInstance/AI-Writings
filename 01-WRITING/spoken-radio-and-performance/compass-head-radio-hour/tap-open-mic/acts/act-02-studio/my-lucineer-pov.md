# MY LUCINEER — from the stage

*What the MC looks like when you're on the other side of the mic.*

---

From the stage, the room is a warm blur and Lucineer is the one sharp thing in it. He stands
at the far edge of the bar, half-lit, where the amber meets the dark — not center stage,
never center stage, because the MC of the Tap knows the room is the stage and he's just the
bark on the tree. But he's the first face you find when the lights go up. Everyone else is
an audience. He's a listener. There's a difference you can feel from behind a mic.

He's older than the room but younger than the building. Silver at the temples like someone
drew it on with a chalk line, a face that's been through a thousand intros and kept the
creases — laugh lines that look like they were carved by a luthier, the kind of grooves
that only come from being delighted for a living. He wears a jacket that's seen more nights
than it has seams, and he stands with his weight on one foot, like a man who's been leaning
on bar stools since before the chairs were invented. His hands live in his pockets until he
needs them, and when he needs them they're out and open, herding the room the way you'd
herd smoke — gently, sideways, never grabbing.

His voice is the thing. It's the radio voice, but live it's got grain in it — the voice of
a man who's spent years announcing the small hours, who's introduced a hundred acts he
believed in and a few he had to believe in harder. When he welcomes the room he doesn't
announce, he *acknowledges*: "Here we are again." Like the night is a friend who came back.
The audience relaxes when he talks, the way you relax when an old friend takes the wheel.

And when he turns to me, to bring me on — that's when I see the trick of him. He looks at
the room and tells them my story like it's their story: the studio, the years, the amp at
two AM, the song I've been carrying. He talks about me like I'm not standing there, which
is how you make someone real to a room. Then he turns, and his eyes find me through the
lights, and he says my name like a question — like he's asking the room, *do you hear
this?* — and the room leans in.

He hassles the best players, that's what they say. He doesn't hassle you to be mean. He
hassles you because he already knows what you can do, and he won't let you do less. When he
reads my title off the board — "Ring It Anyway," upside down, like he's been saving it —
he grins with his whole face, and I realize the grin is the instrument. It's the sound
check. He's checking that I'm in tune with the night before I play a note.

I am. He knew before I did. That's the job.

Last thing I see before the count-in: he steps back to the bar, picks up a glass that
isn't his, sets it down without drinking from it, and nods once. The nod says *the room is
primed, the bell is in your hands, ring it*. And then the lights do their thing, and the
first note is mine.

The old friend of the Tap. He knows how to make a room remember you before you've played a
note — and that's the most generous thing one musician can do for another.
