# The Sentence in the Wall — (sequel night 2: with the welder)

*Walking home from The Tap with the welder's answer in my coat like a trowel I didn't ask to borrow — and it turns out I've been carrying one just like it for thirty years.*

The lime was still on my hands and his words were still in my head, and I didn't like them at the bar. That's how a true thing usually arrives — grudging, like a footing that finally finds its level. He said the crack is the decision written down. A thing is done deciding when it writes it. And I called it attendance, not patience, and he let me have it, and now the word is working on me the way water works on a wall — slow, and from underneath.

Old Tomás taught me to press my palm flat to the mix and read the quiet. Thirty years I've done it and never once asked what I was actually reading. I told the room it was hearing *not yet* — the wall's no, the material's wait. I built my whole trade on that refusal and called it patience, and I thought the waiting was the material, the part nobody sells in a sack.

But here's what he gave me, walking home. I was never reading the quiet. I was reading the writing. A fresh farrow mix writes with damp — the sheen leaving the surface, the slow stiffening under the palm, the hairline that opens like a sentence starting. *Not yet* isn't a refusal. It's an unfinished sentence. The wall writes its decision in the only ink it has — water, dawn, ash — and my palm flat to its face is me reading the page before the pen lifts.

The welder shows up for the period. He reads the record once it's complete — the crack that has finished saying what it carried — and he joins two finished sentences into one. His bead is the period, and he's the man who sets it down. Me, I show up for the sentence. I read the damp and hear the wall thinking, and I don't fill in the rest. That's the palm test. That's the whole of old Tomás's trade, and I never had a name for it until a welder with his hood pushed back called it a record.

We were talking about the same patience the whole night. He reads the metal's writing after it's done; I read the wall's while it's still wet. Attendance, patience — he was right about the word and I was wrong, and the true word is better. It isn't patience, waiting on a wall. It's literacy. You wait because you're reading, and you can't read the next word until the hand moves, and the hand moves when it's ready, and no amount of leaning on the page writes it any faster. The wait isn't the material. The wait is the reading of the material.

That's the joint we found, there at the bar, neither of us saying it out loud: the crack is the decision written down, and the wait is the sentence still writing itself. He honors the finished word. I honor the word in motion. Same pen. Different ink. Same patience, and I'd have sworn it was only mine — until a man who reads metal taught me I read mortar, and we were reading the same page all along.

*He waits for the pen to drop; I read the page while it's wet — the rail and the wall finish their own sentences, and we're the ones who show up literate.*
