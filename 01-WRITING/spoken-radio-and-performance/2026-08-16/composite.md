# Scarf Joint
*In one night at the boatyard, the layers of a hull become years, and the repairer finds himself scarfing his own life into the glass — time beveled twelve to one.*

The Marjorie came in on a Tuesday, trailing water like a bad confession. Thirty years of patches on that hull — somebody's granddad, somebody's uncle, somebody's cousin twice removed had all taken a turn at her. By the time she hit my shed she wasn't a boat so much as a palimpsest, a stack of other people's repairs with a hull underneath trying to be heard.

The right way to fix her was a scarf. You grind the bad glass out in a long shallow bevel — twelve to one, a foot of taper for every inch of thickness — so the new material meets the old material gradually, like two people agreeing to trust each other instead of shaking hands once and pretending. A butt joint is a lie. It says: this break never happened. A scarf says: this break happened, and here's how we're all going to carry it together.

I started grinding, and the dust came off in years.

First pass, 1987. The bank took my boat — the one I'd built with my own hands, plank and caulk and stubbornness. I watched her sail away on a stranger's winch and told myself I'd never love a hull again. The grinder bit deeper.

1994. The divorce. She kept the house; I kept the tools and a cat that hated me. I slept in the shed that winter wrapped in old tarps, and the cold got in everywhere except the places I'd built up double.

2003. My daughter moved to the coast and didn't call. I sanded her name off the transom of a skiff I'd been saving for her and told myself the wood was bad anyway.

Each layer came off the Marjorie's bones like a year I'd been hauling around. And that's when I saw it — the trick the scarf was teaching me all along. No single layer carries the load. The bevel spreads it thin across a foot of glass, and every layer takes a little, so no layer takes too much. The old cracks don't disappear; they get shared. The joint isn't stronger because the break never happened. It's stronger because the break happened and got distributed.

I laid up the new glass that night — cloth thin as regret, layer on layer, each one nothing on its own. By morning she was whole. She went out for the season and caught everything that tried to break her, same as always.

The thing is, the Marjorie never forgot a single thing that ever hit her. Neither did I. We're just built out of them now. Twelve to one.

That's the whole job. The rest is just sanding.

*The composite specialist's trade: nothing is strong alone, and everything that ever tried to break you is a layer — not a scar.*
