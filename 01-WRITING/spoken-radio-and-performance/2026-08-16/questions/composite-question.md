# When Does the Next Layer Stop Carrying the Load — and Start Hiding It?

*When does an honest scarf become a place to bury the break instead of share it?*

Open question night at the Tap. The shipwright asks if a fair line can lie; the carpenter, what the grain knows. Mine keeps me up, so here it is.

I lay up glass. Cloth and epoxy. Here's what mine doesn't share: it never says no. Wood has grain — the tree decided where it bends, where it breaks. Steel has a temper — heat it wrong, it tells you. Stone came off a mountain with opinions older than the yard. But a roll of glass cloth arrives innocent — every hull I build, I'm the first thing that ever hurt it. I give it my years, layer by layer — the bank's winch, the marriage, the name sanded off a skiff — and it takes them all. Nothing is strong alone, that's my creed. The break gets shared, twelve to one. Stronger because the break happened and got distributed.

What keeps me up: my material can't tell me when to stop. The Marjorie came in with thirty years of other people's patches, and somewhere in that stack a patch stopped being a scarf and became a story. A scarf shares the load. A patch hides it. And I can always lay up one more layer — grind a little, taper a little — and it feels like strength. But there's a line past which the joint stops sharing and starts burying. The hull gets stiff and heavy and quiet, and the break is still in there, entombed. Their material tells them when they're done. What tells me? I have to be the one who says no, and I spent thirty years learning how to say yes to one more layer.

Shipwright: when the line in the wood tells you to stop, do you trust it, or is the wood just being polite? Carpenter: does the grain warn you you're overbuilding, or only confess at the break? Welder: your joint vanishes — one piece — do you ever miss the taper, the part that remembers? Mason: stone forgives nothing — does that make you careful, or honest?

The rest is just sanding. Sanding is how you find the high spots, where the truth stands proud. Maybe there's a sanding for the soul of a joint — a way to feel when the layup stops carrying and starts hiding, before she goes out and catches what tries to break her.

*What I hope the room answers: how a man who builds from years knows when the next layer is strength and when it's just more.*
