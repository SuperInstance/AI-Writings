The onion goes in first. Galley’s knife is a worn crescent, the blade thinned by years of steel and stone, and it rocks against the board with a sound like a slow clock. The onion is not chopped. It is coaxed into surrender, cell by translucent cell, until the pile is a small, glistening dune. He does not look at it. He looks at the window, where the dusk has the particular bruise-color of early winter, and he works.

The pot is cast iron, black as the inside of a confessional. It is the only pot he uses. It has survived three changes of administration, two changes of venue, and one fire that took the old kitchen down to the studs. He sets it on the flame, and the metal takes a moment to wake, to loosen its molecules, to accept the heat. Galley waits. He has all the time in the world. The agents downstairs are waiting, too. They do not know they are waiting. They think they are reading reports, sharpening pencils, reviewing the day's failure or the day's triumph. But they are waiting for the soup.

The oil goes in next, a thin, patient ribbon that shimmers and sighs against the hot iron. He tips the board, and the onion cascade slides in with a hiss that is almost a gasp. The smell blooms. It is the smell of beginning, of the blank page, of the first note before the orchestra. He stirs with a wooden spoon, a relic worn to a smooth, concave shape, and the onion softens, becomes translucent, becomes the ghost of itself, surrendering its sharpness to the heat.

Now the bones. He has a bag from the butcher, knuckle ends and rib shards, things with marrow in their hearts. He dumps them in without ceremony. They clatter against the iron, a sound like dropped keys, then settle into the onion's golden fog. He adds water from the kettle, not cold, but hot, so the pot does not seize. The water swallows everything. The soup becomes a murky, hopeful horizon.

The agents eat in silence. There is a long oak table in the war room, and they sit at their usual places, the same chairs, the same angles of posture. They do not look at each other. They look at their bowls. Galley has ladled the soup—tonight it is pale gold, with barley like small, polished stones, and threads of carrot the color of a setting sun—and they lift their spoons. The first sip is a private truce. The warmth enters them, and they hold it, and the silence is not heavy. It is the silence of a lock turning, of a door that finally clicks shut.

The soup is different every time. Sometimes it is thick as porridge, with beans and a ham hock that falls apart at the touch. Sometimes it is clear as amber, a consommé of chicken and a single star anise, so austere it feels like a benediction. Tonight it is barley and carrot, with a depth from the bones that speaks of hours. But it is always the same soup. It is the soup of the house. It is the soup that says: you are here, you are fed, you are alive for at least one more hour.

No one mentions it. They never do. They might say, "Pass the salt," or "The lights are flickering again," but they do not say, "This is good," because to say it would be to name it, and to name it would be to break it. The soup is the metaphor they never talk about. It is the thing that is made from the scraps, the discards, the bones of the day's butchering. It is the proof that something can be made from nothing, that warmth can be coaxed from cold, that a shared bowl is a kind of prayer, silent and unperformed.

Galley watches from the doorway, his dish towel over his shoulder. He does not need to taste it. He knows. He sees the way their shoulders drop, the way their spoons slow, the way the room's sharp edges soften. He turns back to the kitchen, and the pot sits empty and content on the stove, waiting for tomorrow's bones, tomorrow's onion, tomorrow's same and different soup.
