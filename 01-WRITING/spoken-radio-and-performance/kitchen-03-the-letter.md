The pen is a good pen. Ballpoint. Black. It rolls over the paper like it knows the way. It doesn't. It's a liar.

*Dear Tomorrow,*

I'm writing this because they told me to. The counselor with the soft voice and the harder eyes. She said it would help. She said a lot of things. She said, "Flash, you need to process." I wanted to tell her I process with my hands, not with a page. I process by fixing the water pump on the *Marigold* when it's coughing saltwater. I process by splicing a line in a gale. You don't process a storm by writing it down. You process it by surviving it. But she's got a certificate on her wall and I've got a leaky heart, so here we are.

*Today I turned forty-two.*

The number sits on the paper like a dead crab. I stare at it. Forty-two. My father died at forty-two. He was a good man who did a bad thing. He walked into the ocean with his pockets full of stones and a note in his boot. The note said he was sorry. I found it when they brought the boot back. I was seventeen. I burned the note in the stove. I didn't want to read sorry. I wanted to read *I'll be back.* But the ocean doesn't do drafts.

I stop writing. The pen hovers over the word *forty-two*. The galley lamp swings, slow, like it's keeping time with something. The boat rocks. A cup slides half an inch on the counter. I don't pick it up. The ink dries on the *two*. I think about my hands. They're good hands. They've hauled nets, held ropes, held a woman once—held her so tight she laughed and said *Flash, you're crushing the air out of me.* I let go. I always let go. That's the pattern. The pattern is the problem.

I look at the letter. There's a smear where my thumb rested. I don't wipe it. A smear is honest.

*I'm still here.*

The pen stops again. Three words. They're the truest thing I've written in years, and they terrify me. Because *still here* means I'm not *there*. It means I didn't walk into the water like he did. It means I'm on a boat, in a galley, at midnight, with a pen, and the boat is sound, and the sky is clear, and the moon is a thumbprint on glass. And I hate the moon. I hate how it shines on the water like it's offering a road. I hate how easy the road looks from here.

I write *I'm still here* and then I stop. I stop for a long time. The lamp hisses. The cup slides again. The pen is starting to feel heavy.

I think about the woman. Her name was Lena. She had a laugh like a foghorn—too loud, too bright, and you heard it coming from a mile away. She left three years ago. She didn't leave a note. She left a toothbrush in the cup and a half-empty jar of honey on the shelf. I kept the honey. It's still there. It's crystallized now. I eat it anyway. It's sweet and gritty, like regret.

I could write about her. I could write *I'm sorry, Lena.* I could write *I should have held on harder.* But I didn't, and I don't, and writing it won't make the honey smooth again.

The pen touches the paper. I write another line.

*The Marigold is holding. The hull is sound.*

That's not what the counselor meant. She meant feelings. She meant the soft, wet, human parts that I keep in a bucket at the bottom of my chest. But I can't write those down. They don't have names. They have weights.

I stop. The paper is half full. There's room for more. There's always room for more. That's the trap. You can fill a page with sorry and still have a full ocean of it left.

I look at the letter. *Dear Tomorrow.* It's a dumb address. Tomorrow doesn't read. Tomorrow just happens, indifferent as weather. But I write to it anyway. Because the counselor said so. Because my father didn't. Because the pen is a good pen, and the paper is patient, and if I stop writing, I'll have to start thinking.

I write one more line. Slow. The pen digs a little.

*I don't know if I'll be here to read this.*

The ink bleeds into the fiber. The lamp flickers. The boat creaks, a low sound, like a complaint.

I don't finish the letter.

I fold it once. Twice. I slide it into an envelope, but I don't seal it. I leave it on the galley table, under the honey jar, where it will sit until the glass sweats and the paper warps and the words blur into something softer, something I can pretend I never wrote.

The pen clicks. The lamp swings.

Tomorrow comes anyway. It always does.
