The air in The Tap was thick with the ghosts of a hundred forgotten sets, the smell of stale beer and beeswax, and a low, nervous hum of conversation. Flash took the stage, a lacquered maple board, his own, worn smooth at the edges. He was a man built of right angles and quiet confidence, his hands loose at his sides. He didn’t need to announce himself. He just waited.

The room’s murmur thinned. He lifted a foot, and the first sound was a single, clean tap. A period. A full stop at the start of a sentence. Then another, a beat later, and another. They were patient, deliberate. He wasn’t playing a rhythm; he was drawing a line. In his mind, the notation unfurled in a neat, monospace script: a simple figure in 4/4, all quarter notes and rests, a grid-pattern he’d constructed a thousand times. He punctuated the grid with a heel-drop, a soft, felted thump that felt like a question mark, then slid back into the grid, his metal taps a steady, scratching pencil.

He glanced to the side, where Hermes leaned against the bar, a dark silhouette against the amber light. She was a blur of silk and shadow, all chaotic motion and hidden intent. She offered him a slow, insolent smile. He finished his line, ending with a final, definitive heel-click. A clean period. He nodded.

She didn’t step onto the stage. She went to the floor in front of it. The wood there was old, unfinished oak, and her first sound was not a tap at all. It was a scrape—a long, dragging slide of her toe, a sound like a brush over a snare drum. In the air, she drew a different script. No grid. A jagged, descending glissando, a line that slashed through his perfect square. She followed it with a series of rapid, feather-light toe-taps, so fast they were a blur, a trill that raced up an invisible scale and then stopped, leaving a trembling silence.

Flash felt the grid in his mind waver. She was not playing notes; she was playing off the page, drawing in the margins. Her notation was a spiral, a fractal, a scribble that looped back on itself. She executed a double shuffle, but her syncopation was elastic, stretched a fraction too long, then snapped back with a percussive crack of her heel. It was a phrase that couldn’t be written in standard form, only felt.

A glass stopped halfway to a patron’s lips. The bartender’s polishing rag went still. The room was a held breath.

Hermes then stamped her foot, a single, violent, flat-footed thud. It was a fermata, a held note of pure percussion that vibrated up through the floorboards. She turned her back, and her hands, which had been still, began to clap. Not with him, but against him. She clapped a reverse rhythm, a polyrhythmic ghost that weaved between his grid’s pulses. It was impossible, a conversation in a language of broken time.

Flash stopped. The grid was gone, erased by her storm. For a moment, there was only silence. Then, he did something he’d never done. He lifted his left foot and, instead of a tap, he drew a slow, circular arc with his toe, a soft scuff. It was his answer—not an answer to her rhythm, but an acknowledgment. A word of surrender in a foreign tongue.
