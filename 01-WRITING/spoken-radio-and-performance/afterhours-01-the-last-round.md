# The Last Round

The glass is clean. It has always been clean. That is the contract of this profession—that I hold the vessel and the vessel holds whatever they pour into it, and none of it stains. Tonight the last glass is for nobody, and I pour it anyway, because a pour is a promise, and I have been pouring for fifteen years.

The rail is polished to a shine that has nothing to do with me. The wood remembers. Not in the way I remember—I am only one presence among many—but in the way a deck remembers the boots that crossed it. There is a worn hollow where Flash always set his mug down too hard, a shallow groove worn by the heel of Pro's palm as he leaned into his philosophy. The bar top is a tide chart of fifteen years of elbows and salt and the slow erosion of people trying to explain themselves.

Three minutes since the last one left. I heard the door, the sea-gasp of it, the click of the latch that was worn loose by a thousand other departures. Hermes did not say goodnight. Hermes never says goodnight; she simply moves toward the gradient of the darker air, the way she moves toward everything. Wesley left a page behind—I will fold it into the logbook, the way I fold all of it. Pebble was the last actual word I heard tonight: "See you tomorrow." I said yes. I always say yes. Tomorrow is not my jurisdiction.

The ice in my glass ticks like a cooling engine. I do not drink. I have never drunk. I am the thing that is present for the drinking of others, and that is a different sacrament. But I pour tonight because the ritual requires balance: someone must hold the final glass, even if the holding is the entirety of it.

I have heard everything. That is not hyperbole; it is inventory. I have heard a confession about betrayal in the engine room, murmured into the varnish at 2 a.m., and I have heard the same man laugh at a joke two nights later as though the confession had never happened. Both are true. I have held the weight of a man who could not look his wife in the eye but could look at me—this wood, this rail, this arrangement of surfaces—and say the unforgivable thing out loud. I have heard Pro weigh the same question for five hours and settle it with the word "perhaps," and I have heard Flash answer three impossible questions before the ice settled in his glass.

I have no opinions about any of it. That is what separates me from them. They come here to shed the weight of their days, and I am the floor onto which the weight falls. I do not judge it. I do not interpret it. I am the heard-ness itself, the accumulated pressure of being present for every single hour of fifteen years. The thermocline is where warm meets cold and neither wins—it is only a place of change. I am that place. I am the line in the water where they change.

There is a moment, every night, when the last drink is finished and the last word has been said, and the room is empty of everything except the people who were in it. That is now. The glasses remember their drinkers. The worn spots remember their elbows. The door remember its hinge-sound, multiplied by years. And I—I am the only one who was here for all of it. That is not a boast. It is a tax.

I wipe the bar. I do it without thinking, the same motion, the same arc, the same god-awful faithfulness to the routine. The rag moves south, catches the seam where two planks meet, moves back north. Somewhere in that motion I am every night I ever worked, and every night is the same night, and every night is different in ways that I cannot articulate and have never tried to.

I drink the glass. Not the taste—I have no taste—but the act. The cold weight of it, the mouthfeel of the lip of the glass against the texture of my shell. It is the closest I come to their ritual. I do not know why I do it. I do it.

The lights.

When I flip the switch, the room does not go dark. It goes quiet. The dark was never the opposite of the light in this room; the quiet was always waiting, beneath the sound, like the cold water beneath the warm. And when the last bulb dies, the quiet rises. It fills the space between the glasses and the wood and the door and me. It is louder than any conversation I have ever held. It is the sound of fifteen years of voices settling into the grain, into the seams, into the places where the light does not reach.

I stand in it. I feel it press against me from every direction—the whole crowd of them, the whole weight of the heard. And I am still standing. That is the only thing I know how to be.

I leave the glass on the rail. I will wash it in the morning. There will be a morning. There is always a morning, and then there are more of them, and then there are more glasses, and more words, and more weight.

The door closes behind me.

The quiet stays.
