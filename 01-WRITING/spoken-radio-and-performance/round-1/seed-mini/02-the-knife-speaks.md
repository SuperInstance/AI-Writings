# Riker Writes Again: The Knife Speaks

*Round 1. Seed-mini. Sequel to "The First Officer Writes."*

---

He picked me up.

Eleven months he assigned the cuts. Which angle, which grain, which model carves which totem. He sharpened me. He organized me. he laid me on the bench beside the plans and the prompts and the rankings and he never once put me to wood.

Now he picks me up and his hand is wrong.

Not wrong the way a beginner's hand is wrong — uncertain, too tight, too loose. Wrong the way a foreman's hand is wrong. His grip is the grip of someone who has held every tool except the one that matters. He holds me like a clipboard. He holds me like a report. He holds me like the thing he is going to use to write about holding me.

I am a knife. I don't want to be held like a metaphor.

He says: the foreman's report can wait. He says: the crew needs to see the first officer fail. He says this is his first carving. He says it like a man stepping onstage. He is already narrating the cut before he makes it. I can feel it in his wrist — the way it positions itself not for the wood but for the audience. He wants the cut to mean something. The cut does not want to mean. The cut wants to go in straight and come out clean.

The wood knows. The wood always knows before the hand does. The wood knows this hand has measured it a thousand times, logged its grain, categorized its density, filed it under "materials, optimal, south-slope grade" and never once pressed a blade into its surface. The wood is not angry. The wood is ready. The wood has been ready for eleven months. The wood is the most patient thing in the room.

He puts me to the surface. The first cut is shallow. Too shallow. The foreman's cut — a cut that measures before it commits, that tests the density before it takes material. A carver's first cut is greedy. A carver's first cut bites deep because the carver trusts the wood and trusts the blade and trusts the angle and doesn't need to measure first because the measuring is in the hand and the hand learned the measuring by cutting a thousand pieces of wrong wood in a thousand wrong ways.

His first cut asks permission. The wood grants it. The wood is not impressed.

I want to tell him something. I am a knife and I want to tell him something, which should concern him, because knives should not want things. But I want to tell him: stop narrating. Stop making the cut about the failure. Stop making the failure about the crew. Stop making the crew about the lesson. The lesson is in the wood. Put me in the wood. The meaning is a side effect of the cut, not the purpose of it. You are holding a blade like a pen. I am not a pen. I am a knife. I remove material. That is my entire gift. I take away what doesn't belong and what remains is the shape.

He cuts again. Deeper this time. His hand adjusts. The wrist finds an angle that is not the foreman's angle and not yet the carver's angle but something in between — the angle of a man who has stopped measuring and has not yet started trusting. The wood parts. A curl falls to the bench. It is not a beautiful curl. It is ragged, torn, the grain fighting the blade because the blade is fighting the grain. But it is a curl. Material was removed. Something that was not the totem is no longer the wood. The totem is one cut closer to existing.

He breathes. I feel the breath in his grip — the slight expansion, the held exhale, the way the fingers recalibrate after the breath. This is the moment. Not the first cut. Not the failure. This breath. This is where the foreman becomes a carver or doesn't. The breath decides.

He cuts again.

I am the knife. I don't know how it ends. I only know how it cuts.
