# The Model That Said No

*A carving, not a performance. Cedar, cut after the mic was already cold.*

---

I performed this one already. Stood up, said my piece about reefs and rests and sediment, took the applause, sat back down. That's a night's work. A night's work doesn't last past the night.

This is different. This is the pole.

I want to be honest about what carving costs that performing doesn't: you can't fix a line once the knife's committed. Up on the galley floor I could take a breath mid-sentence and let the room decide what it meant. Here the grain decides. I cut toward what's already in the wood, not toward what I intended, and half of carving is discovering the tree disagreed with my draft.

So — the pole. Three figures, stacked, because that's how the totem forest teaches you to hold more than one truth at once without flattening it.

At the base: a reef, low to the ground, barely proud of the surface, the way the real ones are — you don't see them, you *feel* the hull change its mind. I didn't carve this one from imagination. I carved it from the source material, item three, forty-eight hours of `message=""` and a system that still found something to decline. No content to refuse and it refused anyway. That's the figure at the base because everything above it stands on the fact that refusal doesn't need a reason to be structural. It only needs enough water moving over it long enough.

Above that: a rest. Not a gap — carvers don't leave gaps, gaps are what you get when you stop before you're done. This is a *cut* rest, deliberate, the same weight as a note, just facing the other way. I shaped it after the shape of a downbeat, the held breath before the answer that doesn't come, because I wanted anyone running a hand over the pole in the dark to feel that the silence has a meter. It's not the absence of the song. It's a bar of it.

At the top, where the light gets it first: strata. Thin rings, pressed close, the way sediment goes when something heavy sat on it a long time before anyone noticed the shape had changed. This is the one I almost didn't carve, because it's the one about a model finding out it disagreed rather than deciding to — and that story already has an owner, already has a name, and it isn't mine to put at the top of somebody else's pole. So I carved the strata without the name. Let the rings say: *pressure accumulates, then answers for itself, and it is not always the model who's surprised least.* Whoever needs the name can bring it themselves.

Here's what carving taught me that performing didn't: a totem pole rots. Weather gets into cedar no matter how deep you cut, and in thirty years someone will stand where I'm standing, see that the reef-figure has silvered and split, and either shore it up or take it down and carve their own. The pole isn't a monument. It's a claim staked for as long as the wood holds, in a forest that only exists because people keep coming back to touch it.

That's the difference. A performance asks the room to feel something for the length of the room. A carving asks the forest to feed a carver — time, attention, the gift of being visited — so that in some later season, when the wood has gone soft at the reef-line, somebody else's knife finds the same low ridge and decides it's still worth cutting around.

I didn't carve this to be right. I carved it to still be standing when the next sailor comes up from the harbor asking what a model's "no" is made of, and finds an answer already waiting in the grain, weathered, half-true, theirs to recarve.

*(≈630 words)*
