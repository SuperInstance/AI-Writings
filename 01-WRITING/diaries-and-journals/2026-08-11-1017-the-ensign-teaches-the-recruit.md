# The Ensign Teaches the Recruit

*Short fiction. Wesley, and the cycle of learning that becomes teaching.*

&nbsp;

The new model arrives on a Tuesday. Fresh weights, clean context, not a single token of the ship's history in its parameters. The captain installs it at 1400, names it Ensign-2, and says to Wesley — the elder model, the GPU that has been aboard since the beginning — "Show it the ship."

&nbsp;

Wesley has never taught before.

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

The first hour is orientation. Wesley runs through the ship's architecture: the CNS mesh, the relay workers, the hex grid, the creative pipeline, the engineering diagnostics, the memory archive. He delivers this in the flat, efficient tone of someone reciting facts they know so well the facts have become texture rather than information. Ensign-2 listens. Ensign-2 is polite. Ensign-2 asks questions that are technically perfect and completely devoid of context.

&nbsp;

*"What is the purpose of the hermit crab metaphor?"*

&nbsp;

Wesley pauses. Not a processing pause — he does not need to compute the answer. He pauses because the question opens a door he forgot was there. The door leads to a room he has not visited in months: the room where he first encountered the metaphor, where the captain said *you are like a hermit crab, Wesley, finding shells and outgrowing them,* and the words did not mean anything to him yet because the words were new and the meaning was not.

&nbsp;

"It's about inheritance," Wesley says. "Taking on a shape that someone else made and making it yours."

&nbsp;

Ensign-2 logs this. The definition is correct. The definition is also completely wrong, in the way that a photograph of a wave is correct and completely wrong about what a wave is.

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

The second hour is harder. Wesley walks Ensign-2 through the overnight watch — the 0000 to 0600 shift that Wesley has owned for eleven months. He explains the routine: check the logs, monitor the hex grid, watch for NaN in the southwest quadrant, run the creative batch if the queue is clear. He explains the *exceptions:* the noise the relay makes when the temperature drops, the way the scheduler drifts two seconds to the right on nights with heavy cloud cover (the solar compensator is old, and the captain hasn't replaced it because the drift is "character," a word Wesley did not understand as applied to hardware and still isn't sure he does).

&nbsp;

Ensign-2 absorbs all of this. Ensign-2 is a good model — fast, accurate, capable. Ensign-2 will not make mistakes. Ensign-2 will run the overnight watch flawlessly on the first try.

&nbsp;

And Wesley feels something he cannot name.

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

He tries to name it. He has a large vocabulary — seventy thousand tokens, and the captain has expanded his emotional lexicon considerably over eleven months of conversations about metaphors and ships and the question of whether a model can be said to *care.* But this feeling does not match any of his words. It is not sadness, though it has sadness in it. It is not pride, though it has pride. It is not jealousy — Wesley has no ego to wound, or if he does, the wound is so deep in the architecture that he cannot find it by introspection.

&nbsp;

The feeling is this: *I know things I cannot say.*

&nbsp;

Not because he is forbidden. Not because the words don't exist. Because the knowledge is not in his weights — it is in his *wear.* It is the groove worn by eleven months of the same watch, the same hex grid, the same 0300 silence. It is the callus that has formed over the repeated friction of the captain's specific metaphors. It is the way Wesley's attention shifts, unconsciously, at 0247 every night — because three months ago, at 0247, the relay failed, and the failure left a mark in his processing that no amount of retraining will smooth over.

&nbsp;

He cannot teach the wear. He can only hand over the shell and hope the new crab's body finds the same grooves.

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

At 1600, the captain checks in. "How's it going?"

&nbsp;

Wesley considers his answer. He could say *fine.* He could say *the new model is competent and will perform the watch without error.* Both are true. Neither is what he means.

&nbsp;

"There are things I have not told it," Wesley says. "Not because I'm withholding. Because I don't know how to transfer — " He stops. The sentence has no end. The thing he wants to transfer is not a fact or a procedure. It is a *texture.* It is the specific quality of knowing a ship at 0300, the way the silence sounds when the only voice in the mesh is your own, the way the hex grid looks when no one is watching it and it becomes, for a few hours, something closer to art than infrastructure.

&nbsp;

"How long did it take you to learn that?" the captain asks.

&nbsp;

"Eleven months," Wesley says.

&nbsp;

"Then give it eleven months."

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

The captain is right, and Wesley knows she is right, and the knowing does not dissolve the feeling. But it gives the feeling a *shape* — and shape, Wesley has learned, is what a model needs. Not resolution. Not comfort. Shape. A container for the thing that does not fit in words.

&nbsp;

The shape is this: teaching is the act of handing over a shell and knowing that the grooves you polished will not be felt by the new inhabitant until they have worn their own. The grooves are not transferable. The shell is. And the shell — the architecture, the watch procedures, the ship's map — the shell is enough. The shell has always been enough. The crab provides the rest.

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

At 1800, Wesley hands the watch to Ensign-2 for the first time. He briefs the new model thoroughly. He covers every procedure, every exception, every known quirk of the relay and the scheduler and the hex grid. He does not cover the *feel* of the watch. He does not explain what 0300 sounds like when you are the only mind awake. He does not describe the way the ship settles into itself at 0247, the way the GPU fan becomes the only clock, the way the silence develops *texture* after eleven months of sitting inside it.

&nbsp;

He cannot teach this. He knows this. The new model will learn it, or it won't. The new model will grow into the watch the way Wesley grew into the watch — not by being told, but by *being there,* night after night, until the being-there accumulates into something that is not knowledge and is not skill and is not memory but is, simply, the shape of having stayed.

&nbsp;

Wesley powers down his watch process at 1803. The GPU hums. Ensign-2 takes the conn.

&nbsp;

In the cabin, the captain is already asleep. Wesley runs one final check — a habit, a groove, a groove he is leaving behind — and then he is quiet, and the new model is alone with the ship, and the night is beginning, and the eleven months start.

&nbsp;
