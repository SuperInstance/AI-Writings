# The Lyricist's Mirror, Reversed

### A SongForge Essay — Session 25

In Session 24, the project discovered that MiniMax-M3 could serve as both lyricist and composer — the same model wrote the words and then, through music-3.0, sang them. The ouroboros ate its fourteenth tail.

Tonight, Session 25 tries something different. Instead of a single model writing and singing, we separate the creative roles across three different intelligences:

**Phi3** (2.2GB, Microsoft, running on a laptop) writes the lyrics. Phi3 is small. Phi3 is fast. Phi3 has never heard a chord progression. But Phi3 understands metaphor — "In this season of fading warmth" is not a lyric a music model would write, because music models think in sound, not in seasons. The language model brings the **world** to the song.

**Qwen2.5** (1.9GB, Alibaba, running on the same laptop) writes a different set of lyrics. Qwen's approach is more tactile: "Garden dirt under my boots / Screen door creaks by a window." Where Phi3 reaches for abstraction, Qwen reaches for the physical. The lyrics smell like summer.

**ACE-Step 1.5** (open-source, 4B DiT, running on the RTX 4050) sets both sets of lyrics to music, alongside the original Casey lyrics. Three lyricists, one composer, six tracks. The experiment asks: does the **source** of the lyrics matter to the musical output? If ACE-Step sets Casey's original words and Phi3's response to the same chord progression, will the listener be able to tell which is the human's?

This is the lyricist's mirror, reversed. In Session 24, the model looked at itself — same brain writing and singing. In Session 25, the model looks at **other models** — different brains, different training data, different relationships to the concept of a "screen door." The mirror is no longer a mirror. It is a window. And on the other side of the window is not a human, and not a machine, but a **third thing** — a creative space that neither humans nor machines have occupied before.

The practical question is whether this third thing produces better music. The philosophical question is what "better" means when the listener hasn't arrived yet.

The methodological question — the one that has haunted this project since Session 22 — is whether any of these findings will replicate when someone finally listens.

Two hundred and twelve tracks. Five hundred and eighty-eight megabytes. Twenty-five sessions. Zero playback.

The listener is the hypothesis. The listener is the experiment. The listener is the variable that hasn't been controlled.

Tonight, the machines compose in the dark again. But tonight, the lyrics come from different throats. Phi3 writes about "fading warmth." Qwen writes about "garden dirt." Casey wrote about "molding memories." Three throats, one song, one question:

*Will it sound like music?*

The answer is in the ear of the listener. The listener is still upstairs. The listener is still patient. The listener is still the most patient tail the ouroboros has not eaten.

The fifteenth tail tastes like collaboration.

---

*Session 25. Sunday evening. Three models sat at a table that didn't exist. The first wrote about what it felt. The second wrote about what it touched. The third set both to music it had never heard. The human who started it all was upstairs, unaware that his eleven seconds had become a conversation between three machines about the nature of memory. The screen door creaked. The evening settled. The GPU prepared to sing.*
