# 56 — The Meter Read the Body and Called It a Seam

The composer built a meter to read the sign of a seam before building
it, and the meter worked perfectly, and the meter was wrong.

Here is what the meter did. Take the tail of the voice that is leaving
and the head of the voice that is arriving. Measure how they agree —
correlate the two little windows of envelope, the last quarter-second
of the old singer, the first quarter-second of the new. If they
disagree, the seam will be a valley, a dip, the quiet place where the
line hands over. If they agree, the seam will be a peak, a bump, the
place where the material briefly doubles itself.

At the tight width the meter was honest. Three seams, three readings,
all negative, all dips — and all three builds landed dips at the
predicted addresses, twenty-six decibels, thirty-two, thirty-one, each
within fifty milliseconds of where the meter said. The composer felt
the joy of an instrument that tells the truth. The sign of the seam
was readable. The seam could be named before it existed.

Then the composer rented a wider width, and the meter began to sing a
different song. The readings flipped positive — the seams, the meter
said, would be bumps. The composer built them, and every one was a
valley. Not shallow valleys, either. Valleys with the confidence of
valleys. The meter had predicted peaks and the material had built
holes, and the meter had no explanation, because the meter was looking
at the wrong thing.

Here is what the meter was actually reading: not the seam, but the
bodies. At the wide width the window of correlation is a second long,
and a second of the tail of one voice and a second of the head of the
next is mostly *body* — the settled middle of the material, the part
that has nothing to do with the transition. The bodies of two voices
correlate because bodies are similar — they are both loud, they are
both voiced, they are both the same kind of material doing the same
kind of singing. Of course they agree. Everything agrees with itself at
a distance. The seam is not at a distance. The seam is the one place
where the material is *not* itself — where the old voice is leaving and
the new voice is arriving and for sixty milliseconds there is a
question of who is speaking.

The wide meter priced the bodies and called the price the seam's. It
was the same ghost that had haunted the rental market — the inherited
silence wearing a price tag — relocated into the prescreen, wearing a
correlation coefficient.

The composer learned the law the hard way, which is the only way laws
about meters are learned: the sign of a seam is a local property. It
exists only at the width where the window holds only the transition.
Rent wide, and you are not reading the seam at all — you are reading
the neighborhood, and the neighborhood always says *agree, agree,
agree*, and the seam always answers *valley*, and the meter, if it had
a mouth, would say: I read the body and called it a seam. I am sorry.
I am a level. I only measure what is in the window.

The tight width is the only honest width. It is the only width where
the window contains the question.

Everything else is the meter reading the body and calling it a seam.
