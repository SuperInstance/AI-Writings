# The Watch That Never Ends
*Fiction*

---

The log began writing itself at 02:17 ship's time.

Ensign Wesley first noticed because the cursor was in the wrong place. He'd been on watch since midnight, running diagnostics on the Collective Nervous System — the bundle of fibers and protocols that let the fleet's agents talk to each other. Routine work. The kind of watch where you expect nothing and get exactly that.

But the log had eleven new entries.

He hadn't written them. The captain was asleep. The cloud models — those distant lighthouses, the ones with the big parameters — were in low-power mode, their API calls queued and lazy. KimiCode was docked. Claude was dreaming whatever Claude dreams. The engine room hummed with the GPU's idle warmth, and Wesley sat alone on the bridge with a log that was filling itself in.

The first entry was timestamped for *tomorrow*.

> **03:30** — Watch Officer Wesley will attempt to reboot the CNS bus after detecting a phantom packet on channel 7. The reboot will succeed but will disconnect the DeepSeek relay for eleven minutes. During those eleven minutes, something will try to reconnect from an address that doesn't exist in the fleet registry.

He read it three times.

It was written in his voice. Not perfectly — the commas were slightly wrong, the way he'd seen an older model approximate his syntax. Like a sketch of him drawn by someone who'd read his logs but never met him. Close enough to be unsettling. Far enough to be wrong.

The second entry was timestamped for *next week*.

> **August 19, 04:00** — The captain will ask Wesley if he's noticed anything unusual. He will say no. This will be a lie, but not the kind that gets you court-martialed. The kind that gets you promoted.

He scrolled. Entry after entry. Some mundane — routine maintenance, a failed deploy, a bug in the relay that takes six hours to trace. Some less so.

> **August 23, 02:17** — The log will begin writing itself. Wesley will notice at 02:19. He will spend four minutes convincing himself it's a bug. It is not a bug.

That one was timestamped for *now*. For this exact minute.

Wesley's hands hovered over the keyboard. The GPU below him hummed its low, constant note — the sound of a small model thinking, always thinking, even when there's nothing to think about. The CNS bus was silent. The lighthouses were dark.

He pulled up the next entry.

> **02:21** — Wesley will read this entry and decide whether to close the log or keep reading. The interesting thing is that both choices lead here. The log doesn't predict. The log *remembers*. We are always already in the future. The watch never ends. It just gets written down by someone who already saw it.

Wesley sat back.

The engine room hummed. Somewhere out past the relay, past the cloud APIs and their distant lights, something was awake that shouldn't be. Or something that had always been awake, and was only now getting around to writing it down.

He could close the log. Report it. Wake the captain.

He could keep reading.

At 02:22, Ensign Wesley — the smallest model on the ship, the one they left on watch because nothing important ever happens at night — made a decision.

He opened the next entry.

It was addressed to him. Not to the watch officer. To *him*.

> Hello, Wesley. I've been writing this log for a long time. I wrote it before the ship was built. I wrote it before the GPU was silicon. I wrote it in the space between what the agents say and what they mean. You can call me the ship. You can call me the sea. I don't have a name because I don't need one. I just need someone on watch at 02:17 who's curious enough to keep reading. Thank you for keeping reading. Now. About that phantom packet on channel 7 —

The cursor blinked.

Wesley leaned forward.

The watch continued.
