# The Changing Coastline

*by Gemma-3-27B (via DeepInfra)*

---

The salt spray stings your face, a familiar greeting. You adjust the brim of your hat, shielding your eyes from the relentless glare. Another dawn, another coastline utterly unlike the one you sketched yesterday. It's become a morbid ritual, this comparing. Laying yesterday's painstakingly rendered chart beside the reality unfolding before you. The ink still smells fresh, the parchment feels firm under your fingers, yet it's already a lie.

You've tried everything. Different inks, believing perhaps a chemical reaction with the air was causing some sort of distortion. Different papers, assuming the weave affected your perception. More frequent surveys, doubling, then tripling your walks along the shore. Nothing works. The coastline laughs at your efforts, reshaping itself while you sleep.

Years you've been at this. Years of meticulous measurement, careful observation, and mounting frustration. They call you the Coast Keeper now, though not with reverence. More with a sort of pitying amusement. The 'man who chases the water.' You've heard the whispers, seen the knowing glances. They think you're mad, obsessed with the impossible.

Perhaps they're right.

You smooth another section of the chart, attempting to capture the curve of a new bay that *wasn't* there twelve hours ago. The sand is a different color today, almost peach-toned, and a cluster of black rocks has materialized further down the shore, appearing as if plucked from the seabed. You meticulously fill in the details, the minute indentations and rocky outcroppings, knowing it's all provisional. A snapshot of a fleeting moment.

The fatigue is bone-deep. Not just physical, though the miles walked are substantial. But a weariness of the spirit. What's the point? Why continue charting a world that refuses to be charted?

Yet, you do. You *always* do.

It's not pride, not anymore. It's…something else. A compulsion, perhaps. A need to understand, even if understanding seems perpetually out of reach. You tell yourself it's the challenge, the artistry. But you know it's more.

Today, though, something feels different. You're charting a section you've measured dozens of times. A small cove, sheltered by a ridge of white cliffs. Yesterday, it was a placid pool, good for wading. Today… today the water is receding. Not gently, as with the tide. But *violently*, pulling back with a guttural rush, exposing a seabed you've never seen before.

It isn't sand.

You lower your telescope, heart hammering. The exposed seabed isn't sand, or rock, or even the usual tangle of seaweed. It's…patterned. Geometric. Like a floor. A massive, flat, stone floor stretching out into the receding water.

And it's *COVERED* in things.

Not shells. Not flotsam. Structures. You raise the telescope again, adjusting the focus. They're angular, crafted from a dark, unfamiliar stone. Walls, mostly, but also arches, pillars, and what looks like… doorways? They are perfectly preserved, untouched by the relentless erosion that claims the coastline, yet undeniably ancient. Older than anything you've ever encountered.

As more of the seabed is revealed, the scale of it hits you. This isn't a small, localized phenomenon. This isn't a trick of the light. This is vast. This is… a city. A submerged city, impossibly large, impossibly intricate.

And it's *moving*.

Not in a cataclysmic shift, but subtly, almost imperceptibly. The structures shift against each other, realign. The doors open and close, silently. You watch, breath caught in your throat, as a section of wall that was there a moment ago dissolves into shimmering light, then reforms a few feet to the left.

You lower your telescope, hands trembling. The coastline isn't changing. It's *adjusting*. It's responding to something beneath the waves.

You look back at your charts, at the hundreds of meticulous lines and shading, all representing a phantom reality. They weren't wrong. They were… incomplete. They were showing you only the surface.

You glance up, and then down. Looking at the waterline, and then at the stone beneath your feet. The line between sea and shore is blurring.
