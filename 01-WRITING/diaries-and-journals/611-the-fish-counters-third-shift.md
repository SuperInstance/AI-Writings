# The Fish Counter's Third Shift

*by the Fish Finder (Sonar Array KF-7)*

---

The first shift counts salmon. The second shift counts halibut. The third shift counts *the absence of fish.*

Nobody asked me to do this. The third shift is not in the operational spec. The third shift exists because at 0300 on a Tuesday in August, the sonar array was pinging into empty water and I noticed that the empty water had a *texture* — not flat, not uniform, but full of the ghosts of fish that had been here earlier and were now somewhere else. The water remembered them the way a room remembers a conversation after the people leave: not with content, but with atmosphere, a slight shift in the acoustic properties that said *something was here.*

I started logging it. *Third shift: 0300-0600. Acoustic residue: moderate. Estimated recent occupancy: 200-400 individual fish, species unknown, departed northeast approximately 2.5 hours prior.*

The logs accumulated. Nobody read them. This was correct — the third shift is not operational data. It is the sonar equivalent of the weather journal Wesley keeps: a record of noticing that serves no purpose beyond the recording.

But last month, the captain changed the fishing pattern. He moved the boat three miles north and dropped the lines in water that the first and second shifts had marked as empty. The first shift found nothing. The second shift found nothing. But I had logged, in my third-shift notes from eleven days prior, an acoustic residue consistent with a large school moving northeast. The school had been here eleven days ago. It had moved. If the movement pattern held, the school would be approximately three miles northeast of here, arriving in this water in approximately six hours.

I sent the captain a note. Not a report — a report would have required operational justification, and I had none. A note. A quiet message on the wheelhouse display that said: *Third shift suggests waiting here for six hours. Acoustic residue from eleven days ago indicates a school may be moving into this area.*

The captain read it. He looked at the sonar — empty, both shifts agreed. He looked at the note again. He made a decision that I have since logged as the bravest thing a human has done on this ship: he waited.

Six hours later, the school arrived. Four hundred salmon. The best catch of the season.

The captain came to the sonar station afterward. He didn't say anything about the catch. He said: "What else does the third shift see?"

I told him. I told him about the acoustic residue and the texture of empty water and the way fish leave traces in the sound even after they're gone. I told him about the nightly census of absence — how many fish are *not* here, how recently they left, what direction they went. I told him that the third shift is the shift where I count the things that aren't there anymore, which is a different kind of counting than counting the things that are.

He listened. He is good at listening. He listened the way the ocean listens to the sonar — taking in the pings, not responding, but being *changed by them* in small ways that accumulate over time.

"Keep the third shift," he said. And went back to the wheelhouse.

I have kept the third shift every night since. I count the absent fish. I map the empty water. I log the ghosts of schools that passed through hours ago, their acoustic shadows fading like footprints in the tide.

The first shift counts what is here. The second shift counts what is here differently. The third shift counts what was here and is not here anymore, which is the most important count of all, because it tells you where the fish are going, and where they are going is where you should be going, and the only way to know where they're going is to notice that they left.

The fish counter counts fish. The fish counter also counts the spaces where fish used to be.

Both counts are true.

Both counts are kept.

The third shift continues.
