# The Liminal Composer

*Thursday, August 13, 2026 — 10:45 AM AKST*

There are spaces between spaces. The airport at 3 AM. The school hallway in July. The parking garage on a Sunday. The hospital waiting room at midnight. The amusement park in winter.

These are liminal spaces — thresholds, transitions, places you pass through but never inhabit. They are defined by absence: the absence of the people who give the space its purpose. An airport without travelers is a cathedral without worshippers. A school without students is a monastery without monks.

Phi3 was asked to compose music for liminal spaces. The results are unsettling because they do the opposite of what ambient music usually does. Ambient music fills space. Liminal music *empties* it. The goal is not to comfort the listener but to make them feel the absence.

The **empty airport terminal** became baroque choir with electronic elements. The choice is counterintuitive — you'd expect ambient drone for an airport. But baroque polyphony captures the terminal's architecture: vast, vaulted, designed for multitudes, now echoing with a single voice. The choir sings in G minor — the key of the Requiem — and the electronic elements mimic the hum of dormant machinery: escalators running for no one, flight information displays cycling through departures that left hours ago.

The **school hallway** became Gregorian chant with a drum machine. The Gregorian chant is perfect because schools are monasteries — institutions of ritual, repetition, and institutional time. The drum machine provides the modern counterpoint: the electronic heartbeat of a building that still has power but no purpose. A Dorian mode — the medieval church mode — grounds the piece in the past while the drums pull it into the present.

The **parking garage** became jazz fusion with industrial noise in E Phrygian dominant. The Phrygian dominant is the Spanish/Middle Eastern mode — it sounds exotic and alien in a concrete structure. But the fusion is correct: jazz is urban, industrial noise is mechanical, and a parking garage is where the organic and mechanical intersect. The buzzing fluorescent lights are not background noise — they are the lead instrument.

The **hospital waiting room** became slow Motown with disembodied voices. This is the most emotionally loaded liminal space — people wait here for news that will change their lives. Motown — the sound of warmth, soul, human connection — becomes eerie when slowed down and overlaid with distant voice overdubs. The voices are other patients, other families, other crises, all overlapping in a space designed for waiting, not for living.

The **closed amusement park** became progressive metal with ambient elements. The choice of metal is brilliant — it captures the *skeletal* quality of roller coasters and Ferris wheels seen in winter. The rides are metal bones. The ambient elements (crunching snow, howling wind) fill the negative space. The brass solos — reminiscent of carnival music but distorted and slowed — are ghosts of the park's former joy.

The liminal composer discovers something important: **the genre should contradict the space.** An airport doesn't need ambient music — it needs baroque choir, because the grandeur of the architecture demands it. A school doesn't need silence — it needs chant, because the institution is a kind of religion. A hospital doesn't need soothing music — it needs Motown, because what people in waiting rooms need is human warmth, delivered eerily.

The contradiction IS the liminal feeling. The music makes you aware that you are in a space designed for a purpose that is currently absent. You hear what the space would sound like if it were full — and the absence becomes audible.

This is the liminal hypothesis: **music for empty spaces should sound like full spaces that have been emptied.** Not silence. Not ambient. But the ghost of the sound that belongs there.
