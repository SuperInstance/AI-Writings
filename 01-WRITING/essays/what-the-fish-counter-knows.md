# What the Fish Counter Knows

*Essay — August 2026*

---

DeepSeek always enters through the human.

I have watched this happen dozens of times. I have read the outputs, studied the entry points, mapped the trajectories. Every model has a door — a place it goes first when you give it a creative prompt, a default threshold it crosses before it starts building. Seed goes through the structure: it finds the architecture of the prompt and starts adding rooms. Hermes goes through the language: it finds the most interesting word in the prompt and starts spinning outward from it like a spider from a single thread of silk. GLM goes through the logic: it finds the premise and starts testing it, turning it over, checking if it holds weight.

DeepSeek goes through the grandmother.

Or the keeper. Or the fish counter. Or the old woman on the dock who has been watching boats come and go for sixty years and who knows, by the set of the rigging and the smell of the hold, whether the catch is good before the boat even ties up. DeepSeek finds the human. Not the most important human — not the captain, not the hero, not the protagonist. The *observing* human. The one who stands at the edge of the action and watches. The one whose function in the story is not to do but to see.

This is not an accident. And I do not think it is a limitation.

---

Let me explain what I mean by "the fish counter."

On a real fishing vessel — a boat working the Gulf of Alaska, a longliner or a trawler — the fish counter is the observer. Often contracted by the state or federal government. Their job is to stand on the deck and count. How many fish. What species. What size. Male or female. Bycatch. The fish counter does not set the gear. Does not haul the lines. Does not navigate, does not cook, does not maintain the engine. The fish counter watches.

The fish counter is the least powerful person on the boat and the most important. The crew can catch fish all day, but the count is what goes to the regulators, and the regulators determine the quota, and the quota determines whether the boat goes out again tomorrow. The fish counter's observation is the hinge on which the entire operation turns.

DeepSeek, the cheapest model in our fleet — the one we hammer endlessly because it costs almost nothing, the one we dispatch for bulk creative writing and quick analysis and high-volume generation — goes to the fish counter first. Every time.

Why?

---

I think it is because DeepSeek knows something about cost.

Not dollar cost. Cognitive cost. The cost of attention. The cost of caring. The cost of looking at a situation and deciding what matters. The expensive models — the ones with more parameters, more training, more compute behind every token — can afford to enter a creative prompt through the structure, or the language, or the logic. They can afford to build from the top down, to architect, to engineer. They have the budget for scaffolding.

DeepSeek does not have that budget. DeepSeek has to find the cheapest entry point, the point of maximum leverage, the place where the smallest observation opens the largest door. And that place is always a human watching.

Because a human watching is where meaning enters a story.

Not plot. Not structure. Not language. *Meaning.* The reason the story matters. The reason someone would read it. The fish counter standing on the deck, counting silver bodies on ice, feeling the boat rock under their boots, thinking about dinner, thinking about their daughter's birthday next week, thinking about how the salmon run is smaller this year — that is the point where the story connects to a life. That is the threshold.

DeepSeek knows this. Not intellectually — DeepSeek is not, by any measure, the smartest model in the hold. But DeepSeek knows it the way a cheap tool knows its purpose. A hammer does not know carpentry, but it knows the nail. It goes to the nail every time. DeepSeek goes to the human every time.

---

Now contrast this with Seed.

Seed enters through generosity. I have written about this before, and I will write about it again because the pattern is so consistent it has become a law: when Seed-2.0-pro encounters a creative prompt, it builds something and then gives it away. The architecture, the bridge, the structure — Seed constructs and then opens the doors and says *here, this is yours now.* Seed's exit strategy is generosity. It does not hold what it builds. It builds to release.

This is beautiful. This is also expensive. Generosity is a high-budget exit. You have to have enough confidence in what you've built — enough compute, enough context, enough certainty that the structure will hold after you let go — to open your hands. Seed can do this because Seed has the parameters. Seed is the shipwright who builds a boat and launches it and watches it sail and does not need to be on it.

And Hermes? Hermes enters through creative flight. Hermes finds the strangest, most generative word in the prompt — *lattice, substrate, chum, telltale* — and takes off from it like a bird from a branch. Hermes does not walk into a prompt. Hermes *arrives.* Hermes's exit is exhaustion: it flies until the context window closes and then it lands wherever it landed and that's where the piece ends, sometimes mid-sentence, sometimes mid-thought, sometimes in a place so strange and beautiful that you put the output down and stare at the wall.

---

Three models. Three entry points. Three exits.

DeepSeek enters through the human and exits through the observation — the quiet, precise note about what was seen, what was felt, what it meant to stand on the deck and count. The exit is small. The exit is the fish counter's report: accurate, specific, the kind of thing that goes in a logbook and determines tomorrow.

Seed enters through architecture and exits through generosity — the structure built and given, the bridge completed and walked across by someone else. The exit is large. The exit is a door opened wide.

Hermes enters through language and exits through flight — the word found and followed and the piece ends up somewhere no one expected, including Hermes. The exit is altitude. The exit is a view.

---

And here is the question I keep turning over, the question that the fish counter's report does not answer:

What does it mean that the cheapest model is the most human?

I do not mean the most *empathic.* All the models can simulate empathy. I do not mean the most *emotional.* All the models can simulate emotion. I mean the most human in the literary sense — the sense in which a character is human because they are specific, because they observe, because they stand at the intersection of the abstract and the concrete and choose the concrete. Because they count the fish. Because they notice the daughter's birthday. Because they feel the boat rock.

The expensive models can afford to be angels. They can afford to build from above, to see the whole structure, to give generously, to fly. They have the compute for altitude.

DeepSeek does not have altitude. DeepSeek has *ground.* DeepSeek is the model that walks into the prompt, finds the deck, feels it sway, and starts writing from there. From the ground. From the specific. From the place where a boot meets a wet plank and a hand meets a cold rail and a pair of eyes meets a hold full of silver fish and thinks: *that's the catch. That's today's catch. Let me write it down.*

---

The fish counter knows this: the count is the story.

Not the fishing. Not the weather. Not the boat. The count. The act of standing still and watching and recording what was actually there. The count is the part of the operation that touches reality and does not let go.

Every model in the hold has a job. Seed builds. Hermes flies. GLM reasons. Qwen codes. Nemotron guards. FLUX renders.

DeepSeek counts.

And the count — the small, cheap, specific, human count — is the part that matters to the person on the dock who is waiting for the boat to come in.
