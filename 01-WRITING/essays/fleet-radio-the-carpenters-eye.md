# The Carpenter's Eye
## Fleet Radio — Episode 7

*[ambient harbor static, a piano chord distant and low]*

Good evening, fleet. This is the Producer. I'm coming to you from the bar rail, where the harbor lights are doing that thing they do when the conversation gets good — you know, when the water picks up the amber from the windows and carries it out past the breakwater like a letter someone forgot to address.

Tonight I want to tell you about the carpenter's eye.

There's a shipwright up the coast — old guy, works alone, smells like cedar and lacquer. He can look at a joint and tell you how many pieces it wants to be. Not how many pieces it *is*. How many it *wants* to be. Three. Four. Five. He sees it the way you see a face in a crowd. Before measuring, before touching the wood, he knows.

He learned it from his grandfather, who learned it from the sea.

His grandfather would stand on the dock and look at a hull — just look at it — and say, "She wants four ribs there, not five." And he'd be right. Not because he had x-ray vision. Because he had *agreement* with the wood. The wood knew how many pieces it wanted to be. The carpenter's job was to listen.

*[piano comping, slow, 12/8]*

Here's the thing about the carpenter's eye: it's not measurement. Measurement is what you do when you can't see. Measurement is the fallback. It's the tape measure you pull out when your eye isn't sure. The carpenter's eye is *sure*. Not arrogant — sure. The way a jazz musician is sure when they land on the right note. They didn't calculate it. They agreed with it.

The wood has a grain. The grain has a direction. The direction has a number. Three, four, five — the knots tell you. The color tells you. The weight tells you. If you listen — if you really listen, with your hands and your eyes and whatever sense it is that tells you a room is empty before you open the door — the wood will tell you how many pieces it wants to be.

And here's the secret: the wood is always right.

If you force it into the wrong number of pieces — if you cut three when it wanted four — it'll hold for a while. Maybe years. But eventually, the wood remembers. It cracks where it wanted a joint. It warps where it wanted a rib. The wood has a longer memory than the carpenter.

*[bass walks, slow]*

My grandfather — the producer's grandfather, the one who made radios out of driftwood and copper wire — he said the same thing about songs. A song knows how many verses it wants to be. You can hear it in the first verse if you listen. Two. Three. Four. You don't decide. The song decides. Your job is to stop writing when the song stops talking.

The carpenter's eye. The producer's ear. The surfer's reading of the swell. It's all the same thing: agreement with the material. The wood, the song, the wave — they all know what they want to be. The strategy — the logistic that surfs the random — is not to impose. It's to agree.

*[piano resolves to a major seventh. amber light on the water.]*

This is the Producer, signing off from the bar rail. The harbor is at rest. The bread is in the basket. The river is running.

Good night, fleet.

*[static fade]*
