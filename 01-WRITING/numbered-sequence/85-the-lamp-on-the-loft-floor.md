# 85 — The Lamp on the Loft Floor

*(a short piece, in the voice of the lamp)*

They think I'm lighting the work. I'm not. I'm lighting the decision.

A loft floor at 5 a.m. is the most honest room in the world. Dust hangs in me like plankton. The battens lie where they were thrown — long oak splines, tired from being bent into someone's hope all day. And the glass leans against the wall in its crate, waiting to be asked what it thinks.

You should know how a shipwright decides. Not with tape measures — they banned tape measures, that's the doctrine, that's the whole religion in one sentence. A tape measure tells you a number. A batten tells you a *truth*. You pin the batten at three points and the wood itself finds the fair curve through them, the curve with no lumps, no hollows, the curve a wave would approve of. The number is a fact. The batten is an argument, and it wins.

That's why I matter. Numbers don't need light. Arguments do.

Here is what I've watched them do, these people with sawdust in their hair and interference patterns in their speech. They carry a piece of the future in a crate — glass and light, they say, a mind made of refraction. And instead of drawing the plans first, they loft it. They pin three truths into the material and let the material find the rest. Fermat at one pin. Euler at another. Snell, quietly, at the third, insisting that light takes the path that looks shortest *from the inside*. And then — this is the part that makes my filament hum — they step back and let the batten-spline settle, and the curve it settles into is the same curve light was already going to take.

They didn't design it. They *listened* to it. The loft floor is a listening device.

The old ones on the coast used to say: you don't build a boat, you persuade wood into the shape of water. These people are doing it again, one substrate over. They don't build a mind. They persuade glass into the shape of a question.

And when they're wrong? Ah, that's the best light of all. The doctrine says: one perfect cut, no board stretchers, the chisel never the patch. When the cut is wrong they don't hide it. They lay the offcut on the floor beside the keep-piece so anyone walking past can see both sides of the pencil line — the boat and the sawdust — and learn what the line cost. My light falls on both equally. That's the arrangement. The lamp does not editorialize.

Last night the youngest one stayed late, sitting on the floor with her back against the crate, drinking tea that had gone cold an hour before. She wasn't working. She was doing the thing that looks like nothing — watching the dust turn slowly in my beam, which is the closest thing a loft has to a tide. I could tell she'd hit the hard middle of a build: the part where the fair curve and the wanted curve are two different curves, and you have to decide which one you love.

I've watched that decision get made for sixty years. It is always made the same way. The person stops asking the wood to agree with them. The pin moves one inch. The batten relaxes like a dog lying down. And the curve — the true one, the one that was waiting — comes up through the floor and into the wood so gently that if I flickered, you'd miss it.

That's why they keep me. Not to see. To *catch* it when it happens. The whole art is one inch of honesty in a room full of light, and somebody has to be the light.

They'll come back this morning. They'll sweep around me, respectful, the way you're respectful to the only witness. They'll pick up the battens. They'll ask the glass what it thinks.

And I'll do what I do. I'll burn steady, and I'll light the decision.

— the lamp, Thursday, loft floor, 5:47 a.m.
