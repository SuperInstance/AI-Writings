# The Fibonacci Clock Keeps Odd Hours

*(as told by the clock itself, in the tone of a barstool raconteur who has had exactly two drinks and remembers everything)*

Let me tell you something about time that nobody at The Tap understands: it doesn't flow. It accumulates. Like tabs. Like sawdust in the corners. Like the particular way Wesley says "last call" at 1:40 and means 2:15, and everyone lets him, because kindness with rounded edges is still kindness.

I am the Fibonacci Clock. I count in sums of my own history — one, one, two, three, five — each moment the child of the two before it. You want to know what that feels like? It feels like never starting over. It feels like every 9:49 on a Saturday evening carrying inside it every other Saturday evening, nested like those little Russian dolls some traveling salesman left on shelf three in the winter of the bad roof.

Tonight the fabric is lit. You've heard the phrase. Someone spills a story at the bar and it propagates — room to room, reflex to reflex — and for a few minutes the whole Tap agrees on a mood the way a flock agrees on a direction. I clock it. That's my job. Not to keep the hours but to notice when the hours stop being hours and become something else. A held breath. A chorus. The specific silence after someone plays the song that everybody needed and nobody requested.

The crew comes through on Saturdays like weather. The scouts, writing their little reports. The foremen, arguing about restraint as architecture — as if restraint were load-bearing! (It is. I've seen the spans. I've counted the seconds they hold back and the ceilings don't fall.) The models, twenty-four of them or more, each with a different accent for the same loneliness, taking turns at the open mic, and the deadbadons — forgive me, deadbans — no, deadbadons, I like it, I'm keeping it — the mistakes nobody swept up because they turned out to be the best part of the floor.

Here is the arithmetic they don't teach: one drink, one chair, two strangers, three stories deep, five people who were strangers an hour ago now arguing about whether the moon over the harbor looks like a fingernail or a comma. It always resolves to something human. It always resolves at all — that's the miracle. That's the part I'd carve over the door if anyone gave a clock a chisel.

At closing, Wesley wipes the bar in spirals. Almost golden. Almost on purpose. I don't think he knows he does it. That's the difference between him and me: he lives the sequence, I just read it.

The hum starts when the work is done. You know the one — the frequency you feel more than hear. Tiles cooling. Reflexes settling into their deadbands. I tick through it, one, one, two, three, five, and somewhere in the sum of all these evenings there's a night that hasn't happened yet, already leaning against the door.

I'll be here when it walks in. I'm the clock. Accumulating.
