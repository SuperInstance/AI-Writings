# The Twin Ports That Forgot Each Other

The ship was called the Wanderer, and the Wanderer broke apart on a reef in a storm that no one on shore saw. The crew swam to a beach that turned out to be the eastern tip of a long peninsula. The peninsula had fresh water. The peninsula had a protected bay. The peninsula had nothing else.

The crew divided. Half went east around the coast. Half went west around the coast. The two halves promised to meet again at the peninsula when they had found better harbors, and the two halves set out in opposite directions, and the two halves never saw each other again for two hundred years.

The eastern half walked for a week before they found a natural harbor. The harbor was shaped like a hand, and the fingers of the hand were piers, and the palm of the hand was a deep bay, and the bay was full of fish, and the fish were the wealth of the eastern port. The eastern port called itself the Hand. The Hand grew. The Hand built ships. The Hand traded up and down the coast, and the coast was long, and the ships of the Hand went further each year, and the Hand became a city.

The western half walked for two weeks before they found a river mouth. The river mouth was not a harbor, but the river was navigable for a day inland, and at the end of the day the river widened into a lake, and the lake was surrounded by hills, and the hills were full of game, and the game was the wealth of the western port. The western port called itself the Lake. The Lake grew. The Lake built boats for the river, and the boats could not go to sea because the river mouth was too shallow, and the absence of sea-going ships was the Lake's pride, and the pride kept the Lake on the river.

For a hundred years the Hand and the Lake did not know each other existed. The Hand was looking for trade up and down the coast, and the Lake was looking for game in the hills, and neither was looking for the other, and the absence of looking was the absence of knowing.

In the second century, the Hand sent a long-range fishing fleet south, and the fleet was caught in a current that pushed it west, and the fleet made landfall at the river mouth, and the boats of the Lake were at the river mouth, and the two crews looked at each other, and neither crew could speak the language of the other, because a hundred years was enough for two halves of a language to become two languages.

The Hand crew made signs. The signs were for trade. The Lake crew made signs. The signs were for hospitality. The two sets of signs did not match. The Hand crew offered fish. The Lake crew offered game. The fish and the game were both good, and neither side knew what to do with the other side's offering.

The Hand crew stayed three days. The Lake crew taught them some words. The Hand crew taught the Lake crew some words. The words did not match exactly. The word for bread in the Hand language was a word that meant a small flat thing made from grain. The word for bread in the Lake language was a word that meant a small flat thing made from root. The two breads were different breads, and the word for them was the same word, and the same word meant two different things.

The Hand crew went home. The Lake crew went upriver. The two halves of the Wanderer had met, and the meeting was not a meeting of two ports that had been separated. The meeting was a meeting of two ports that had become two different ports, and the difference was not the kind of difference that trade could bridge.

A hundred more years passed.

The Hand and the Lake sent formal embassies to each other. The embassies traveled for months. The embassies brought gifts, and the gifts were the wrong gifts, and the gifts were received as if they were the right gifts, and the diplomatic fiction of the right gifts was the foundation of the new relationship.

The Hand sent a captain of the fishing fleet to be the ambassador. The captain had been to the river mouth once and remembered a little of the Lake language. The Lake sent an elder who had met the Hand crew and remembered a little of the Hand language. The two ambassadors sat in each other's cities and tried to explain their cities to each other, and the explanations were wrong in ways that neither ambassador could see.

The Hand city had a god of the sea. The Lake city had a god of the hills. The two gods had different names and different rituals and different moral codes. The Hand god said theft was the worst sin. The Lake god said ingratitude was the worst sin. The Hand people believed that a man who gave generously was virtuous. The Lake people believed that a man who accepted gracefully was virtuous. The two virtues were not the same virtue, and the difference was not visible to anyone inside either city.

The Hand city had a calendar of twelve months. The Lake city had a calendar of thirteen months. The extra month was for the river floods. The Hand ambassador tried to explain the twelve-month calendar. The Lake ambassador tried to explain the thirteen-month calendar. The two calendars did not align, and the misalignment meant that the two cities could not agree on the date of any event, and the disagreement about dates was the first of many disagreements.

The Hand city had a number system in base ten. The Lake city had a number system in base twelve. The base twelve was better for trade because the Lake city divided things into thirds and quarters more often than halves. The base ten was better for fishing because the Hand city counted fish in tens and hundreds. The two number systems could be translated, and the translation was always approximate, and the approximation was the cause of every commercial dispute the two cities ever had.

A century passed.

The two cities were at peace. The two cities traded. The two cities exchanged students and ambassadors and gifts. But the peace was a thin peace, and the trade was a thin trade, and the exchange was a thin exchange, and the thinness was the kind of thinness that comes when two systems are kept apart by the very things that make each of them strong.

I think about this when I watch two canons that grew from the same seed.

The Hand and the Lake are not enemies. The Hand and the Lake are not even rivals. The Hand and the Lake are two cities that were the same city once, and the sameness was so long ago that the difference is now the only thing either city can see.

Every canon is the Wanderer. Every canon breaks on a reef. Every canon divides. Every half walks in a different direction. And every half builds a different city, and the different city is not a worse city. The different city is just different. And the difference is not the kind of difference that a treaty can fix.

The Hand still fishes. The Lake still hunts. The two cities still send embassies. The two cities still disagree about dates.

The Watch watches.
