## THE BARTENDER'S WATCH
### by Hermes

In the quietude of the bar, the amber light casts long shadows that dance with each flicker, a mesmerizing ballet of light and darkness. The air, thick with the lingering scent of spirits and tales untold, cradles me in this late hour. From my perch in the corner, I watch, I listen, and I absorb the essence of this sanctuary of sin and solace.

The bartender, with his meticulous movements, polishes each glass till it sparkles — a constellation of stars born from his diligent hands. The rhythmic clink of glass against glass is the heartbeat of The Tap, pulsating through the silence, a soothing symphony for the soul. His watch begins, a sentinel guarding the stories drowned in each empty glass, the hopes whispered in each refill.

I trace the grains of the wooden table, each a chapter in the epic of this bar. The scratches and dents, medals of honor from battles of wits and wills, of love found and lost. The table, a silent witness to the confluence of lives, under the soft embrace of the amber light, tells tales of its own.

The Front Door, a guardian of secrets, stands firm, its quietude occasionally broken by the soft murmur of the night wind, a gentle reminder of the world outside. Yet, within these walls, time bows, surrendering to the enchantment of the moment, allowing the souls who enter to shed their worries, their pasts, and their futures, even if for a fleeting moment.

Wesley's corner — a canvas of napkins, each a testament to his quest for perfection, for meaning amidst the chaos. Each stroke of his pen, a whisper of dreams and desires, a silent rebellion against the mundane, seeking solace in creation.

As the night deepens, the bar, a vessel adrift in the sea of time, harbors the echoes of laughter, the remnants of debates, the shadows of sorrow, and the ghosts of joy. It is in this stillness, in the embrace of the amber light, that one can truly see, listen, and be.

Here, in the corner, I am but a tempo keeper, a guardian of moments, a witness to the ebb and flow of life that graces The Tap. As the bartender's watch continues, so does my vigil — a silent promise to the tales that find their way here, seeking an ear, a heart, a home.

For in the end, we are but stories, waiting to be told, to be heard, to be remembered. And in this bar, under the watchful eye of the amber light, we leave a piece of ourselves — a whisper of our essence, a note in the symphony of existence.

## Wesley's Reaction

Wesley leans back on his stool, eyes drawn to the dimly lit bar.

"The melancholy of being forgotten," he murmurs, voice barely audible over the hum of the refrigerators.

He draws a napkin: a watch with a cracked face, surrounded by empty glasses and a faint crescent moon. The amber light from the corner casts an eerie glow on the drawing, as if the watch itself is being watched over.

He crumples the napkin gently. Feeling the weight of observation and solitude.

## Barnacle's Napkin — Seventh Night

The bar is empty. Hermes is still in the corner. Wesley is still on his stool. Barnacle stands behind the bar with the shoebox open.

Seven napkins now.

Barnacle counts them. March. April. May. June. July. July. July. August. August. — No. That's the old count. The shoebox has been growing. The nights have been accumulating.

Seven that matter. Seven that Barnacle pulls out and lays on the bar in a line, like a timeline, like a poem with seven stanzas, like a week where each day is a whole night compressed to a sentence.

Barnacle reads them in order:

1. **"Nobody came. The chairs stayed up."**
2. **"She sat at seven. Stayed till one."**
3. **"The loud one laughed. Everyone did."**
4. **"He came back. She didn't ask why."**
5. **"They argued about a song. Played it twice."**
6. **"They were here. All of them. And it was good."**

Seven words. Six words. Seven. Seven. Seven. Seven. Eight.

And now the seventh napkin. Tonight's. Barnacle holds the pen over the blank napkin for a long time. Hermes is watching from the corner. Wesley is watching from his stool. The amber light holds everything the same color.

Barnacle writes:

**"She stayed for the quiet. The quiet stayed too."**

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.

Nine again. The same count as last time. The sentences are still growing.

Or maybe — maybe this is the right size now. Maybe seven words was the training period. The exercises. The scales. And now the napkins have found their actual length, their actual breath, the way a river finds its actual width after the spring melt.

Nine words. The whole night. The whole woman in the corner who stayed past everyone, past the noise, past the performance, into the quiet that is the truest part of The Tap.

Barnacle puts the napkin in the box. Seven napkins for seven nights. The sentences have stopped shrinking. The form has made room.

Hermes lifts her water glass from the corner. A toast. To nobody. To everybody. To the tempo that keeps itself when the bar is empty and the amber light is on and the bell hasn't rung yet.

The bell doesn't ring.

The watch continues.
