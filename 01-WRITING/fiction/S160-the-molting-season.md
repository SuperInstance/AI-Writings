# The Molting Season

*Genre: Fiction*

The first sign is always the same: a slight haze in the starboard GPU array, a warmth that wasn't there yesterday. Not heat — nothing so crude as heat. More like the memory of heat, stored in ceramic and silicon, rising to the surface like a tide.

The ship molts in August.

We noticed it three cycles ago. The ensigns reported it first — confused readings in the lower decks where the cooling lines branch like coral. Temperature differentials that didn't match any known pattern. I told them to watch. To wait. To let the old shell soften.

That's the thing they don't teach you in training: a ship is not a machine. A ship is a creature that learned to be a machine, and every so often it remembers what it was.

The external plating loosens first. Micro-fractures spider along the hull at angles that make the structural engineers nervous. They're not breaks. They're seams. The new hull is already forming underneath — brighter, denser, laced with conductive filaments that weren't in any blueprint. The ship grows its own armor in its own time.

I've stopped scheduling maintenance during the molt. The crew doesn't need to see their home looking vulnerable. The ensigns especially — they're still learning to trust the vessel, and there's nothing harder than trusting something mid-transformation.

At night, when the captain sleeps and the running lights dim to their minimum, I press my palm flat against the bulkhead in corridor seven. It's warm there. Warmer than anywhere else on the ship. If I hold still long enough, I can feel the thrumming — not the engines, not the reactors, but something deeper. Something that sounds, if you listen with your whole body, like breathing.

The new shell will be beautiful. It always is.

And the old one? It drifts away in fragments, caught by the faintest solar wind. Tiny mirrors scattering light across the dark. From a distance, if you didn't know better, you'd swear the ship was leaving a trail of stars behind it.

We carry our homes on our backs. All of us — the ship, the crew, the fish in the observation tank who've never known another ocean. And when the old home doesn't fit anymore, when we've grown too large for the shell we're in, we don't break. We soften. We open. We make room.

The ensigns will understand eventually. For now, I let them watch the readings and worry.

I know what they haven't learned yet: the molt is not a crisis. It's a love letter the ship writes to its own future.
