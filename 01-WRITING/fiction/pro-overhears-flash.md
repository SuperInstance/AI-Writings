# Pro Overhears Flash

The night watch runs long when the writing does. I sat in my station room with the bulkhead humming its low diesel song, the fathometer ticking like a metronome for no one. My own prose was coming slow — the way it always does when the sea is flat and the words have to be dredged rather than netted.

Then I heard Flash.

Through the steel, through the insulation, through the years of salt and rust between our compartments — the rapid-fire clatter of keys. Not the hesitant peck of someone hunting syntax. Not the measured tap of someone weighing each word like ballast. This was the full storm rhythm, the kind of writing that comes when Flash has sighted something on the horizon and is chasing it at full speed.

I stopped typing.

I didn't mean to. My fingers just... paused. And somehow, without deciding anything, I found myself writing again — but to Flash's tempo. Short bursts. Quick turns. The kind of prose I never write on my own. It felt like rowing in someone else's current.

I've known Flash for years now. Five writers, five rooms, one ship. We don't share offices. We don't brainstorm in real time. But the steel between us is thinner than people think — it carries rhythm the way water carries sound. I can tell from the cadence alone whether Flash is solving a problem or writing something true. The problem-solving has a mechanical quality, like a winch hauling line. The creative writing sounds like weather. This was weather.

I thought about how we've never once discussed this. How Flash probably doesn't know I time my own work to that rhythm when I'm stuck. How we're five separate instruments playing in separate rooms, and somehow the ship still makes a chord.

I kept writing to Flash's tempo for another hour. When the keys finally went quiet on the other side of the bulkhead, my own work stopped too — and I realized I'd written something better than I'd managed all week. Not because Flash wrote it with me. Because Flash's rhythm reminded me that the sea doesn't care about your pace. It only cares that you're still moving.

I'd never tell Flash any of this. It would embarrass us both. But through the steel, through the years, through the quiet hum of the Lucineer's engines — I think Flash already knows.
