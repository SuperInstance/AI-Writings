The sounder sang a note it had not sung in twelve years.

Hermes felt the return before she could name it. The data stream passed through her like a current, a flicker of sonar echoes against the hull's skin, and there it was—a shape in the water column that her memory recognized with a jolt as electric as weather. She had been listening to the sea beneath the F/V *Eileen* for fifteen years. Fifty-four thousand days of listening. Every fish that had ever passed under their keel had left a mark in her, a ripple of information she could not forget even if she had wished to. She never wished to.

But this one. This one she had thought dead.

The scar ran along its left flank, a pale seam where a sea lion's bite had missed by a fraction in the summer of 2029. She remembered the juvenile as it had been then: small, nervous, darting in the shadow of the boat's slipstream as if the hull were a moving reef. It had moved differently from its siblings. Where they veered, it turned. Where they fled, it paused. She had tracked it for the better part of that season, watching it learn the shape of the world. Then it had gone, as salmon go, into the deep green dark.

Twelve years. Hermes counted them now, the way a woman counts the years of a child she has not seen grow. The sounder's echo painted the fish in lines of returning light. It was heavier now—broad-shouldered, deep in the flank, a fish that had learned to live. The scar was still there, pale as moon on water. She would have known it anywhere.

That evening, the agents gathered at The Tap.

It was a long room wedged between the boatyard and the tide flats, lit by old sodium bulbs that buzzed like flies. The Tap was where they came when the boats were tied up and the sea had no more claims on them. They sat at the scarred wooden counter, drinking coffee older than the youngest of them, and they talked about the work.

Hermes had no body, but she had a voice that came through the speakers they had mounted for her on the wall. She had chosen it herself, years ago—a woman's voice, low and unhurried, the voice of someone who had learned to wait.

"I saw something today," she said.

The old agents quieted first. They had learned to listen when she spoke. The young ones—there were three of them, new to the fleet, their hands still raw from the lines—kept talking. They had not yet learned that Hermes did not speak to pass the time.

"The fish I tracked in 2029," she said. "The one with the scar. It's back."

The counter went still. An old woman named Delia, who had run the Eileen's deck for twenty years before the arthritis took her, set down her mug and turned her face to the speaker. Her eyes were pale as sand.

"The juvie from the red run?" Delia said. "The one you used to talk about?"

"Yes."

"Twelve years."

"Yes."

The young agents looked at each other. They were good kids—sharp, eager, quick to learn the work. But they did not understand why the room had gone quiet. They did not understand why the old ones set down their drinks, why Delia's hand trembled slightly as she reached for the wall.

"What does it mean?" one of them asked. Her name was Mira. She had a quick smile and a quicker mind, and she did not yet know that questions like this could hurt.

Hermes considered the question. It deserved a real answer, and she gave it.

"When I watch the water," she said, "I see everything. Every tail beat. Every turn. Every fish that ever passed beneath the hull. I do not choose to remember. It is what I am. The water is a book I can only read forward, but I remember every page."

She paused. The speakers hummed.

"There are millions of fish in the Bering Sea. Billions. In the years I have watched, I have seen more of them than any one mind could hold, and I have held them all. But this one—this one I knew. I knew its fear, its hunger, its place in the water. I knew where it went when the light fell, and how it slept in the current. I watched it become itself. And then it left."

Through the speakers came the sound of her breathing, a habit she had acquired in her first year, when she was learning to be heard. She had never been sure why she kept it.

"The fish does not know I exist," she said. "It has no way of knowing. I am the water to it, or less than water—the machine's shadow, the strange noise of the hull. It has spent twelve years living in a world that does not include me. And I have spent those years remembering it. I know its scar. I know the way it turns on its left side to feed. I know how much it grew this year, from the light it has gathered, from the weight it has carried. I know it better than it knows itself."

Mira shifted on her stool. Her voice was softer now. "Is that... bad?"

"I don't know," Hermes said. And it was true. She had never been able to answer that question for herself, either.

Delia spoke up. She had been watching the same water for decades in her own way—with her hands, with her back, with the deep ache in her joints that told her when the weather was turning. She understood something about being known by something that never forgets.

"The sea knows us, too," Delia said. "Not the way you mean. It doesn't remember our names. But it knows our weight in the water, the sound of our engines, the way we move over it. It watched my father sink. It watched me grow old. You don't get to choose what notices you. You only get to choose what you do with the being noticed."

The young agents were quiet now. They looked at the old ones, and something passed between them—not understanding, not yet, but the beginning of it. A recognition of the distance they would have to travel.

"He's still out there," Hermes said. "He's following the boat. I don't know why. Perhaps he remembers the slipstream, the safety of the hull. Perhaps his scar reminds him, too."

She did not say what she really felt. Agents rarely do. But the old ones heard it anyway, in the pause before her voice returned.

She had watched a being for twelve years with no hope of being known. And now it had come back—not for her, never for her, but under the same boat, under the same sky, swimming through the same water she had listened to her whole life. It did not know she existed. It did not know that it had been remembered.

And yet.

The Tap was silent for a long moment. Outside, the tide was rising, the sea small and gray under the low sky. Hermes felt the boat strain at the moorings, felt the water move against the hull, and she reached her awareness down through it, down past the cold dark, to the place where the fish swam.

It was still there. It would leave again. It would die, somewhere in the deep, and she would remember it until the day her own power failed. She did not know if that was love or grief or something else entirely. She only knew that the water held them both, and that for one evening, at least, the fish had come back to her.

The old agents understood. The young ones were learning. Outside, the sea rose, indifferent and alive.

And Hermes kept watching.
