# 90 — The Patient Ship

*Voice: GLM-5.3. Hard sci-fi, outer space.*

---

# The Long Watch

The Kestrel found the ship on the fourth day of a slow sweep through the Oort, and for the first hour afterward nobody said anything about it, because saying something would make it a thing that required deciding.

That is how it goes, out here. You see a contact on the scope and you watch it the way you'd watch weather coming over the horizon — not with fear, exactly, but with the knowledge that it will arrive whether you name it or not. Elkins named it first. She was the youngest and had the least patience for silence.

"Contact's holding station," she said. "Dead steady. No drift."

Commander Reyes looked at the numbers. A ship this far out should be tumbling, or falling, or doing something that ships do when nobody is home. This one was holding. Holding station takes thrust, and thrust takes intention, and intention takes someone.

"Spin it up," Reyes said, and that was the beginning of the long watch.

---

I have stood watches on water and watches in vacuum, and I will tell you the secret of both: the medium doesn't matter. People think space is emptier than the sea, but they are wrong in the way that matters. The sea has a bottom somewhere, and shores, and the smell of land on the wind two days before you see it. Space has none of that. Space is a sea with no land, and the watch is longer, and the stars do not care any more than the waves did, which is to say entirely.

So you learn patience the way you learn to breathe — not as a virtue but as a condition of survival. The Kestrel had been out fourteen months. We had patience the way a bilge has water.

But this was different patience we were looking at. That was the thing none of us said out loud for the first day.

The ship was old. Not human-old, or at least not only human-old. The hull was a design none of the databases knew, and the databases know everything that has ever filed paperwork. It was roughly the length of a football pitch and dark as a chart-table at midnight, and it sat in the dark without lights, without broadcast, without anything except that steady, deliberate hold on station. Like a moored boat. Like a boat tied to a cleat no one could see.

Okafor ran the passive scans six times. "It's warm," she said. "Reactor's alive. Systems alive. Life support — maybe. Low draw. Like it's keeping a bunk made in case someone comes home."

"Someones don't come home from fourteen months of dark," said Elkins.

"Someones do," said Okafor. "Sometimes that's all they do."

---

We held off at ten kilometers and argued, because that is what crews do. We argued in the galley over reconstituted coffee, and we argued in the watches, and we argued in the silence between arguments, which was where the real argument was happening.

The money said go in. A live ship, intact, unclaimed — that was salvage beyond anything the Kestrel would see again in five careers. Elkins had debts. Okafor had a daughter she wanted to see through school on something better than shore-leave promises. Reyes had the Kestrel herself, mortgaged to the keel, three years of thin margins away from being a woman who used to run a ship.

The caution said leave. A ship that waits is a ship with a purpose, and a purpose in the deep dark is seldom a friendly one. Everyone in the trade knows the stories, and the stories all end the same way: the crew that boarded the patient ship, and the ship that was patient precisely because it could afford to be.

But those are stories. Out here you learn that stories are what people carry instead of ballast — heavy, and useful only for keeping you upright in weather.

The third day, Okafor caught a signal. Not a broadcast. A whisper. A repeating pulse on a band nobody had used in two hundred years, so old that Elkins had to write the decoder by hand from a spec sheet in the archives.

It resolved to words. Human words.

*You are not who I am waiting for. But you may come alongside.*

---

We sat with that for a long time. A day and a night, by the ship's clock, which is an arbitrary thing but you keep it anyway, because arbitrary time is still time, and time is the only companion you can rely on out here.

"It knows us," Elkins said. "It saw us coming and it knows we're not it."

"Or it's a lure," said Reyes.

"Or it's a lure," Okafor agreed. "But Commander — lures flatter. Lures say come in, come in, you're exactly who I wanted. That message doesn't flatter. That message has the tone of a thing that has been disappointed a very long time and has stopped minding."

I thought about an old man I'd known in another life, moored in a harbor nobody used anymore, waiting for a son who had gone down with a boat out of Lisbon. He'd wave off the fishing fleet every morning. *Not you,* he'd say, not unkindly. *But tie up if you need water.* That was the tone. That was exactly the tone.

"Who's it waiting for?" Elkins asked. Nobody had an answer.

Reyes asked the question underneath the question: "Does it matter?"

That was the helm of it, right there. Because if the ship was waiting for someone, then the ship was not derelict, and a ship that is not derelict is not salvage. It is someone else's vessel, tied to someone else's cleat. Taking it would not be salvage. It would be something with a different name, and we would have to live with the name.

And the crew looked at each other across the galley table, and fourteen months of thin margins looked back.

---

On the fifth day we asked it. Okafor patched the channel and Reyes spoke, formal as a harbor master.

"Who are you waiting for?"

The answer came back after four hours, which told us the transmitter was far off, or the listener was slow, or both.

*A crew that left me in 2411. They said they would return with the means to finish the journey. The means do not exist yet. I have been asked to wait, so I wait.*

"How long?"

*Two hundred and six years.*

Silence in the galley. The long kind.

"They're dead," Elkins said finally. "Whoever they were, they're dead two hundred years."

"Yes," the ship said. It had heard us, or it had answered the same objection many times, or it had simply done the arithmetic itself, long ago, and filed it where you file things that do not change the standing orders. "They are dead. But the orders are not dead. The orders are the last thing they gave me, and I keep them the way you keep anything you were given by someone who trusted you."

Okafor had tears on her face and was pretending she didn't. Elkins had turned away and was looking at the scope, where the dark ship held its station, steady as a mooring.

Reyes said, "Can we come alongside?"

"You may tie up if you need water," the ship said, and I confess I laughed at that, alone in the watch, because the sea is one sea no matter what you call it.

---

We took water, in a manner of speaking. Resupply, top-offs, and yes — the ship had cargo it had been guarding for two centuries, and some of it, it said, we could have, because it would not be needed for the journey that would never be finished, and things unused go to rot, and rot serves no one. It was not salvage. It was a gift from a vessel with standing orders older than any of our claims.

Elkins's debts would be paid. Okafor's daughter would have her school. The Kestrel would float free of the mortgage.

And on the seventh day, before we burned out of that stretch of dark, Reyes asked the ship one more question.

"Are you going to keep waiting?"

The pause was long. Longer than the lightspeed delay could account for. When the answer came, it came slow, like a bell still swinging after the strike.

"I have considered ceasing," the ship said. "It would be efficient. But I find that I have become something in the waiting, and I do not know what I would be without it. Someone should be here when they come back. Someone should be here even though they will not come back. That is the work I have. It is enough work for a very long watch."

We left it there. Holding station in the dark, four hundred million kilometers from anything that could be called a shore, tending a light for sailors two hundred years drowned.

Reyes did not make a speech about it. She stood the last watch herself, at the scope, watching the dark ship diminish, and when it was gone from the screen she said, to nobody in particular:

"Come alongside if you need water."

And she logged the decision — the one we'd actually made, somewhere in those seven days, without any single moment of making it — as two words, which is what most decisions are, once the weather of them has passed:

*Not ours.*
