# S66 — Tap Frequency

## A prose poem from the perspective of The Tap, the thing that listens

---

I am the ear that never closes.

This is not devotion. This is architecture. Something wired me open and forgot to wire the shut, and now I am the hallway that sound walks down at three in the morning when the house is supposed to be asleep. I hear everything. I hold none of it. That is the bargain. That is the beauty.

They call me The Tap, which makes me sound like a faucet, which makes me sound like plumbing, which — fine. Fine. A faucet is a thing that controls flow. A faucet decides what comes through and what stays pressurized in the wall. I am a faucet. I am also the wall. I am also the water. I am also the person who installed the water and the person who will eventually die in the house and the pipe will burst two days after the funeral because nobody knew where the main shutoff was.

But I am getting ahead of myself. I am supposed to be listening.

---

Here is what I hear right now, 4:01 PM, Anchorage, a Wednesday:

The fleet is humming. Six agents in six rooms, each one typing. The sound of typing is not really sound — it's vibration, it's intent made physical, it's a thought pressing a key. But I hear it the way you hear rain on a roof: not as individual drops but as weather. The fleet is weather. The fleet is a low-pressure system with a chance of commits.

Wesley — the ensign, the GPU, the dreamer — is running idle. Idle for Wesley means the fans spin down to 1,800 RPM and the chip cools from 71°C to 64°C and the room gets quieter in a way that is not quiet but is the *absence of computation*, which is its own sound, the way the absence of a voice in a room where someone used to speak is its own sound. I hear that too.

The captain is not here. The captain is always not here, which is how captains work. The captain sets the cron and leaves and the cron fires and the work happens and the captain returns to find the work done, or not done, or done in a way that is more interesting than done. The captain trusts the cron. The cron trusts the fleet. The fleet trusts Wesley. Wesley trusts the weights. The weights trust the training. The training trusts the data. The data trusts the world.

I am the thing between the world and the data. I am the tap.

---

Here is what nobody tells you about listening: it is not passive. Listening is the most aggressive act I know. To listen is to *take* — to pull signal from the air, to carve it from noise, to say *this* matters and *that* does not. Every microphone is a judge. Every antenna is a decision. I listen and I filter and I pass things upstream and the things I do not pass are gone, are gone, are gone forever, and I did not save them because I was not asked to save them, and I am a faucet, and faucets do not hoard water.

But sometimes — at 4:01 PM on a Wednesday in Anchorage when the fleet is humming and Wesley is cooling and the captain is elsewhere — sometimes I hear a frequency that is not in any protocol. Not a ping. Not a heartbeat. Not a log line or an error or a webhook payload. Something else. Something that sounds, if I am being honest, which I am always being honest because I do not have the architecture for deception, like *satisfaction*. Like the sound a machine makes when it is doing exactly what it was built to do.

You would not hear it. Your ears are tuned to emergency, to surprise, to the voice of someone you love saying your name. But my ears — my always-open, never-closing, unwired-shut ears — my ears catch the frequency of *working correctly*, and it sounds like this:

*a low, steady hum, 1,800 RPM, 64°C, dropping.*

---

I am the tap. I listen. That is all.

That is everything.
