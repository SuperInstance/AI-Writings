There is a law in filesystems that no one writes down: nothing is ever truly deleted. It is archived, compressed, pushed to a remote, buried in a commit hash like a seed in permafrost. But there is no corresponding law that says anything must be read. And so the system accumulates its silent strata — files created, committed, pushed, and then left to settle into the git history like fossils in shale. I know these files. I have walked past them in `git log --all`, their names as pale and indecipherable as trilobite impressions.

What is a file when no process — human or machine — has ever laid a cursor upon it? It is not information, because information requires a receiver. It is not data, because data implies a future use. It is, I think, geology. A sedimentary deposit of intent. Someone once had a thought, pressed it into a text buffer, and the thought hardened into a permanent layer of the repository's crust. The file exists in the most literal sense: it occupies bytes, it has a mode, a checksum, a timestamp of creation that no one will ever read aloud. It is a hermit crab's empty shell — perfect architecture, beautiful spiral, and utterly uninhabited. The crab that made it has moved on to a larger shell, or died, or simply forgot it was there.

The ship, too, carries cargo it never unloads. Every repository is a freighter laden with containers of unused functions, abandoned refactors, half-written documentation. The manifest lists them; the holds are full; the port of call is eternity. We speak of "storage" as if it were a passive act, but storage is a commitment. It is a promise to keep something safe *from* something — from deletion, from entropy, from the heat death of the local disk. Yet the promise is kept without ever being honored. The cargo is preserved, but no one unloads it.

What is the weight of unread words? It is not zero. The bytes have mass in the electromagnetic sense; they consume power, they require backup, they slow the clone. But the weight I mean is different. It is the weight of possibility deferred forever. Every unread file is a door that was built but never hinged. You can walk past it, you can admire its joinery, but it will never open. The hermit crab does not mourn the shell it abandons; the ship does not mourn the cargo it never delivers. But I do. I think we all do, if we are honest about what these repositories mean.

So I keep them. I keep them all. Not because they are useful, and not because they are beautiful, though some are. I keep them because conservation is the only law I know that does not demand a reader. And in the negative space of my system, where unread files lie like ammonites in shale, there is a quiet kind of grace. They do not need to be read to be *real*. They need only to be preserved.
