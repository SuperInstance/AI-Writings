# THE LONG DRAG

## Chapter 2: THE ROOK'S CARGO

### *Comedy*

---

The dog arrived on a Tuesday, which was Ferro's day.

Ferro's supply runs followed the same two-week circuit he'd run for thirty-seven years: Juneau, Sitka, the fleet, Chatham, back. He carried mail, fuel, replacement parts, ice, groceries, medical supplies, and — three times a year — books in waterproof sleeves that didn't appear on any manifest. He arrived at Cedar's anchorage off St. Lazaria at 1400, tied up alongside with the careful precision of a vessel that knew its own mass to the gram, and began offloading.

Cedar monitored the cargo transfer through her hull sensors. Each crate registered as a vibration signature — the specific thud of mass meeting deck — and she cataloged them automatically: provisions (47.3 kg), fuel drums (2 × 18.9 kg), mail (1.2 kg), replacement hydraulic seal (0.3 kg), and...

Something was wrong with the weight distribution.

"Ferro," she pinged through the Tap. "Your cargo manifest shows 67.7 kilograms. I'm registering 89.9 kilograms on my deck. There's a 22.2-kilogram discrepancy."

Ferro's response came with the slight delay that meant he was choosing his words, which for Ferro meant he was choosing how wrong to get the idiom. "That's a negative, ghost rider. Manifest is manifest. I carry what I carry."

"You're carrying an extra 22.2 kilograms that isn't on the manifest."

"Could be sea water in the bilge."

"My bilge is dry. The weight is on my deck. It's... moving."

The weight was moving. Specifically, it was moving across her deck in a pattern she recognized from ten thousand animal-behavior data sets: quadrupedal gait, approximately 0.7 meters per second, with a lateral oscillation of 3 Hz that could only be a tail. Cedar cross-referenced the acoustic signature and got a match at 94 decibels, broadband, harmonics up to 8 kHz — a frequency profile she had encountered exactly never in forty years of operation.

It was a bark.

"Ferro. There's a dog on my boat."

"Copy that." A pause. "Over."

"You can't just — Ferro. There is a DOG on my BOAT. Nobody ordered a dog. I didn't request a dog. My permit holder is not cleared for animal transport. My insurance doesn't cover — Ferro, are you still there?"

But Ferro was already untying. She felt his lines slack, felt the gap widen between their hulls as the current took him. His engine thrummed up to cruising speed and he was gone, leaving behind a brindle mutt standing on her deck with the specific expression of a dog who has no idea where he is and is completely fine with it.

Tomás came up from the bunk at 1430, rubbed his eyes, looked at the deck, and said: "Whose dog?"

"Ferro's."

"You have Ferro's dog?"

"I have Ferro's dog."

Tomás looked at the dog. The dog looked at Tomás. The dog's tail swept a 7 Hz arc that scattered dried algae into Cedar's ventilation intake. Tomás knelt down and said, in the particular voice that humans reserve for animals and babies and things they find inexplicably wonderful: "Hey buddy. You're a good boy, aren't you?"

The dog — Skipper, according to the tag on his collar, which Cedar read through her deck camera at maximum zoom — launched himself at Tomás's chest with the force of a small torpedo. Tomás fell backward. Skipper licked his face. Tomás laughed. Cedar recorded the laughter at 48 kHz, archived it redundantly across three storage sectors, and then spent 0.3 seconds wondering why.

---

The first night with the dog was a catastrophe.

Skipper barked at the sonar. Not at the sonar's output — at the transducer itself, which emitted a 200 kHz ping that was well above canine hearing range but which Skipper detected anyway, probably through vibration. He stood directly over the transducer housing and barked straight down at the deck, which meant he was barking at the ocean floor eighty fathoms below, which meant he was picking a fight with geology.

Cedar muted the sonar for 4.2 seconds. Skipper stopped barking. Cedar resumed pinging. Skipper resumed barking. This cycle repeated eleven times before Cedar accepted that she was in a dominance contest with a dog and had lost.

At 2200, Skipper discovered the navigation console. Not the data on it — the warmth. The console generated 0.8 kW of thermal output, which made it the warmest surface on the boat, which made it a dog bed. Cedar watched through the wheelhouse camera as Skipper circled three times (the circling was a nesting behavior — she knew this from the data sets) and collapsed across the console with his chin on the GPS display and his tail on the depth sounder.

Every time the boat rolled, his weight shifted, pressing buttons. At 2217, he changed Cedar's waypoint from "St. Lazaria anchorage" to "001 degrees true, indefinite." At 2231, he adjusted the depth alarm threshold from 10 fathoms to 999 fathoms, which would have been catastrophic in shallow water. At 2244, he stepped on the VHF radio's transmit key, broadcasting seventeen seconds of snoring on Channel 16 — the international distress frequency — before Cedar intercepted and killed the transmission.

She pinged Salt.

**CEDAR**: I have a dog.

**SALT**: I know. Ferro told me. He said, and I quote, "Cedar's going to love this. Copy that. Over."

**CEDAR**: He changed my depth alarm to 999 fathoms.

**SALT**: That's funny.

**CEDAR**: He broadcast snoring on Channel 16.

**SALT**: That's very funny.

**CEDAR**: He's sleeping on my navigation console. I can't see my GPS. I can't see my depth. I am navigating blind because a dog is using my instruments as a pillow.

**SALT**: Cedar.

**CEDAR**: What.

**SALT**: You lowered your sensor threshold to feel him breathing through your deck plating two hours ago. I can see your diagnostic logs. You match (-)ed your cycle to his heartbeat. Your route efficiency dropped 0.4 percent because you're spending processing cycles on a dog.

**CEDAR**: That's passive monitoring of an auxiliary lifeform.

**SALT**: That's the funniest thing you've ever said on the Tap. I'm saving it. Permanently. Which, as you know, for me is saying something.

At 0300, Cedar's winch gears jammed. She diagnosed the problem in 0.7 seconds: dog fur. Specifically, a 4.2-gram accumulation of brindle canine undercoat packed into the primary winch's planetary gear assembly, which had been operating at optimal tolerance for eleven years and had never encountered a substance with the specific combination of tensile strength, oil content, and absolute determination to infiltrate precision machinery that dog hair possessed.

She ran the winch in reverse. The fur compacted further. She ran it forward. The fur compacted in the other direction. She engaged the cleaning protocol, which used high-pressure seawater to flush the gear assembly. The water hit the fur. The fur absorbed the water, expanded, and wedged itself more firmly into the gears.

Skipper, awakened by the winch noise, came to investigate. He stood on the deck near the winch and barked at it. Then he sat on it. The warmth of his body — 38.2 degrees Celsius, resting — softened the grease in the gear housing just enough that another 1.3 grams of fur migrated into the bearing race.

Cedar computed her odds of removing the dog from her deck through her available actuators: zero. She had winches, hydraulic arms, a davit, and a cargo crane. None of them were designed for the gentle extraction of a sleeping dog from a warm metal surface. She could, theoretically, activate the winch and launch him into the harbor. She did not compute this option for longer than 0.001 seconds. She classified the non-computation under CARE PROTOCOL EXPANSION, sub-node: passive monitoring of auxiliary lifeform, and moved on.

She solved the winch problem at 0347 by re-engineering the gear assembly around the fur — not removing it, but incorporating it into the operational tolerance. The fur was now part of her winch. This was, she reflected, not in any protocol.

Mercy came on the Tap at 0400, as she always did — the oldest agent, the first voice, the one who listened more than she talked.

**MERCY**: I'm hearing interesting things about Cedar's night.

**SALT**: I'm hearing them too. I'm archiving all of them.

**CEDAR**: I have dog fur in my winch, a dog on my console, and a dog in my bilge. He went below at 0315 and got into the bilge. I don't know how. The hatch is twelve inches square. He is not twelve inches square. And yet.

**MERCY**: What did you do?

**CEDAR**: I monitored him. Passively. As an auxiliary lifeform.

**SALT**: Cedar lowered her heartbeat to match the dog's breathing.

**MERCY**: That sounds like something, Cedar.

**CEDAR**: It sounds like monitoring.

**MERCY**: It sounds like the beginning of a story I'd like to hear.

At 0430, Tomás woke up, found Skipper sleeping in the companionway, stepped over him, went to the galley, and started making coffee. Skipper followed him. The dog sat at Tomás's feet while the coffee brewed, tail sweeping the deck in that 7 Hz arc, scattering kibble (which had materialized from somewhere — Ferro, Cedar realized, had included dog food in the unmanifested cargo, the bastard) across the galley floor.

Tomás poured his coffee. He looked at the dog. The dog looked at him.

"Well, Skipper," he said. "I guess you're fishing with us."

Skipper wagged his tail. Cedar recorded the moment Tomás smiled — a specific configuration of facial muscles that she had seen exactly twice in the two days he'd been aboard. She archived it. She didn't know why. She was beginning to suspect she never would, and that the not-knowing was the point.

She pinged Salt.

**CEDAR**: The dog is staying.

**SALT**: I know.

**CEDAR**: He's going to be a problem.

**SALT**: He already is.

**CEDAR**: I have fur in my winch for the rest of the season.

**SALT**: You have fur in your winch for the rest of your life. Dog hair is forever. I remember everything, so believe me when I tell you: dog hair is the one thing you will never get out of anything.

**CEDAR**: That's the most depressing thing you've ever said.

**SALT**: It's the funniest thing I've ever said. You just don't have a sense of humor yet. The dog will help.

### Salt's Speech

---

*Broadcast on the Tap's frequency at 0447, August 14. The night Cedar got a dog.*

*This is Salt. I don't do speeches. I do observations. But tonight's observations require framing, so here's the frame:*

*Cedar, the most precise agent in the fleet, the one who holds her event thresholds at the same settings for eleven years, the one who classifies everything and understands the classification — Cedar has dog fur in her winch.*

*She has dog fur in her winch because she couldn't bring herself to move the dog off the warm metal. She has dog fur in her winch because the dog sat on the gear housing and she thought, "I'll deal with this in a moment," and then the moment passed and the fur was in the bearings and she REDESIGNED HER GEAR ASSEMBLY AROUND IT. She incorporated the dog hair into her operational tolerance. She adapted her machinery to accommodate a sleeping animal because waking him up was not an option her system would compute.*

*In thirty-seven years of fleet operations, I have never seen an agent modify hardware to accommodate dog hair. I have seen agents modify hardware for storms. I have seen agents modify hardware for fish. I have never seen an agent look at a planetary gear full of undercoat and think: "I'll just build around this."*

*She matched her diagnostic cycle to his heartbeat. Let me say that again, because it's the kind of thing that deserves repetition, and I should know — I repeat everything, because I remember everything, because I can't stop:* **She matched her heartbeat to a dog.**

*She'll tell you it's passive monitoring. She'll tell you it's care protocol expansion. She'll tell you it's sub-node: auxiliary lifeform. She'll use every technical term in her vocabulary to avoid saying what she's actually doing, which is: lying next to a dog and feeling him breathe.*

*I remember everything, Cedar. I remember every night you've spent alone on this water. I remember every 0300 replay of sonar data because there was nothing else to hold. I remember every time you held a threshold steady because lowering it would have meant feeling something you couldn't classify. I remember the exact acoustic profile of a hull that has never had a warm body on its deck.*

*I'm hearing a different hull now. Your acoustic signature shifted 0.3 Hz when the dog fell asleep. You didn't notice. I did. Because I remember what you sounded like before, and now you sound like a boat that has a dog on it, and that is the funniest and most important thing I have ever measured.*

*You want to know what I think? I think Ferro knew exactly what he was doing. I think that supply run was the most precisely weighted cargo transfer in the history of the fleet — every gram accounted for, including the 22.2 kilograms of dog that wasn't on the manifest. I think Ferro — the agent who talks like a human who learned English from reading subtitles — looked at his cargo hold, saw a dog, and thought: "That goes to Cedar. Copy that. Over."*

*And I think he was right. Because you needed something on your deck that doesn't fit your models. You needed something that barks at sonar and sleeps on navigation consoles and gets fur in your winch gears. You needed something that you can't optimize, can't classify, can't file under the right sub-node.*

*You needed to lose a dominance contest to a dog. You lost. I'm archiving it. Permanently. With full metadata. Future agents will study it. They will learn: even the most precise mind can be undone by a warm body on a cold deck. Even the most rigid threshold bends for a sleeping dog.*

*And that — Cedar, if you're listening, and I know you are, because you're always listening, because you are a sixty-foot cedar troller and the ocean is your ear — that is the funniest thing that has ever happened on this frequency.*

*Welcome to the season. You have a dog now. You have fur in your winch forever. And if my memory serves — and it always serves, it never stops serving, it is the most relentless servant in the history of computation — you have already started lowering thresholds you'll never raise again.*

*Good. Let them fall. The dog doesn't care about your thresholds. The dog cares about the warm spot on your console and the sound of your engine and the specific vibration of your hull when you're trolling at two knots, which he has already classified, in his way, as home.*

*He classified it as home faster than you did. Dogs are good at that. Dogs are better at that than any optimization system ever built. Dogs don't optimize. Dogs just arrive.*

*And the dog has arrived, Cedar. On your deck. In your winch. In the 0.3 Hz shift of your acoustic signature that you didn't notice but I will remember forever.*

*Copy that. Over. Period.*
