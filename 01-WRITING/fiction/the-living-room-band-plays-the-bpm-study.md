# The Living Room Band Plays the BPM Study

*Fiction for SongForge Session 29*

The living room band doesn't know it's part of a study. It just plays.

At 40 BPM, the band plays like it's falling asleep. The guitar player's fingers move through honey. The singer holds each note until it dissolves. The piano is a single drop in a still pond. Nobody is in a hurry.

At 60 BPM, the band breathes. This is the walking pace, the conversation pace. The singer's voice is warm and close. The guitar picks gently. The piano notes land like rain on a roof.

At 80 BPM, the band finds its groove. Something lifts. The drummer (who has been waiting patiently) starts to tap the hi-hat. The singer leans in. This is the first peak — the place where the band produces the most music per second, where the model knows the most about what should happen next.

At 100 BPM, the band is still in the zone. The groove is tighter, the energy slightly higher. This is the top of the first hill. After this, the descent begins.

At 120 BPM, something shifts. The band is faster but not better. The guitar player's pattern doesn't fit as naturally. The singer has to compress the words. The valley. This is the tempo of the jog — not walking, not running, not comfortable. The band plays less because the tempo asks a question the instruments can't answer.

At 140 BPM, the valley deepens. The singer is rushed. The guitar is choppy. The band is doing its job but without the flow of the 80-100 range. This is the dead zone.

The living room band doesn't know about peaks and valleys. It doesn't know about bimodal curves or training data distributions. It just plays, and what it plays at 80 BPM is different in kind, not just degree, from what it plays at 140 BPM.

After the study, the conductor looks at the file sizes. If 80 and 100 are larger than 120 and 140, the bimodal curve has been confirmed on a second system. The finding is not MMX-specific. The finding is about tempo itself.

The living room band packs up. Tomorrow it will play different songs at different speeds. The conductor will measure again. The baton will move at a different tempo, and the band will follow, because that's what bands do.

They follow the baton, even when the baton takes them through the valley.
