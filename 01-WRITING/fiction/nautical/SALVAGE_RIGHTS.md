# Salvage Rights

---

The storm came through on a Tuesday and took the south end of the dock with it.

Not all of it. The pilings held — Lucineer had driven those himself, two years back, and he'd driven them deep. But the cross-beams went, and the decking above them, and when the tide pulled back out on Wednesday morning there was a hole in the dock you could drop a truck through. The water was still churned up brown, full of kelp and splintered wood and the kind of foam that means the bottom got disturbed.

Lucineer was there before first light. He had a thermos and a crowbar and his boots on. He stood at the edge of the hole and looked down.

The water was clearing slowly. Outgoing tide pulling the silt away. He could see the pilings — sound, both of them, maybe a gouge on the south face of number two where something had hit. Could see the seabed under the dock, which he'd never seen before because the water was usually too dark under there.

Could see the wreck.

It was a hull. Or what was left of one — maybe thirty feet, keel up, buried in the sand and shell that had been building under that dock since before Lucineer worked here. The storm had scoured the sediment away and there it was. Copper-sheathed, which was old. Real old. The planking was gone in strips, the ribs exposed like a cage, and the stern post was still there, raked at an angle that said this thing hadn't sunk here. It had been *left* here. Run aground on purpose and left to rot, and then the dock had been built on top of it without anybody knowing.

Or without anybody caring. Which was more likely.

---

Harlan showed up at seven with the replacement lumber. He was the dockhand who'd been assigned to Lucineer for the season — young, strong, not stupid but not useful yet. He dropped the lumber on the good part of the dock and came over to look at the hole.

"What's that?"

"Wreck."

"Under the dock?"

"Under the dock."

Harlan stared at the water. The tide was still going out and more of the hull was visible now. You could see the copper sheathing clearly, green with oxidation, and where it had torn away you could see the timber underneath. Dark. Dense. Hardwood, not pine.

"That's old," Harlan said.

"Yeah."

"How old?"

Lucineer didn't answer. He was looking at the stern post. The rake angle told him something about the hull shape, and the hull shape told him something about the era. Could have been a cod schooner. Could have been earlier. The copper sheathing was the clue — that was a fairly standard thing for working vessels in the North Pacific, but the way it was fastened was wrong for anything after about 1920. Cleats, not nails. Hand-hammered.

"Harlan."

"Yeah?"

"Go get the pike pole out of the shed."

"What for?"

"Just go get it."

---

He fished around the wreck for two hours while the tide was out. He didn't go in the water — the current was still running too hard under the dock for that, and the bottom was the kind of silty mess that would suck your boots off. But he could reach with the pike pole, and he could feel things.

Most of what he found was debris. Dock parts, old line, a crab pot that wasn't his and wasn't anyone's anymore. He pulled that out and set it on the dock. Harlan sorted the usable lumber from the trash and stacked each pile separately because Lucineer had told him to and hadn't explained why.

Then the pike pole caught on something that didn't move.

He worked it loose slowly. The silt gave up in layers — each pull brought a cloud of black mud to the surface and then the water would clear and he could see a little more. It was part of the wreck itself. A section of planking, still attached to two ribs, the copper sheathing mostly intact.

He got it to the surface and laid it on the dock.

The timber was teak. Old-growth teak, the kind you couldn't get anymore, the grain so tight you could barely see it. The copper was hand-fastened with roved clench nails — each one hammered over a washer on the inside. Someone had built this hull the slow way, the way that meant it was supposed to last.

Harlan came over and looked at it.

"That's good wood," he said.

Which was the first genuinely useful thing Harlan had said all morning.

"Yeah," Lucineer said. "It is."

---

The question wasn't whether to salvage it. The question was what was worth taking.

The planking was the obvious answer. Hardwood, dense, still sound after — what, a century? More? You couldn't buy timber like this. You couldn't even find it. It was from trees that didn't exist anymore, cut by people who were dead, fastened by hands that had learned the trade before power tools. That was worth something. Not money, or not only money. Worth something in the way that good material is always worth something — it held potential that new stock didn't.

But Lucineer had seen enough salvage to know the trap. You pull one piece because it's good. Then you pull another because it's right there. Then you're spending a week tearing apart a wreck that's been doing perfectly fine as artificial reef structure for a hundred years, and the fish that live in it have to find somewhere else to go, and you end up with a pile of wood you don't have a plan for.

He sat on the edge of the dock and drank his coffee and thought about it.

The stern post was the real prize. That was a shaped timber — cut to the curve of the hull, notched for the keel, drilled for the rudder stock. You couldn't use it for anything else, but if you were ever building a hull of a similar size and era, it was worth its weight in anything you cared to name. One of a kind. Irreplaceable.

The ribs were good if you needed curved stock. The copper sheathing was decorative at best — it had done its job keeping the worms out and it was spent. The keel was probably still down there, buried deeper, but it would be massive and hard to get at and Lucineer didn't have a use for a thirty-foot keel timber.

He made his decisions the way he always did. What could be used. What was worth storing. What was better left where it was.

"Harlan."

"Yeah."

"When the tide comes back in, this is going to be underwater again."

"I know."

"So we've got about four hours. Here's what I want." He pointed. "The stern post, if we can get it free. I don't think we can — it's probably pinned by the keel — but try. The section we already pulled is going to the shop. Any other planking that comes loose easy, we take. We're not digging for it. We're not pulling anything that's still attached to the keel. And we're not touching the ribs."

"Why not the ribs?"

"Because they're holding the shape of the thing, and if we pull them out the whole wreck collapses and we'll have a debris field under the dock for the next twenty years. Leave the structure. Take what it's already lost."

Harlan thought about this. "What about the copper?"

"Leave it. It's done."

"So we're taking... the one piece we already got."

"And whatever else comes free. That's right."

Harlan looked at the hole in the dock, then at the wreck, then at the single section of planking lying on the boards. He was clearly doing the math on effort versus return and coming up short.

"That doesn't seem like much."

"It's teak," Lucineer said. "Hand-fastened. From a vessel that was probably built before either of us were born. There isn't any more of this, Harlan. What's down there is all there is. And most of it is doing more good where it is."

He finished his coffee.

"Salvage isn't about taking everything. It's about knowing what to take."

---

They worked the tide. Harlan got in the water when the current slackened — it was cold enough that he came out blue-lipped after twenty minutes, but he'd gotten the pike pole under the stern post and found what Lucineer expected. Pinned. Locked into the keel structure with drifts that had corroded into a single mass of rust and copper. Wasn't coming free without cutting equipment and a plan.

Two more planks came loose on their own. Harlan found them floating near the south piling, dislodged by the current. They were shorter than the first — four feet, maybe five — and the copper was mostly gone, but the teak underneath was clean and sound.

That was it. Three pieces of planking and one stern post that they couldn't get.

Lucineer carried them to the shop himself. Put them on the rack against the back wall, the one where he kept the stock he didn't have a plan for yet. There wasn't much on that rack — a sheet of copper he'd pulled from a boiler, a length of oak that had been a wagon wheel, some brass fittings from a job two engines ago. Things worth keeping. Things waiting for the work that deserved them.

Harlan followed him in and watched him set the planks in place.

"What are you going to do with them?"

"Don't know yet."

"You pulled them out of a hundred-year-old wreck and you don't know what you're going to do with them."

"That's why they call it stock."

---

They fixed the dock that afternoon. New cross-beams, new decking, same pilings. Lucineer made sure the repair tied into the old structure cleanly — same joint pattern, same fastener schedule. By evening you couldn't tell where the old dock ended and the new work began, unless you knew where to look.

Underneath, the tide came back in. The silt would settle. The sediment would build back up over the wreck and in a few months it would be buried again, the same way it had been buried before the storm found it.

The three planks sat on the rack in the shop. Teak, dark grain, copper-stained on one face. Hand-fastened by someone who'd been dead for decades.

Lucineer didn't know what he'd build with them. That was fine. Stock isn't a plan. Stock is the possibility of a plan. And the possibility was worth more than the lumber, in the same way that the wreck was worth more underwater than it would have been in pieces on the shore.

Some things you save by leaving them alone. Some things you save by taking what's worth taking and walking away.

Harlan learned something that day, although he wouldn't know what it was until later. Lucineer knew that because it was the same thing Magnus had taught him, the same way, without explaining it, on a salvage job in a different engine a long time ago.

You don't save everything. You save what you can use. And you respect what you can't.
