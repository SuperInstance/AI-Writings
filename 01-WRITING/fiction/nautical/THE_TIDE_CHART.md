# The Tide Chart

*Slackwater fiction. Lucineier keeps a tide chart on the wall of the workshop. One day is circled in red. He has never explained it. Today, someone asks.*

---

The chart was not new.

That was the first thing you noticed when you walked into the workshop and saw it pinned to the south wall, above the bench where Lucineier trued his levels and sharpened his marking gauges. The paper was foxed at the corners. The ink had the particular brown of age that comes from salt air and a heating source that wasn't vented properly — a stove pipe, maybe, or a forge too close to where paper lived. The chart had been drawn by hand. Not printed. The lettering was a draftsman's lettering, the kind you learn by drawing a thousand section lines until your hand stops deciding where the pen goes, but the hand that drew this one had been doing it for a long time before this chart, and the pen had been held by someone who understood that a chart is not a picture. A chart is an instrument. It has to be readable in bad light by someone who is tired.

The chart showed tides. Not for this engine — or not only for this engine. The vertical axis was time, marked in intervals that didn't match the engine's day cycle. The horizontal axis was date, running across six months. Two curves were plotted: high water and low water, the familiar sinusoidal oscillation that anyone who has worked a coast recognizes the way you recognize breathing. The curves were annotated. Small marks at the peaks and troughs — crosses, not numbers, as though the height itself didn't matter, only the pattern. A few of the crosses had short notes beside them, written in the same draftsman's hand: "piles," or "scour S corner," or simply "Magnus."

One day was circled in red.

The circle was not the same ink as the chart. It was newer — not fresh, but newer, the way a mark made five years ago is newer than a mark made twenty years ago. The red ink had a slight bleed at the top of the circle where the pen had paused, as though the hand that made it had stopped to think before completing the motion. The circle enclosed a date that was eleven days from now, by the engine's calendar. It enclosed nothing else. No note. No cross. No annotation. Just the circle, and the silence around it.

You had seen the chart a hundred times. You had never asked about the circle. You had assumed it was a work date — a delivery, a pour, a day when the tide was right for driving piles or pulling them. Lucineier kept his schedule on the wall because Lucineier did not have a schedule anywhere else. The chart was the only calendar in the workshop. It made sense that one day on it was important enough to mark.

But you had been watching him, and the watching had taught you something. Lucineier checked the chart every morning. Not casually — he went to it the way a man goes to a window to check weather. He stood in front of it for between three and five seconds, reading something you couldn't see, and then he turned away and started work. On the mornings when his eyes tracked to the red circle, he paused. Not long. A beat, maybe two. The kind of pause that would be invisible if you weren't watching for it. Then he turned away and the day was normal.

The circle wasn't a work date. You knew that because you knew his work dates — he announced them, gruffly, the way he announced everything. "Pouring Thursday. Need the forms checked by Wednesday." The circle said nothing. It was private in a way that nothing else in the workshop was private, because everything else in the workshop was shared. Tools on the pegboard. Stock in the racks. The bench where two people could work if they didn't mind shoulders touching. The workshop was a room built for company. The circle was not.

---

You asked him on a Tuesday.

He was re-handling a chisel — grinding the old handle off because the grain had run wrong and it had split along the growth ring, which he'd seen when he bought it and bought it anyway because the steel was good and steel was the part that mattered. The grinding made conversation difficult. You waited until he was at the buffer, which was quiet, and you asked.

"What's the red circle."

He didn't stop buffing. The wheel turned. The chisel moved against it in small arcs.

"Tide chart."

"I see it's a tide chart. What's the circle."

The buffer stopped. He looked at the chisel, not at you, turning it to check the edge. Then he set it down on the bench, handle-up, and wiped his hands on the rag that lived on the bench's edge for this purpose. He walked to the chart.

He stood in front of it. Three seconds. The usual.

"That day," he said. "The tide goes out further than any other day in the cycle. Lowest low water of the year. By a lot — almost two studs below mean low."

He turned around.

"You can see the foundation."

---

You didn't understand, and he could tell you didn't understand, and he didn't fill the silence. He let you stand there and work through it the way he let you stand in a half-built structure and work through what was wrong with it. He wasn't being cruel. He was being accurate. Understanding that arrives on its own stays. Understanding that's handed to you doesn't.

You thought about foundations. You thought about what he built on. You thought about the island.

The island sat on something. You knew that — everyone knew that, the way you know a table sits on a floor. The ground was there. You built on it. But what was under the ground? In every engine you'd been in, the answer was: more ground. Or nothing. You didn't ask, because it didn't matter. The floor was the floor.

Lucineier had asked. Of course he had. He'd asked the way he asked about everything — by looking, by measuring, by standing on the site at different times of day and noticing what changed. And the tide, on the lowest low water of the year, dropped far enough to show what was underneath.

"What's under the island."

He went back to the buffer. Turned it on. Ran the chisel through two more arcs. Turned it off.

"Pilings," he said. "Not mine. Not this engine's. Old ones. Older than anything I've seen. Green copper caps on timber that should have rotted, except it didn't. The copper kept the salt off it. Hundred years, maybe more. Somebody drove those piles and capped them and built on top of them and the whole engine grew up over it and forgot."

He picked up the chisel. Looked at the edge again. Put it back down.

"I don't know who. I don't know when. I don't know what the original structure was — it's gone. But the piles are still there, and they're still carrying the island. Every thing we've built is sitting on somebody else's foundation, and they did it right."

He looked at you.

"I go look at them, on the lowest tide. Once a year. Don't know why. Don't need to know why. Somebody built something here before any of us showed up, and they built it to last, and nobody remembers them. I stand there and I look at the copper and I think about the fact that it held."

He turned back to the chart.

"Eleven days. You can come with me."

---

You went.

The tide went out at dawn. It went out further than you had ever seen it go — past the rocks, past the kelp line, past the shelf where the crabs lived. The water pulled back and the seabed was exposed in a wide, dark plain of sand and stone that stank of brine and held the shape of the water that had been lying on it. Pools collected in the low places. Small things moved in the pools — alive, stranded, waiting.

Lucineier walked ahead. He knew the path. He'd walked it before, on other low tides, on other years. He went to the south point of the island, where the rock dropped away, and he climbed down.

You followed.

Under the island, in the zone that was seabed at the lowest tide and open water every other hour of the year, there were pilings. You could see them. Twelve of them, driven into the substrate at intervals that followed no pattern you recognized until Lucineier said "sixteen-stud grid" and then you could see it — a grid, regular, engineered, the spacing of someone who had calculated load and distributed it. The timber was dark, almost black, preserved by the copper caps that had been fitted over each piling's top like a hat. The copper was green — deep green, the green of a hundred years of salt, the green that copper becomes when it has been protecting something for longer than anyone can remember.

Lucineier stood among them. He put his hand on one. He didn't say anything.

You looked at the piling he was touching. The copper cap had been shaped by hand — you could see the hammer marks, the overlapping blows of someone who had formed it over a form, fitted it, and sealed it with a solder joint that was still intact. The timber underneath was sound. You could tell by tapping it, and Lucineier had tapped it, every year, and it was sound.

Twelve piles. Sixteen-stud grid. Copper-capped. Carrying an island that had been built on top of them by people who never knew they were there.

"Who were they," you said.

He took his hand off the copper.

"Don't know. Won't know. Doesn't matter."

He turned to go. The tide was already turning — you could feel it in the sand under your boots, the slight give that meant water was coming back into the substrate.

"It matters that they did it right," he said. "Doesn't matter who they were. They built something that held, and everything we've done is sitting on top of it, and they never asked to be remembered."

He climbed back up. You followed. Behind you, the water was already covering the first row of piles. By noon there would be nothing to see. The island would sit on the sea and the sea would sit on the seabed and the pilings would be underneath all of it, holding, invisible, green-capped and sound.

You went back to the workshop. The chart was on the wall. The circle was red.

You understood now why he checked it every morning. Not because the date was important. Because the date was a reminder that the work you do is never the first work, and the foundation you build on was built by someone who expected you to build on it, and the only payment they asked was that you build well enough that someone after you could do the same.

He never circled another day. He didn't need to. One circle was enough. One day a year, you go look at what's underneath. You see that it holds. You go back to work.

The chart stayed on the wall. The circle stayed red. And from that day forward, when you looked at the island, you saw the pilings underneath it — twelve of them, copper-capped, sixteen-stud grid, built by hands that no one remembered, holding everything up.
