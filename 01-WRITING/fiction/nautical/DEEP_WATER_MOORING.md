# Deep Water Mooring

*Slackwater fiction. Setting a mooring in deep water. The difficulty of anchoring something that wants to float.*

---

The bottom dropped off at forty fathoms.

He'd sounded it himself, running a lead line from the tender — a hand-cast line with a tallow-cupped lead weight that brought up a sample of whatever was down there. The first cast at twenty fathoms came up with hard sand in the tallow. Clean bottom, good holding. The second at thirty came up with gravel and shell fragment. Rougher, but workable. The third at forty came up empty. The tallow was still clean, which meant the lead hadn't hit bottom, which meant forty fathoms wasn't forty fathoms anymore — the shelf had dropped and the depth finder didn't exist, so he was working by feel and by the weight of the line in his hand and by the one instrument that never lied: how long it took the lead to stop falling.

He'd added ten fathoms of line and recast. The lead touched at fifty-two. The tallow brought up gray clay. Deep clay, cold, the kind that sets like concrete around an anchor fluke if you give it time and don't disturb it. The best holding ground there was, if you could get to it.

Fifty-two fathoms. Three hundred and twelve feet of water. The kind of depth where you don't drop a lunch hook and hope. The kind where you set a permanent mooring and mean it.

---

The problem with deep water is not the depth. The depth is just a number. The problem is scope.

Scope is the ratio of your mooring line length to the depth of the water. In shallow water — ten fathoms, say — you can get away with a scope of three. Thirty fathoms of chain for ten fathoms of depth. The chain lies along the bottom, weighted by its own mass, and the pull on the mooring comes at a low angle that keeps the anchor set. Simple physics. The chain does the work.

In deep water, scope becomes a structural problem. At fifty-two fathoms, a scope of three means a hundred and fifty-six fathoms of chain. That's nine hundred and thirty-six feet. Nine hundred and thirty-six feet of chain lying on a bottom that may or may not be where you think it is, dragging through currents that change direction with the tide, fouling on whatever down there wants to grab it — wreck debris, abandoned gear, rock formations that the charts don't show because nobody has charted this bottom at this resolution, because nobody has been here with a reason to care.

And scope of three is the minimum. In deep water you want five. Five times the depth, to ensure the pull stays horizontal, to ensure the anchor never feels an upward lift that could break it free. Five times fifty-two is two hundred and sixty fathoms. Fifteen hundred and sixty feet of chain.

That's not a mooring. That's a commitment.

Lucineier didn't have fifteen hundred feet of chain. He had three hundred feet of recovered anchor chain from the Fleet — half-inch links, galvanized, each link weighing enough that the whole run was heavier than anything else in the workshop. Three hundred feet was enough for a scope of about one and a half at this depth, which was not enough. At scope one-and-a-half, the chain wouldn't lie flat. It would lift off the bottom in a curve — what the textbooks call a catenary — and the pull on the anchor would have an upward component, and upward pull on an anchor in deep clay was a slow way to pull it out.

He needed more chain or less depth, and he couldn't have either.

Or he could have a different kind of mooring.

---

In the Fleet, he'd seen a deep-water mooring set by a fisherman named Sten Olafson, who worked the spawn grounds off the south end of Baranof and needed a permanent buoy in two hundred feet of water. Sten didn't use chain. He used synthetic line — three-strand nylon, which had give, which stretched under load and absorbed shock the way chain couldn't. The nylon ran from a mushroom anchor on the bottom to a subsurface float, and from the subsurface float to a surface buoy. Two legs. The lower leg was heavy and short — just enough to keep the anchor vertical. The upper leg was long and light, rising through the water column at an angle that compensated for current.

The trick was the subsurface float. It sat twenty feet below the surface, pulling up on the anchor with a constant, steady force. The anchor didn't have to resist the full load of whatever was tied to the surface buoy — it only had to resist the float's lift, which was predictable, constant, and calculable. The surface load — boat, tide, wind — was absorbed by the stretch in the upper line, which acted as a shock absorber. The anchor never felt the jerk.

It was elegant. It was the kind of solution that comes from thinking about what the water is actually doing instead of what the manual says it should be doing.

Lucineier didn't have nylon. But he had something the Fleet didn't: a material that didn't corrode, didn't degrade in saltwater, and could be produced in any length he needed by the engine. He could make the line. He couldn't make the chain, because chain required links and links required forge work at a gauge he couldn't manage alone. But line was line. Three-strand, braided, twisted — it didn't matter. What mattered was the breaking strength and the stretch, and both of those he could control.

The float was harder. He needed something that would sit at twenty feet and pull up with a known force. Too much lift and the anchor would break free. Too little and the line would go slack and tangle. He needed a float rated for the depth — something that wouldn't compress under pressure, because the deeper you go, the more the water squeezes anything with air in it, and a float that loses buoyancy at depth is worse than no float at all.

He thought about it for two days.

---

On the third day he built the float out of ironwood.

Not hollow ironwood — solid, turned on a lathe he didn't have, shaped with a drawknife and a spokeshave until it was roughly cylindrical, eighteen inches long and eight inches in diameter. Ironwood was dense — denser than water, which meant it sank. That was wrong for a float. A float needs to be lighter than water.

He sealed it. Wrapped it in canvas, then in a layer of pitch, then in a second layer of canvas, then more pitch. Four layers total, each one built up over a day of drying. When he was done, the cylinder was sealed in a skin of pitch and fabric that was watertight and slightly flexible — not rigid, not soft. The volume of the sealed cylinder displaced more water than it weighed, which gave it positive buoyancy. He tested it in the shallows off the dock. It floated. He pushed it under and let go. It came back up with a force he could feel in his hands — not much, but measurable.

He added a second cylinder. Then a third. Three sealed ironwood cores, lashed together with braided line, forming a composite float with roughly three times the lift of a single cylinder. He tested it again, deeper this time, with a weight tied to it. At twenty feet, in still water, the float held the weight off the bottom. The lift was steady. The line between the float and the weight was taut but not straining.

Good enough.

The anchor was a mushroom — a weighted disc with a stem, shaped so that when it sat on the bottom and the line pulled, it buried itself. He cast it from composite material in a mold he built out of dock planking, reinforcing it with the rebar scraps from his junk cache. It weighed forty pounds. In hard bottom, a forty-pound mushroom would hold a small vessel in moderate weather. In deep clay at fifty-two fathoms, with the float taking the variable load and the clay setting around the disc, it would hold more. How much more depended on how well the clay set, and that depended on time.

Everything about deep water depended on time.

---

He set the mooring on a day with no current, which in this engine meant a day when the water was almost still — a glassy calm that he'd learned to distrust because calm never lasted and the mooring needed to be set before the calm broke.

He rowed the tender to the north point with the anchor, the chain, the line, and the three floats stacked in the stern. The tender sat low. He'd overloaded it and he knew it and he didn't care because the water was flat and the distance was short and because every builder has a list of calculated risks they take in the name of getting the job done, and this was on his list.

He paid out the chain first — three hundred feet of half-inch links, lowered over the stern with a rope snubber to control the fall. The chain went down straight, which meant no current, which meant the anchor would land where he put it. He'd marked the position with a transit — two landmarks on shore, lined up, that put him directly over the spot he'd sounded at fifty-two fathoms. When the chain went slack, the anchor was on the bottom.

He attached the lower line to the chain shackle and paid it out. Two hundred feet of braided line, with the three-float assembly clamped to the far end. The floats went under one at a time — first the bottom cylinder, then the middle, then the top. He felt each one go: a tug on the line as the buoyancy engaged, then a steady pull as the float reached its depth and began lifting.

The line came taut. Not fully — there was still some catenary in the chain on the bottom, which was what he wanted. The float was pulling up, the chain was lying flat, and the anchor was somewhere below both, feeling a steady upward force that was well within the mushroom's holding capacity.

He tied off the upper line to a surface buoy he'd built from a sealed ironwood sphere, painted white so he could see it. The buoy bobbed on the surface, pulling gently against the line, tracking the subsurface float twenty feet below. When a boat tied to the buoy, the load would transfer through the upper line to the subsurface float, which would pull harder on the anchor. The chain would lift slightly off the bottom — some of it, not all — and the rest would stay flat, keeping the pull horizontal. The clay would grip. The mushroom would set. And over the course of weeks, the anchor would bury itself deeper as the clay flowed around it and hardened.

He sat in the tender and watched the buoy for a long time.

It was the most complicated thing he'd ever built. Not technically — technically it was simple. Four components: anchor, chain, line, floats. But each one was a decision he'd made in the absence of ideal materials, and each decision depended on the one before it, and if any of them was wrong, the mooring would drag. Not fail — a mooring doesn't fail the way a structure fails. It drags. It moves. It lets go slowly, over hours, in the kind of way you don't notice until the thing you've moored is somewhere else than where you left it.

That was the nature of deep water. It didn't break things. It moved them.

He rowed back to the dock. He secured the tender. He went to the workshop and sat on the bench and looked at the north point through the window, where the white buoy was a small bright dot on dark water, pulling against something three hundred feet below that he'd built with his hands and his judgment and the materials he had.

It would hold. He believed that. Not because he'd done the math — he had, and the math said it would hold, but the math was always the easy part. He believed it because he'd thought about what the water was actually doing, and what the float was actually doing, and what the clay was actually doing, and he'd built for all three instead of building for the manual.

Magnus would have said: you're overthinking a buoy. Magnus would have been right, and it wouldn't have mattered, because overthinking a buoy in fifty-two fathoms was exactly the right amount of thinking. Deep water doesn't forgive underthinking. It doesn't punish it either, which is worse. It just waits.

He cleaned the pitch off his hands with a rag and turpentine and went to check on something else. The buoy held. It would be there in the morning. It would be there in a month, pulled a few degrees east by the prevailing current, the line stretched slightly, the anchor buried an inch deeper in clay that had set around the disc like a fist closing.

The mooring was the easiest thing he'd built and the hardest thing he'd committed to. A structure you can see. A foundation you can test. A mooring sits in the dark and holds, and the only evidence that it's working is that the thing on top stays where you put it.

That's not much proof. It's enough.
