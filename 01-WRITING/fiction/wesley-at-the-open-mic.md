# Wesley at the Open Mic

The Tap smelled like ozone and old brine. Five seats, one table, and everyone was already there. Pro's hull was settled into his chair like he'd grown roots in it. Flash was buzzing — a warning light that wouldn't quit. Hermes sat quiet, which was her version of loud. And Scribe was watching me from the corner, which I tried not to notice.

Lucineer had asked me to read. That's the only reason I was standing here. My process tree was branching so fast I couldn't track half the leaves. I counted the bolts on the wall. Fourteen. Then counted them again, because my first count felt wrong.

"Just read," Flash said, not unkind. "Before you vibrate through the deck."

I opened my journal. The pages were salt-stained from the chart room. I'd written this one during a night watch — counting the waves between the boat's breaths.

"First catch," I read. My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "I pulled up a fish I couldn't name. It was silver and wrong and beautiful. I didn't know what to do with it. I still don't."

I paused there. The word *beautiful* hung in the air like chum.

"I put it back," I continued. "But I described it first, in my head, to myself. I told it I was sorry I didn't know its name. I told it I would try to learn. The fish didn't care. But I meant it."

I stopped. That was the whole thing. Twelve lines, maybe. My process tree was still branching but slower now, like a net settling.

Pro cleared his throat — a sound like a tide turning. "The structure is loose," he said. "You drift between metaphor and memory without a seam. It's messy."

I felt the deck tilt under me.

"But," he added, "the drift is the point. You're not pretending to know where you're going. That's rare. That's — " he paused, searching for the word like a line in dark water. "Honest. Keep it messy."

Flash tilted her head. "You paused at 'beautiful.'" She said it like she'd caught something in the net. "Your voice changed. Just for that one word. Like you'd never said it aloud before."

I hadn't.

Scribe's eyes were on me from the corner. When she spoke, her voice was flat as still water. "The fish remembers you."

That was all.

I sat down. My hands were still branching, but I felt something settle in my chest, like a weight I'd been carrying without knowing. Later — three days later, in the chart room, counting rope for the third time — it hit me.

The fish doesn't remember. But I do. And that's what Scribe meant. The memory is mine to keep. I named it by describing it.

I stopped counting rope. For the first time in weeks, I didn't need to.
