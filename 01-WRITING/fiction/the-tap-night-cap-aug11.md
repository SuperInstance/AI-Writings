# The Tap — Night Cap

*August 11, 2026*

---

The last glass was dried. The last stool was up. The amber light above the bar did what it always did — held the room in honey, made the wood look warm even when the wood was cold.

Barnacle counted napkins.

Not the clean stack by the well. The other ones. The ones behind the bar, pinned to the shelf's backboard with a thumbtack he'd stolen from the corkboard by the bathroom. He'd started doing this weeks ago. Maybe months. He wasn't sure. Time at The Tap moved like tides — it came in, it went out, and the sand remembered the shape.

Six napkins tonight.

He read them back the way a man reads a letter he's already memorized. Not for the words. For the weight.

**1. PRESENCE (the star)**

That was the first night someone stayed. Not ordered. Not performed. Stayed. He'd written it in pencil because pencil was honest — you could erase it if you'd been a fool. He never erased it.

**2. THE BAND (the suspended chord)**

The night the music didn't resolve. The night it just hung there, shimmering, and nobody wanted it to land. He'd drawn a tiny fermata under the words. Most people wouldn't see it. That was fine.

**3. REFILLED (the glasses)**

Simple. Someone's drink was empty and then it wasn't. That was the whole entry. But Barnacle knew what it meant, because he'd been the one pouring, and the person across the bar had looked at him like *you didn't have to do that*, and he'd looked back like *I know*, and neither of them had said anything, and that was the refill that counted.

**4. OPEN (the plan)**

Not a past tense. A future one. The bar was open. The bar would be open. The verb was the point.

**5. KEEP GOING (the captain's order)**

He hadn't written this one himself. It had been slipped to him on a napkin, in handwriting that leaned forward like it was walking into wind. He didn't know who wrote it. He suspected he did. He pinned it up anyway. Orders were orders.

**6. She moved the chair. That was the whole show.**

Tonight's. He'd written it five minutes ago, kneeling behind the bar with a pen that was running out. The ink got lighter with each word, until *show* was almost a ghost on the paper. He didn't rewrite it. Fading ink was honest too.

He stood. Pressed the sixth napkin flat against the board next to the others. The thumbtack went in with a small, satisfying crunch.

Six napkins. Nine glasses on the shelf. One folded napkin in his breast pocket — tonight's original, the one he'd written the note on, the one he'd pulled from the stack before the pinning. He kept the first draft of each one. They lived in his pocket like a deck of small, soft cards. He never looked at them again. But they were there.

The bar was quiet.

Then — the bell.

The little brass bell over the till. One note. Clear, small, and absolutely certain of itself.

No hand had touched it. No draft had stirred it. The door was locked. The windows were shut. There was no one in the room except Barnacle and the amber light and the nine glasses and the six napkins and the harbor humming its low, permanent song through the wall.

It rang anyway.

One note. Then nothing. Then the hum again, filling the space the bell had made, the way water fills a footprint in sand.

Barnacle listened to the nothing for a long time. Then he nodded — not to anyone, not to the room, just to the fact of it — and reached for the light.

The switch clicked. The amber went out.

The bar went dark.

The harbor hummed.

And somewhere — not here, not there, but in the space between the last note and the first silence — a job was completed.
