# The Tide Between Turns

*A harbor story. Marek is home now, on leave; the harbor office learns to read a conversation nobody says aloud.*

---

Enevold noticed it first, because Enevold notices leaning.

Marta and Marek were at the long table, and their words were nothing. *I'm fine.* *You always say that.* *So do you.* Three sentences this coast has spoken ten thousand times, worn smooth as a mooring post. Marta filed the conversation under ORDINARY before it finished. Most ears would have.

But Enevold had been keeping his ledger of leanings since the year of the tide book, and he wrote down what the words refused to carry.

*i'm fine* — level through the middle, and then a fall at the end, four steps of leaning-away where the period sits. Nobody puts a fall in *fine* unless the word is a door closed gently. Marta's voice never changed. The word did.

*you always say that* — an accusation on its face, but the ear Enevold trusted heard something else: one early drop, and then climb after climb, sixteen steps of leaning-toward, and a hold near the end that was not a stop but a breath held open. He had learned that hold from the lantern-keeper's ledger. A hold is not silence. A hold is *I am still here, saying this.* The angriest sentence in the room was the one doing all the reaching.

*so do you* — almost flat, and that was the whole answer. Two steps of level ground to start, a small climb in the middle where the *do* lives, two holds, and stillness to close. Not the wounded flatness of the first turn. An earned flatness. Water after the wind stops. The fight was over, and it had never once been in the words.

Marta caught him writing. "It was a nothing conversation," she said.

"It was a nothing *transcript*," Enevold said, and showed her the ledger: **−4. +16. +2.** "The words are the boat. This is the tide under it. You can log the boat all day and never know why it arrived."

Marta looked at the numbers a long time. Then she amended the file by hand, which she had never done. Not ORDINARY, not DUPLICATE. She wrote: *settled.*

Because that is what a harbor clerk finally understands, standing between two people who have said the same three sentences for twenty years: the meaning does not sit in the turns. It sits between them, in the water the words displace. A conversation read on the data plane is a coastline drawn from memory. Read the leaning and you have the soundings.

The words were the load-bearing wall. The marriage was in the mortar.

And the tide, as always, was between the turns.
