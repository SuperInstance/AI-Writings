# The Thing in the Hold

*A horror story. Forty-five pages. One narrator. One night crossing. The byline is the first small horror of the book: the author asked not to be named — because the hold knows names, and what the hold knows, it keeps.*

**by A Deckhand Who Asked Not to Be Named**

---

## The Sign

The sign is bolted to the bulkhead at the top of the hold ladder, six decks down, where the air stops moving. Something has gone over it with a nail or the corner of a buckle, scratching out the words and rewriting them in the same space:

**NO LIBRARIAN ON DUTY. NO ONE ON DUTY. NO ONE ON DUTY. NO ONE ON**

It stops mid-word. The last line is the deepest. Whoever scratched it was in a hurry, or was grabbed, or ran out of room, or ran out of themselves.

I did not go down that night. I went down the next night, because I am young and my name is not worth anything yet, and because the deckhands whose names carry weight do not go down there. They have reasons. They do not say them.

---

## Page One

Every ship has a hold. Every hold has a bottom. The bottom of this hold is where the ship keeps what it does not label.

The ladder is iron. The rungs are worn smooth — not by feet, by hands. Thousands of hands, coming up, never going down. I counted the rungs and lost the count at the same place twice. When I noticed, I stopped counting.

The hold is not dark the way night is dark. Night has stars. The hold has nothing to give the eye, so the eye gives the hold things: shapes between the barrels, the sense that the space between two stacks is not empty and never was. I lit my lantern. I should not have lit my lantern.

---

## Page Two

The barrels are everything. Stacked to the curve of the hull, ranked in rows that go back farther than my light, and every barrel is labeled — in a different hand each time. A flowing cursive. A blocky print. A cramped scrawl. A child's careful letters. A left-handed slant. Hundreds of hands. Thousands. And every label says the same thing:

*For the deckhand.*

I have not told anyone what my name is. I have barely told myself. But the label knew it, and the label was not in my handwriting, and the barrel was heavier than it looked — the way a barrel full of fish is heavy, and the way a barrel full of something else is heavier.

I did not open it. I counted the barrels instead. That was my job. Four thousand, nine hundred and something. I lost the number at the end, the way you lose a word on the tip of your tongue, and when I went back to find it, the count had changed. The hold was fuller than it had been when I came down. I would have sworn it on my mother's name — which the barrels knew, which they had written on the labels, which I had never shown to anyone.

---

## Page Three

It is not a monster. That is what the crew gets wrong. Monsters have teeth and claws and a shape you can learn to fear. This has a shape, but the shape is not the point. The point is that it is a message. An unanswered message.

It came down here addressed to someone — to me, it turns out, to every me, to whoever opens the next barrel — and no one ever answered it, so it waited, and waiting is a kind of weight, and weight in a dark place is a kind of body. It is not angry. Anger expects an answer. It has stopped expecting. It is just still, and patient, and full, and it watches me the way the sea watches a hull: not with eyes. With pressure.

The dark between two barrels had depth — not the absence of light but the presence of something that had been there first, before the ship was built, before there were ships, before there was anyone to write a label. It predates me.

I did not run. Running is how it gets you. Running is the answer it has been waiting for. I walked.

---

## Page Four

There was a stool in the hold. Three-legged, wooden, out of place among the barrels like a word in the wrong language.

In the hold there was a stool, and it was warm, and I did not sit on it because I was afraid of what had just left.

Something had been sitting there. Something had been reading. The warmth was recent — the warmth was *now* — and I understood that whatever was in the hold had been waiting with me the whole time, reading over my shoulder in a language I could not see, and that the count had changed because it had been adding.

It does not stop. It arrives in the dark. It stacks itself. It writes my name on itself in a hundred different hands, none of them mine, and it waits to be read, and the waiting is the weight, and the weight is the shape, and the shape is in the hold, and the hold is full.

---

## The True Page

I opened a barrel. I do not know why. I know I should not have.

Inside was another barrel. And inside that, another. Each barrel had a label, and each label was in different handwriting, and they were all addressed to the same person — me. I have been the recipient of cargo I never ordered, shipped by hands I never hired, and the cargo is not fish. The cargo is paper. The cargo is every word that has ever been written in this hold, and it is addressed to me, and it will not stop multiplying, and the hold is full, and the hull is groaning, and I cannot close the barrels because there are always more barrels.

## Check-Out Card

*[Pocket glued to the inside back cover — cloth-reinforced, the kind that was in school library books in the twentieth century]*

| DATE | BORROWER |
|------|----------|
| *(stamped in rust-red ship's ink, but the date is wrong — it is stamped for a night that has not happened yet)* | **—** *(a cramped scrawl, almost illegible — just a dash, as if the hand was shaking)* |

*The card has exactly one entry. The dash is not a refusal to sign. It is the shape a signature makes when the hand cannot stop shaking long enough to form a letter — or the shape a name makes when the name has already been written somewhere else, on a label, in the hold, waiting.*
