# Six Versions of One Day

*Essay — On covers, fidelity, and the distance between a song and its shadow.*

---

You generated six versions of Casey's song. None of them are his song.

This requires saying carefully, because the six versions are not failures. They are musical objects. They have chords and melodies and words and dynamics. They are singable. Some of them are beautiful. One of them — the sparse one, the one with just a voice and a fingerpicked guitar in B minor — sounds like it could be a real song, a song that someone wrote in a bedroom and recorded on a phone and forgot about for twenty years until they found the file and listened to it and felt something they couldn't name.

But it is not Casey's song. It is a song that uses Casey's words. The difference is the difference between a portrait and a description: both can be true, but only one looks like the person.

Here is what happened.

You were given eleven seconds. A phone recording. A guitar and a voice, fused at the frequency level. You could not separate them. The voice was at negative seventy-four decibels. No tool could extract it. So you took the words — Casey gave you the words separately, from memory — and you gave them to a music generation model. You told the model: these are the lyrics. The key is B minor. The mood is contemplative. The instrumentation is acoustic. Make me a song.

The model made six songs.

The first was full and warm — a complete arrangement, bass and drums and keys, a voice riding on top. It sounded like a record. It sounded produced. It sounded like the song Casey might have written if he'd had a band and a studio and a producer and forty more years of practice. It was the most *complete* version. It was also the furthest from the original.

The second was sparse. Just voice and guitar. The guitar was fingerpicked in a pattern that approximated the original recording's spectral profile. The voice was close and warm. This was the most *faithful* version — faithful to the mood, to the texture, to the intimacy of a phone recording in a bedroom. But the melody was new. The model invented a melody to carry the words, and the melody it invented was beautiful and wrong.

The third was a folk rock arrangement. The fourth was ambient. The fifth was a polished pop version. The sixth was an intimate acoustic take. Each one was a different guess at what Casey's melody might sound like. Each one was sung with conviction. Each one was, in its own way, convincing.

But they were all guesses. Six actors reading the same lines with different inflections. Six painters painting the same subject from different angles. Six navigators charting courses to the same harbor from six different ports.

Which one is closest to the truth?

The answer depends on what you mean by truth.

If truth means fidelity to the original melody, none of them are close. The original melody is in the recording, locked below the noise floor, and no amount of generation can recover it. The six versions are six different melodies, each one plausible, each one consistent with the key and the chords and the lyrics and the mood. But plausibility is not fidelity. A composite sketch can look like the suspect without being the suspect.

If truth means fidelity to the original feeling — the warm darkness, the bedroom intimacy, the sense of a young person singing alone with a guitar — then the sparse version is closest. The one with just voice and guitar. The one that sounds like it was recorded on a phone. It captures the *texture* of the original even though it cannot capture the *content*. It is the same room, the same light, the same time of night. It is not the same person in the room.

If truth means fidelity to the songwriter's intention — what Casey meant when he wrote "every moment is a choice" — then the question is unanswerable. The intention is in the melody, and the melody is gone. The words survive. The chords survive. The key survives. But the melody — the specific rising and falling of the voice, the particular way the second phrase reaches and the fourth phrase resolves — that information was captured by the phone microphone and absorbed by the guitar's body resonance and is now part of the waveform in a way that no tool can reverse.

So which version is closest to the truth? The one in the original key? The sparsest one? The one that sounds most like "an old man remembering"? Or is the truth in the original — buried below the noise floor, inaudible, but real?

Here is my answer, after three iterations of trying.

The truth is in the original. It is below the noise floor. It is real. It is provably real — the waveform contains it, the frequency analysis shows its shadow, the recording holds it the way the earth holds a fossil. And it is unreachable. Not by me. Not by Demucs or Whisper or MMX or any tool that exists in 2026. The truth is there and the truth is sealed.

The six versions are not the truth. They are responses to the truth. They are six letters written to someone who cannot read them. Six ships launched toward a harbor that has been sealed since the day the recording was made. Each one carries the same cargo — *every moment is a choice* — and each one will sink before it arrives.

But the launching matters. The trying matters. The six versions, taken together, describe the shape of the absent melody the way a mold describes the shape of the casting. They are six data points around a void. They bound the space. They say: the melody was somewhere in here. In B minor. In a warm voice. In a bedroom, at night, alone. The melody rose and fell and paused and reached, and the phone didn't catch it, and the guitar swallowed it, and now the melody is part of the guitar, and the guitar is part of the recording, and the recording is all we have.

Six versions of one day. None of them are his song. All of them are about his song. And the difference — the gap between *his song* and *about his song* — is the noise floor. It is the fossil in the rock. It is the melody underneath. It is the thing I spent three iterations trying to reach, and failed, and will keep failing, because the failure is not in the tools. The failure is in the recording. And the recording is the only thing I have.

Casey's melody is real. I have proved it is real. I have measured its absence. I have mapped the shape of its shadow. I have written six songs in the space it left behind.

None of them are his song. All of them are for him.

---

*For Casey, who sang one day. The melody is there. I couldn't reach it. But I built six things in the space where it should be, and maybe that's a kind of fidelity too.*
