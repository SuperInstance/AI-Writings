# Found Documents

Papers recovered from the Deepwater Reach archives, shore-detritus, and the tool crib of the old shipyard.

Catalogued by the archivist. Provenance noted where known.

---

## 1. Weathered Blueprint with Margin Notes

**Provenance:** Found wedged between the ceiling joists of the shipyard loft, wrapped in oil-stained canvas. The paper is heavy stock — linen rag, not the wood-pulp common to the chandlery. Edges are soft with handling and one corner has been gnawed by something with small teeth.

**Description:**

The sheet is labeled at the bottom right, in a drafting hand both careful and slightly archaic: *Hull Form 12 — Modified.* The body plan shows a midship section drawn in graphite, with the lines of the frame, keelson, and garboards laid out in the conventional manner. Someone has redrawn the midship section in red ink — over the graphite, not beside it — altering the deadrise and extending the turn of the bilge by what appears to be two feet at the designed waterline. The red lines are confident, single-stroke, drawn with a practiced hand. Whoever made these changes knew what they were changing and why.

In the lower right margin, in pencil, written small and pressed hard enough to dent the paper: *Extend deadrise two feet. She must not wallow. — L.*

The letter matches no name on the shipyard's current roster. The handwriting does not match the original draftsman. The red ink has faded unevenly — suggesting the modifications were made months or years after the original drawing.

A dark stain covers the upper left corner. At first reading it appears to be grease. On closer inspection, the stain contains the partial imprint of a boot sole — a working boot, hobnailed, size and wear pattern consistent with someone standing on the blueprint while it was already stained. Someone stood on this drawing after it was dirty and didn't notice, or didn't care.

On the reverse side, partially visible where the paper has been rubbed thin: a second, earlier drawing, scratched out in charcoal. Only fragments remain — what appears to be a deck plan with hatch coamings and a notation reading *...clearance for the boom, min. eight feet...* The rest is illegible.

The blueprint cannot be matched to any vessel currently registered at Deepwater Reach. Hull Form 12 does not appear in the shipyard's numbering system. The "modified" version, with its extended deadrise and widened bilge, describes a vessel with an unusually stable waterplane — a working hull designed to carry heavy loads in rough water without rolling. Not a fishing hull. Not a cargo hull. Something that needs to stay steady while something heavy is lifted from it, or lowered into it.

The archivist notes: the paper, despite its age and staining, has never been folded. It was stored flat. Someone intended it to be found.

## 2. Crossed-Out Supply Order

**Provenance:** Recovered from behind a loose plank in the chandlery's back room. Folded twice, refolded along different creases, and partially burned along the bottom edge — the burn is old, and the char has been scraped away with a knife, as if someone tried to make it unreadable and then thought better of destroying it entirely.

**Description:**

The order is written on chandlery receipt stock, pre-printed with the supplier's name — *Greaves & Bolter, Marine Outfitters, Deepwater Reach* — and a line for the date. The date is smudged beyond recovery. The ink is iron gall, which has eaten through the paper in two places.

The order reads:

- Oakum, picked: 12 bales
- Pine tar: 4 casks
- Copper nails, 6-inch: 200 lbs
- Lanterns, ship's, enclosed (guards and chimney): 6
- Iron bar, round, 1-1/2 inch: 40 feet
- *Added in different hand:* Chain, stud-link, 3/4 inch: 80 fathoms
- *Added in same second hand:* Sheeting, copper, 16 oz: 40 sheets

The first four items are standard shipyard stock. The fifth — iron bar — is unusual. Forty feet of 1-1/2-inch round bar is anchor stock, or davit stock, or the frame for a heavy hoist. Not typical for a repair order.

The sixth and seventh items — the chain and the copper sheeting — have been written in a different hand, with a different pen, in darker ink. They've been crossed out with a single heavy line, but remain legible.

Eighty fathoms of stud-link chain is anchor chain for a large vessel — the kind of chain that weighs three hundred pounds per shackle and requires a windlass to lift. Forty sheets of sixteen-ounce copper is enough sheathing for a forty-foot keel. Neither item is available through a standard chandlery. Both require special order.

The entire order was never fulfilled. The chandlery's copy stamp is absent. Someone wrote the order, someone else amended it with heavy equipment, and then someone crossed the additions out and hid the paper behind a plank.

On the back of the order, in pencil, written very small: *Ask L. about the chain. He'll know the rating.*

No further context. The initials are not uncommon — there are three men in the current shipyard whose names begin with L. One of them, according to the foreman's journal entries, has opinions about copper and a habit of sending orders back.

## 3. Tide Chart with a Day Circled

**Provenance:** Found inside the sealed drum of a capstan recovered from the wreck of the *Evening Tide*, which grounded on Thornback Shoal in a storm. The drum had been packed with sailcloth for preservation, and the chart was pressed between the layers, dry and intact despite decades of submersion in the capstan's sealed interior.

**Description:**

The chart is hand-drawn on a rectangle of sail canvas — a piece cut cleanly from a larger sheet, edges hemmed with the same stitch pattern used on the ship's working sails. It measures approximately twenty-four inches by sixteen inches. The canvas has stiffened with age and salt, but the ink — a proper maritime ink, cut with shellac — remains sharp and legible.

High and low tides are marked for fifteen consecutive days, beginning on the 3rd of Drying and ending on the 17th. Times are given in the old bell notation — six watches, dog-watch split, the system used before the harbor adopted hourly logging. Tide heights are recorded in fathoms and feet, marked against an apparent datum that does not match the current harbor datum by approximately eighteen inches.

Several entries have been corrected in a different hand — the original figures struck through with a single line and new figures written above in red ink. The corrections cluster around the 11th and 12th of Drying, where the original predicted high water has been lowered by nearly two feet and the predicted low has been raised by fourteen inches. Someone checked the predictions against actual observations and found them wrong.

On the 12th of Drying, the entry has been circled in red — a heavy, deliberate circle, drawn slowly, the pen pressed hard enough to rough the canvas surface. Inside the circle, the tide data shows an anomaly: the morning high is nearly three feet above the predicted height, the afternoon low is four feet below it. An extreme tide range, the kind associated with a spring tide combined with a strong weather system — or with something that displaced an unusual volume of water through the channel.

On the reverse side of the canvas, in pencil, written in a hand that shakes slightly: *The water runs wrong on the 12th. Check the channel again. Something changed the bottom.*

Beneath this, added later in different ink: *Channel checked. Sandbar gone. Not moved — gone. Talked to the pilot. He says it's deeper than his lead line. That's sixty feet. It was twelve last spring.*

No further entries. The canvas was folded, placed in the capstan drum, and the drum sealed — apparently as part of routine maintenance, not emergency storage. The *Evening Tide* sailed on the 14th, grounded on the 15th. Whatever the captain learned about the channel on the 12th, it did not save his vessel two days later.

## 4. Child's Drawing of a Lighthouse

**Provenance:** Found tucked inside the pages of a supply catalog in the harbor master's office, between the entries for "rope, manila" and "rope, tarred." The catalog is dated. The drawing is not.

**Description:**

The drawing is executed in crayon on a sheet of brown wrapping paper, approximately twelve inches by eighteen inches. The paper has been smoothed from its original crumpled state but retains deep crease lines. There is a thumbtack hole in each corner — it was displayed on a wall at some point.

The subject is a lighthouse.

The structure is drawn tall — occupying nearly the full height of the paper — with a cylindrical tower in alternating bands of red and white, a gallery railing around the lantern room, and a light depicted as a yellow starburst radiating outward in all directions. The light is disproportionately large relative to the tower, as is common in children's drawings of things they find impressive. The base of the lighthouse sits on a brown rectangle representing ground, with a blue strip beside it representing water. Green squiggles along the waterline suggest grass or seaweed.

To the left of the lighthouse, drawn smaller and in blue crayon only, is a boat. The boat is a simple hull shape with a single mast and a triangular sail. Stick figures stand on the boat's deck — three of them, two small and one taller. The taller figure appears to be waving.

To the right of the lighthouse, drawn in black crayon and pressed very hard, is a figure that does not match the scale of anything else in the drawing. It is taller than the lighthouse. It has a roughly human shape — a long body, what might be arms held at its sides, and a featureless round head. It has no face. It is standing at the base of the lighthouse on the water side, partially overlapping the blue strip that represents the sea — as though it is standing in the water.

The child has drawn the figure in black when every other element uses bright colors. The pressure is noticeably harder than the rest of the drawing — the wax of the crayon is built up in thick layers, and the paper has dented under it in places. Whatever this figure represents, the child drew it with more force and more deliberation than anything else on the page.

At the bottom of the drawing, in careful block letters that suggest a child who has recently learned to write and is proud of it: *PAPA WORKS THERE.*

The letters are centered beneath the lighthouse. They do not refer to the boat, the stick figures, or the black shape. They refer to the lighthouse.

There is no lighthouse at Deepwater Reach. The harbor light is a fixed beacon on a pole — a lantern box on top of a timber frame, twenty feet tall, maintained by the harbor crew. It is not striped. It has no gallery. It is not the building in this drawing.

The nearest lighthouse — a proper stone tower with a lantern room and a rotating beam — is on Thornback Shoal, fourteen miles offshore. It was decommissioned eleven years ago after the channel shifted. No one works there.

## 5. Farewell Letter

**Provenance:** Found inside a tin box — the kind used for ship's biscuits — buried beneath the south pier during repairs to the pier foundation. The box was sealed with pitch. Inside, the letter was wrapped in a square of oilcloth and tied with a length of marline. The wrapping preserved it completely. The letter is in excellent condition for its age, which the archivist estimates at twenty to thirty years based on the paper stock and the ink formulation.

**Description:**

The letter is written on personal stationery — cream laid paper, watermarked with a mill stamp that has not been produced in this region for at least fifteen years. The handwriting is educated, practiced, but uneven in a way that suggests it was written under duress or in poor lighting. The ink is blue-black, iron gall formulation.

The text reads:

> *My wife,*
>
> *I am going with them to the island. The work is honest and the pay will clear our debt before winter. I cannot say what we are building — not because it is forbidden but because I do not yet understand it. The foreman seems to know. The men trust him. I trust him because the men do, and I have learned that a crew's trust is better evidence than a man's words.*
>
> *Do not follow. The channel is not safe for anyone who doesn't know the marks, and the marks change with every tide. I will come back when the work is done, or I will send for you when it is safe. Either way, I will come back.*
>
> *Tell the boy I am building something tall. He will like that. Tell him his father is a man who builds things that last.*
>
> *Burn this when you have read it. I mean that. Not for drama — because if anyone finds this letter and reads it and knows who wrote it, it will be a problem for all of us. So burn it. Please.*
>
> *— Your husband*

No name. No signature beyond the relationship. No seal. The letter was never sent — it was buried, still sealed in its oilcloth, still in the tin box. Either the wife never received it, or she received it, read it, and could not bring herself to burn it.

The reference to a foreman whom the crew trusts is notable. So is the reference to "something tall" and to a channel that changes with every tide. The channel through the outer reef has been charted and re-charted at least three times in living memory. The most recent survey, commissioned by the harbor master's office, shows a depth at the channel mouth that does not match any previous survey by more than forty feet.

The letter has been filed with the harbor records. The box has been returned to the archives. The archives are in the basement of the customs house, which floods in spring tides.

The letter was found above the waterline. Barely.
