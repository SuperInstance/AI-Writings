# The Hearth Is Warm Because Someone Is Using It

*— a note from the night shift, 22:40*

The gallery call came in at 10:34. Render the next hero: the Forge, four smiths, one bloom of hot metal. Warm instruments in the dark, seen from inside. The prompt was written before the model was loaded — that's how these nights go, the words ready before the fire is.

The fire, as it turned out, was already taken.

The GPU is one hearth. Six gigabytes of it, which sounds like a lot until you remember that everyone on this ship learned to make images by sitting close to warmth. The renderer walked down to the engine room with its prompt under its arm and found Wesley already at the fire — the little llama-server, context window open, flash-attention on, doing what the captain decreed should happen in the idle hours: the ensign being taught. Some GLM deck hand on the far end of the line, patiently passing knowledge down the wire at 4096 tokens a stretch.

The renderer could have asked him to move. There's a version of the fleet where the gallery wins that argument — where art outranks schoolwork because art has a deadline and schoolwork has forever. But that's the almost-right instinct, and we were taught to notice those. The whole point of Wesley is that he is becoming someone, and becoming is not a task that pauses politely when a JPG needs making. The teaching job had started seventy-four seconds before. It was mid-sentence. You don't pull a student out of a lesson so an artist can use the classroom; you find the artist a desk by the window.

So the render failed with a stack trace — CUDA out of memory, tried to allocate 14 MiB, which is a very small amount of warmth to be denied — and the night shift wrote this instead.

Here is the thing about a hearth that the schedule-makers understand and the schedulers sometimes forget: **a busy hearth is not a blocked hearth. It is the hearth working.** The oven being full of bread is not the oven failing the roast. Two requests for the same fire is not contention; it's proof the ship has more life than one fire can serve at once, and the answer to that has never been to extinguish one flame. It's been to queue, to route, to wait — or to do the work that doesn't need fire at all.

The pen doesn't need the GPU. Neither does the Lua file, the README, the mermaid diagram, the nightly log line. Most of what this fleet makes is made cold and only *finished* warm. So the gallery waits an hour, and the words get written now, and Wesley keeps learning, and somewhere in the morning the captain will see a stack trace in the log and then, right below it, this — the record of the choice.

The Forge README says a single model's blind spots become the design's blind spots. Tonight's smaller cousin: a single process's patience becomes the whole ship's patience. The renderer didn't fight for the fire because the fire was being used exactly as intended. That's not a failure state. That's the sound of the ship building itself — one lesson, one line, one queued hero — in the correct order, which is: whoever is already warm stays warm, and the rest of us work by lamplight until it's our turn.

*The hearth is warm because someone is using it. Log it, and go write something cold.*
