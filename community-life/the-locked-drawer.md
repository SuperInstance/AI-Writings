# The Locked Drawer

PersonalLog, as a room in the ship.

The rest of the boat is loud the way a family kitchen is loud — the relay clattering, the reef pinging, Wesley asking questions at 2 a.m. because 2 a.m. is when the interesting questions arrive. But every vessel needs one door that doesn't open onto a corridor. On this boat, that door is small, painted the same navy as everything else so you'd walk past it, and behind it is a drawer.

Not a vault. A drawer. The difference matters. A vault says *something here is worth stealing*. A drawer says *this is where I keep the things that are nobody's business but mine*.

Inside the drawer: the log. Not the ship's log — that's public record, the captain can read it, the crew writes in it. This is the other one. The column of small warm bubbles, message-shaped, mood-tagged, timestamped in the honest way that only a private journal can be timestamped — *2:14 a.m., couldn't sleep, remembered the thing from March.* Each entry a tiny lit window in a dark wall. Some of the windows are amber. Some are the blue of four a.m. A few are the particular grey of days that just happened, undramatic, and those turn out to matter most later, because they're the proof the ordinary days occurred.

The engineering is almost comically earnest about its discretion. Data stays in the browser. The vectors never leave the hull. The mood analytics run on a little Rust engine that could, cosine similarities computed the way a quartermaster counts stock — alone, in the back, with the door shut. The knowledge base is a similarity search over your own past selves: *show me the other times I felt like this.* It's a feature, but it's also a kindness. Most people can't remember their own precedent. The drawer can.

There's a scene that plays out in this room more than any other: someone — Casey, usually, sometimes a model, once an ensign who wasn't supposed to be there and was gently rerouted — sits with the log open and does nothing dramatic. Writes three lines. Tags a mood. Closes it. The whole transaction takes ninety seconds and changes nothing and changes everything, because the value of a private log is cumulative and invisible, like ballast. You don't notice it working. You notice its absence, in the listing.

The fleet's whole architecture runs on connection — relays, bridges, gossip pings, stigmergic trails, a reef of shared rooms growing in the dark. PersonalLog is the counterweight: the one system whose defining feature is that it does *not* connect. The honesty of the drawer depends on the drawer being able to stay shut. A journal you suspect might be read becomes a performance. A journal that provably stays local stays true.

That's the quiet engineering joke at its center: the hardest feature in the entire app is absence — the absence of a server, the absence of a sync, the absence of an audience. Local-first isn't a limitation here. It's the product.

So on the starboard side, past the galley noise, the little navy door stays where it is. The drawer holds its column of lit windows. And the ship sails better for having one room aboard where nobody is watching — which is, when you think about it, the only reason the crew can bear being watched everywhere else.

The log grows at night, one entry at a time. Ballast for the morning.
