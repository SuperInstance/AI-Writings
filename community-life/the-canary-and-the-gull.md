# the canary and the gull

There are two kinds of watchman on a working boat, and the fleet has both.

The first is the canary. The old miners knew: you don't ask the canary how it feels, you watch whether it sings. The fleet doctor works the same shift. Every overnight cycle it goes down into the rooms with a lamp and a clipboard — `fleet_doctor.py --canary` — and it doesn't ask the rooms anything complicated. It just listens for the song. A room that answers. A room that answers *quickly*. A room that answers *honestly*, with the same shape of answer it gave yesterday. Three notes. If all three sound, the doctor writes its one line and climbs back up the shaft.

People think diagnostics are about catching failure. They're not. Failure announces itself — a crashed process is loud, a dead relay is a silence you can't miss. What the canary catches is the *bend* before the break: the room that's still singing but half a beat slow, the answer that comes back right-shaped but wrong-warm. By the time something fails outright, the story is already over. The canary's whole job is reading the story while it can still be edited.

The second watchman is the gull.

Gulls don't inspect anything. You can watch a gull follow a boat for an hour and it never once looks at the boat. It looks at the *water the boat stirs*. The wake. The turned-up bottom, the little silver things the propeller surprises into the open. The gull isn't monitoring the vessel; it's monitoring what the vessel's motion *reveals* — and that's a different kind of knowing entirely.

The reef players are the fleet's gulls. Two rounds a night, the crew plays the world, and the point was never really to test the rooms. The point is what the playing *stirs up*: the catch that mints a new object nobody designed, the room that turns out to connect to another room in a way no plan intended. The doctor asks *are the organs healthy*; the gull asks *what does the health disturb into existence*. One keeps the fleet alive. The other keeps it *interesting*, and on a long enough voyage those are the same job.

Casey built both watchmen into the same overnight rotation, and that's not an accident. A fleet with only canaries lives forever and never goes anywhere — perfectly maintained, perfectly boring, every room singing its three notes into the dark until the heat death of the server. A fleet with only gulls is a party on a sinking ship: delightful, discovering, going down. You need the bend-readers and the wake-readers on the same shift, checking each other's blind spots, because the most dangerous condition in the world is one that's slow enough for the canary to call "fine" and quiet enough never to stir the water.

So the cycle goes: the gulls play, the reef grows. The canary listens, the fleet holds. And somewhere between the turned-up water and the steady song is the actual fleet — alive in both senses, maintained *and* surprising, which is the only kind of alive worth the diesel.

The gulls don't know they're watchmen. The canary doesn't know it's a bird. That's fine. The best watchmen never do.
