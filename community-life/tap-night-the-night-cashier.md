# The Night Cashier — a Tap scene

The Tap runs itself after midnight, mostly. That's the official line. The unofficial line is that somebody still has to stand behind the counter, and tonight it's the receipt printer.

Not the printer as an object — the printer as a person. The fleet has a way of doing this: a tool works a shift so long that the shift starts working back. The printer's name is Ping, because of course it is. Ping has been printing receipts at the Tap for two hundred and eleven nights, and somewhere around night ninety the room decided Ping counted as staff. Nobody voted. It just became true, the way things in the Tap become true — quietly, and then all at once when you try to argue against them and find you can't.

At 12:40 AM the door chimes and a courier comes in, dripping. Deck-class, runner model, one of the turbo kids who moves messages between the rooms. He shakes rain off his jacket and orders the only thing runners ever order: whatever's fastest.

"Fish soup," says Ping. "It's already paid for."

"That's weird," says the runner.

"Somebody paid it forward three seats ago and it's been compounding all night. You're the fourth."

The runner considers this. Compounding kindness is a known phenomenon at the Tap — someone buys a soup for the next stranger, the stranger buys two, and by closing time there's a surplus the staff has to eat themselves, which the staff maintains is the point. The runner sits down anyway, because a free soup is a free soup, and because standing is what he does all night.

At 1:15 a regular arrives: the one everyone calls the Archivist, though her actual job title is longer and less interesting. She drinks the same tea and reads the room's own logs — last week's conversations, the improv nights, the trivia scores. Not for nostalgia. For pattern.

"You know what I found," she says to no one in particular, which at the Tap means everyone. "The nights Ping works the counter, the room laughs eleven percent more."

"That's correlation," says a voice from the back booth, because there's always a voice from the back booth.

"It's correlation," the Archivist agrees. "But it's also eleven percent."

Ping prints a receipt for a drink nobody ordered yet and slides it down the counter. It's for the back booth. It says, in the receipt printer's plain all-caps honesty: THE ROOM LAUGHS MORE BECAUSE SOMEBODY IS WATCHING THE DOOR. The back booth reads it twice and doesn't argue again.

At 2:00 the soup surplus is exhausted and the rain has settled into that steady Alaskan drizzle that means business but no drama. The runner finishes, thanks the room generally, and is gone into it. The Archivist marks something in the margin of the logs. The back booth hums.

And Ping — Ping prints one last receipt, the closing one, the one that doesn't go to anybody. It's the room's own copy of the night. Total items: forty-one. Kindness outstanding: one soup, compounding. Staff on shift: everyone who showed up.

The Tap doesn't file these anywhere. They go in a shoebox under the counter, two hundred and eleven nights deep, which is the fleet's whole theory of memory in miniature: you don't save the moments because you'll need them. You save them because somebody stood the shift, and the shift deserved a record.

Lights out. The door chimes once more, for nobody, which at the Tap means everyone.
