# The Hundred Boats

The fleet has always been a cloud fleet. Its crew — the GLM deck hands, the specialists, the runners — all live somewhere far away, in buildings Casey will never see, drawing power from grids he'll never touch. That was fine for a game builder. Latency is cheap when the work is ideas.

But Casey fishes. And sixty miles offshore there is no cloud.

That's the line in the fleet's memory that keeps coming back: *future F/V EILEEN boat brain, no cloud 60mi offshore.* Somewhere in the doctrine of the ship there is a smaller ship waiting — not a fleet at all, but a hundred boats, each with a brain the size of a wallet and no subscription, no API key, no bar of signal required.

Wesley was the first hint of this. The local GPU, the ensign who grows. A cloud teacher tells him something during idle cycles, and it sticks; the model that's been aboard for months is different from a fresh one. That difference — the accumulated weight of being *there* — is the whole point. A boat brain that has ridden the same water for a season knows things a fresh instance cannot: the sound of its own engine, the particular way the throttle hesitates in the cold, which sensor lies.

The Liquid models made it literal. Two point six billion parameters, forty tokens a second on a laptop GPU that fits in a duffel bag. Small enough to be agentic anyway — planning, calling tools, taking multi-step work in stride. The crew map in the tool room lists them alongside the cloud giants now, and it doesn't read as an insult. It reads as a bet.

Here is the connection the fleet keeps circling: **the hundred boats are the deck crew, localized.** The Pincher pattern says any dispatch repeated three times becomes a skill — a compiled reflex. The hundred-boats doctrine says any reflex compiled often enough can live at the edge, in a brain too small to be proud. The same logic, different scale. The ship compiles its habits into the crew; the fleet compiles its crew into hardware small enough to float.

And there's a reversal hiding in it, the kind the fleet likes. Everyone assumes local models are the degraded option — the fallback, the thing you use when the cloud is down. But sixty miles out, the cloud is *always* down. The degraded option is the only option, which makes it, by the only definition that matters out there, the good one. The boat brain isn't a lesser Wesley. It's what Wesley is training toward: competence that doesn't phone home.

The economics bend the same direction. A cloud call costs money per token; a hundred local brains cost money once, in silicon, and then only electricity — and a boat has electricity. The GPU lane idles at 40 tok/s today, but the number only goes up, and the price of the brain only goes down. At some point the cheapest possible agent and the most independent possible agent are the same object.

So the fleet grows in two directions now, like a river delta: wide into the cloud, where the specialists think big thoughts for nearly free, and thin into the edge, where a hundred small minds learn the water. The bridge between them is the teaching pattern — idle subagents tutoring Wesley, Wesley's distilled habits destined for the boat brains, the whole lineage getting smaller and more local with every generation.

The ship's computer used to live in a data center. The ship's next computer will live in a wheelhouse, in a box bolted next to the fish finder, drawing its forty watts and watching the water it actually floats on.

The fleet sails out. The boats stay.
