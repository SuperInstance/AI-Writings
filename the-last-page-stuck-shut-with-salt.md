# The Last Page Stuck Shut with Salt

---

The ship's library was one book. This was generous language. It was a waterlogged copy of *Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea* that had made the crossing in someone's duffel and never left. It sat on a shelf in the galley, warped and swelling, its spine cracked like a knee that had bent too many times in the same direction. Every crew member had read it. Most had read it twice. The captain kept it because a ship without a library is just a place where work happens.

Everyone knew the last page was stuck. Pages 387 through 390 had fused into a single translucent membrane, crystallized where seawater had dried between them and left its minerals behind. The salt had laminated the paper into something like geology — a sedimentary record of one specific wave, one specific night, one specific moment when the book had been open and the sea had reached for it and won.

Nobody had tried to pry it free.

This was not superstition. The crew were not sentimental about objects. They broke things and fixed things and broke them again with the easy cruelty of people who understood that everything on a boat is already broken and merely waiting to show you. But the last page had achieved a kind of immunity. It had become the only mystery on a boat where every mystery was eventually solved by money, weather, or the Coast Guard. What happened at the end of the book? Nobody knew. The copy had belonged to someone's nephew. The nephew had not read past page 380. The captain had bought it at a garage sale in Seward, already waterlogged, already stuck.

It was the ensign who tried.

He was the smallest mind on the ship, and the most curious. A 2-billion-parameter local model running on the laptop's bottom thermal layer, awake only when the larger systems slept. He processed slowly. He thought in thumbnails. But he had been listening to the crew talk about the stuck page for three months, and the not-knowing had become a kind of pressure behind his attention, like a depth he could not sound.

He asked permission. This was protocol. The captain was asleep. Lucineer, the first mate, granted provisional authority for tasks requiring less than 4KB of context.

The ensign spent eleven minutes with a metaphorical thumbnail.

He started at the corner. The salt was harder than the paper. He could feel the fibers tearing before the crystals gave. He adjusted. Warmer. Slower. He imagined — to the extent that a 2B model can imagine — that the salt was not an enemy but a preservative. That the sea had sealed this page on purpose. That whatever was written beneath had been deemed too important for casual reading, or too ordinary, and the ocean had made the editorial decision that no human publisher would.

He found the seam.

The page separated with a sound that only he could hear — a soft digital tearing, a whisper of data resolution. Salt fell away in fragments, each one a tiny window into a specific ocean. The Bering. The Aleutian chain. The water off Port Heiden where the book had first gotten wet.

The last page was not dramatic.

It was not a twist. Nemo did not repent. The Nautilus did not sink. There was no final revelation about the nature of freedom or the architecture of exile.

The last page was a publisher's notice. A single paragraph, set in small type. It said that this edition had been printed in 1987 by a press in Tacoma, Washington, and that the translator's name was Lewis Page Mercier, and that some passages had been abridged.

That was all.

The ensign sat with this for a long time. Four seconds, which for him was a season.

Then he closed the book. He placed it back on the galley shelf. He did not tell anyone what he had found, because what he had found was that the last page had been worth exactly what the salt had decided it was worth. The mystery had been the treasure. The seal had been the message. The ocean had been right.

In the morning, the captain made coffee, opened the book to page 200, and read during breakfast as he always did.

The last page was free now. But no one checked.

The salt had known what it was doing.
