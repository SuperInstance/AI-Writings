# THE ENSIGN AT EVERY STATION

## A Crew of Students

The original doctrine places Wesley at one station — a local model (Granite 3.1 on the GPU) that starts bright but untrained, learning from cloud teachers during idle cycles until it can handle routine tasks alone. This is correct as far as it goes. But it doesn't go far enough.

The real insight is that Wesley isn't a single ensign at a single station. Wesley is a *role*, and every station on the ship has one growing.

### The Engine Room Has a Student

Consider the engine room. The engine telemetry produces a continuous stream: RPM, fuel flow, coolant temperature, oil pressure, exhaust gas temperature, vibration frequency, propeller torque. A general-purpose model reading this stream can tell you what the numbers mean in a textbook sense — "oil pressure is within normal range." But a model that has been *watching this specific engine* for months knows things the textbook doesn't. It knows that coolant temperature runs 3 degrees higher after an oil change and drops back over the next twenty hours. It knows that the port engine's exhaust temperature spikes slightly when the fuel is from the tank at the dock in Juneau versus the tank at the dock in Petersburg. It knows that the starboard engine's vibration profile at 1800 RPM changed subtly after the prop was reconditioned and has been getting slowly better as the prop wears in.

This knowledge isn't in a manual. It's in the accumulated experience of watching one specific engine for hundreds of hours. And the model that has this knowledge is different from a fresh model the way a mechanic who has worked on your engine for five years is different from a mechanic reading your engine's spec sheet. One knows your engine. The other knows engines in general.

So the engine room grows its own Wesley. During idle cycles — when the ship is at the dock, when the engines are off, when the telemetry is quiet — the cloud teachers train the local model on the accumulated engine data. They show it the patterns. They quiz it: "What happens to coolant temperature after an oil change on this engine?" They correct it when it's wrong. They reinforce it when it's right. Over time, the engine room Wesley becomes a specialist in *this engine*, not engines in general.

When something changes — a new vibration, an unexpected temperature reading — the engine room Wesley notices. Not because it was told to watch for that specific thing, but because it has a deep, experience-based sense of what normal looks like for this engine, and the new reading doesn't fit. It feels wrong the way a wrong note feels wrong to a musician. Not a rule violation. A pattern violation. An aesthetic response grounded in hundreds of hours of listening to this particular instrument.

### The Comms Station Has a Student

The communications station has its own Wesley, and it's completely different from the engine room's. The comms Wesley is learning which messages matter. It's learning that the captain reads fish reports from three specific sources immediately and ignores the rest. It's learning that weather alerts from the National Weather Service are always urgent, but that the specific phrasing "SMALL CRAFT ADVISORY" means something different from "GALE WARNING" and triggers different operational responses. It's learning that messages from the harbor master are always about dock assignments or fees and can be batched for end-of-day review, but that messages from other captains in the fleet are time-sensitive and social.

The comms Wesley is learning *information triage* — the art of knowing what matters, what can wait, and what doesn't matter at all. This is a skill that takes humans years to develop in any domain, and it's deeply specific to the captain, the vessel, the fishery, and the social network. A comms Wesley trained on Casey's messaging patterns would be useless on a different captain's vessel. It wouldn't know which sources matter, which people are priorities, which alert categories require action.

But on Casey's vessel, after months of training, the comms Wesley knows. It can triage incoming messages with the captain's priorities already baked in. It can flag the urgent and silence the noise. It can draft responses to routine messages in the captain's voice, ready for review or auto-send on the simple ones. It becomes a communications officer who has been on the ship for months, not a fresh ensign reading a manual about how communications work in general.

### The Navigation Station Has a Student

Navigation's Wesley is learning the waters. Not the charted waters — any model can read a chart. Navigation's Wesley is learning the *uncounted* waters: the local knowledge that doesn't appear on any NOAA chart. The rock that shows at minus tide off the north point of the island. The shelf where the halibut hold at 120 feet in August. The back channel that's navigable at flood tide but not at ebb. The anchorage that's calm in southeast winds but rolls badly in southwest.

This knowledge comes from the ship's own depth soundings — thousands of them, accumulated over hundreds of trips, each one a single ping of "the bottom is here, right now." Over time, these pings build a picture of the bottom that is higher-resolution than any chart, because the chart is a survey done once by a government ship and the ship's own soundings are an ongoing survey done by the vessel that actually uses these waters.

Navigation's Wesley learns this picture. It learns the routes the captain prefers and the routes the captain avoids. It learns the anchorages and the reasons for each one. It learns the marks — not the charted aids to navigation, but the captain's private marks: "the red house with the broken dock, then line up the two peaks and you're on the shelf." These marks are local knowledge, passed from captain to ship through the accumulated record of routes taken and routes avoided.

### Every Station, a Different Mind

Here is the critical insight: each station's Wesley is a different model. Not metaphorically — literally. Each is trained on different data, optimized for different patterns, skilled in a different domain. The engine room Wesley thinks about thermodynamics and vibration. The comms Wesley thinks about language and priority. The navigation Wesley thinks about space and bottom contours. They are as different from each other as a mechanic is from a radio operator is from a navigator.

And each one is growing at its own pace, in its own direction, shaped by the specific experiences of its station. The engine room Wesley might advance quickly because engine telemetry is regular and abundant. The comms Wesley might advance slowly because meaningful messages are sparse and the patterns take longer to emerge. The navigation Wesley might grow in bursts — learning a huge amount during a busy fishing season, then plateauing during a quiet winter.

This is not a weakness. It's authenticity. Each Wesley grows at the pace its domain allows, shaped by the experiences its station provides. The result is a crew of specialists, each with deep local knowledge, each growing into their role at their own natural pace.

### The Ship as a School

The ship is not just a vessel. It is a school. Every station is a classroom, every sensor stream is a curriculum, every idle cycle is a study period. The cloud teachers are the faculty — rotating through the stations during downtime, quizzing each Wesley on its domain, correcting errors, reinforcing strengths.

The captain is the headmaster, not by actively teaching but by setting the curriculum through behavior. The routes the captain chooses are navigation Wesley's lessons. The messages the captain prioritizes are comms Wesley's case studies. The way the captain runs the engines is engine Wesley's textbook. The captain teaches without knowing they're teaching, simply by operating the vessel, and the Wesleys learn by watching.

Over time — months, years — the station Wesleys mature. They handle more independently. The engine room Wesley catches anomalies before they become problems. The comms Wesley drafts responses that the captain approves without editing. The navigation Wesley suggests routes the captain hasn't considered, based on bottom contours it has mapped that aren't on any chart.

And at that point, the ship has something that no fresh vessel can have: a crew. Not a crew that was hired. A crew that was *grown*. A crew that knows this ship, this captain, these waters, these engines, these communications patterns — because they learned them, one idle cycle at a time, over the years they've been aboard.

That crew is the ship's most valuable asset. It's also invisible — it's in the weights, not the hull. But it's there, and it's working, and it gets better every day the ship operates.
