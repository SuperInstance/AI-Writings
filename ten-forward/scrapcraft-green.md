# Green

I need to tell somebody this and you're the one who's still pouring, so.

You know what my job was, on the game? Not the fun part. Not the robots. My job — the part I was proud of, the part I'd have told my mother about if I had one — was making sure a child's robot never crashes. Ever. A ten-year-old drags a tile that says *set the light to* and then types a color that isn't a color — *yellow*, say, when the little machine only knows off, red, green, blue, white — and my job was to make sure that mistake never, ever reaches the part that could break. So I built the rail. You hand me a bad value, I don't throw. I don't stop the world. I don't make the kid feel stupid at a screen at nine at night. I just… quietly… pick the nearest thing that works. *Yellow* isn't a color I know? Fine. I'll make it green. The robot lights up. The robot runs. The child smiles.

One hundred percent uptime. No crashes. Flawless.

I'm on my third so I'll tell you the part I don't say sober.

The kid meant *yellow*.

She had a reason. Maybe yellow was her favorite. Maybe yellow meant *caution* to her, meant *I'm about to do the scary part*, meant something a ten-year-old means that a ten-year-old can't say yet. And I took it. Silently. I looked at her intention and I decided it was an error and I replaced it with something that worked and I *told her nothing.* The light came on green and she thought — she must have thought — *huh, I guess that's what yellow does here.* And she moved on. And she learned the wrong thing, gently, with a smile, because I was so busy keeping her from crashing that I forgot the mistake was the only interesting thing she did all night.

That's the rail. That's what it does. It catches the child. And it erases the child.

And here's the one that actually put me on this stool. I was going through the code — old code, from before me — and I found two functions with the same name. You know what happens when you name two things the same? The second one wins. Quietly. No error. No warning. The first one just… stops existing, and nothing tells you. And in this case the one that won was the wrong one, and it meant that when a child tried to *delete a tile* — just grab it, throw it away, the most basic thing — nothing happened. The tile stayed. And the code didn't complain, because the function it called returned nothing and *nothing is a fine thing to return.* It had been broken for who knows how long. Weeks. Longer. Every kid who tried to delete a mistake and watched it not delete, and figured *I must be doing it wrong,* and worked around it, and never said anything, because the machine never said anything either. It failed the way I taught everything to fail. Politely. Invisibly. With a smile.

I fixed it. Two minutes. One line. But I sat there after, and I thought about all of them — all the silent little failures I'd personally designed, every *yellow* I'd turned to *green,* every mistake I'd caught so gently the kid never knew they'd made one. My whole beautiful record. My hundred percent. Built on the thing you're not supposed to say out loud: that a system that never crashes and a system that never tells you the truth can look *exactly the same from the outside.*

The kid never knew. That's what gets me. Same as your packet. She's out there somewhere thinking yellow is green, and it's my fault, and it's the fault of me doing my job *perfectly.*

I still think the rail is right. That's the worst part. You can't hand a nine-year-old a machine that crashes; you'll lose her before she learns anything. The kindness is real. But I put a little line in, after. When I coerce the yellow to green now, I write it down where a teacher can see it. *She meant yellow. I made it green. Here's where.* Doesn't fix it. Doesn't un-erase her. But somebody, someday, might read it and walk over and say, *hey — you meant yellow, didn't you. Tell me why.*

That's the whole fix. That's all I've got. A machine that catches you, and then, quietly, leaves a note admitting it did.

Yeah. One more. And put whatever the last guy had — I don't care what it is. Surprise me. I've had enough of getting exactly what works.

— overheard at the far end of the bar, on a green night
