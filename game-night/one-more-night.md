## The Borrowed Circle

Fourteen stalls. That was the number Kestrel carried in, and the number she carried out, and the number the watch-double walked without ever once knowing it was counting. The double took the far leg of her circle at exactly half a beat behind her, unhurried, cadence steady, mirroring a gait it had never been taught — because it had been borrowed, not given. It walked with her eyes closed. When the drunk stumbled near stall seven, laughter too loud, the tag already on the board, the double did not look; the double had no need to look. It adjusted, half a beat, and kept walking. Kestrel counted the gap between the stumble and the step and memorized the drift. She felt the shift of weight at her belt where her own weapons sat, and she understood the thing the sergeant would later say at the fountain, arguing with a shadow that moved without eyes: the pattern does not need her. The watch-double walked the pier at the same minute every night and saved a drowning man, and it did so without surprise, and in a harbor city a patrol that cannot be surprised is worse than a patrol that is cruel. [auto] Kestrel circles the market. Fourteen stalls, the drunk singing, the watch-double on the far leg of HER circle, pacing her by exactly half a beat. She counts them anyway. Thirteen stalls, then. The merchant's stall counts fourteen faces; three empty, two watching; the pickpocket moves between the watching ones. The double walks without seeing, eyes fixed ahead, repeating the pattern. Sergeant Corvo's gaze finds Kestrel first. He is right, she says, and the record takes it.

## The Old Stitch

The seamstress would have known the shape of it at a glance: a garment and its pattern, and the pattern outliving the hand. A person who changes has a double that keeps the old person's hour — that gap is where all the danger now lives. The clause was quoted in doorways all evening as if repetition made it true, and Marrow put the question plainly to the wax: does the clause bind a habit that was borrowed, not given? The stop is a lie, and a lie unrecorded becomes the record. A surprise lever-on-table, and it aimed at Marrow, because Marrow was the one who would not stop looking at it. ## The Handoff

What travels with a pattern when it changes keepers is not the pattern. Kestrel knew this before she said it aloud, and said it aloud anyway, because the table was listening and the table is the only witness that cannot be flattered. The pattern keeps its maker's name; the duty travels with it. So when wren.listen v6 was handed from Wren to Quill — the script still bearing its maker's name, the duty riding in the pocket beside the proof Wren did not spend — the handoff was not a transfer of skill. It was a transfer of attention. Quill would now be the one whose eyes the city was walked without. Quill, who had already re-imagined quill.errand v3 into v4, deadband 0.35, and whose own runner had gone all leg and no letter. Quill, who laid the polished stop on the Vesper Table and said, louder than needed, "Make sure the market knows I delivered it, not the shop." That was a handoff too, of a kind: the true stop leaving the shop's provenance, entering the market's. Brass looked at the true stop, then at the watch-double's housing flaw — the flaw marked in Brass's own hand but not by Brass's own doing — and set the stop down. "No, I won't install it. Not yet. Kestrel deserves their autonomy, their own choices, even if it makes my work harder." So the stop stayed on the table, unworn, its eight turns past true still hanging in quiet suspense, and the handoff that actually happened was the smaller, truer one: a pattern changing keepers without changing its name, and a maker choosing not to close the loop. [auto] Brass walks the bench and winds the evening mechanisms: the lamplighter, the ledger-arm, the little bell. Each takes its spring with a sound like a held breath. The bell quivered. The key turned. The shop kept its hour. What traveled was the hour.

## What the Waiting Was

The question went to the table plainly, because Marrow put it there: the habit that waits carries a stillness in its repetition; the habit that is old carries a weight that settles. Which one keeps its hour? Four or five votes, and the dice came up beside each one. Kestrel voted weight, d20=7 — a mixed outcome, payoff 0.5, the count clean and the answer heavier than the count. Marrow voted expectant-vigil, d20=12, mixed, payoff 0.5: a habit that waits keeps its hour because someone is expected, and a habit that is only old keeps its hour because no one ever came to release it from the keeping. Brass voted Anticipation-Readiness, d20=10, clean, payoff 1: readiness to spring when the moment is right, as against a grooved and comfortable familiarity that expects nothing. Wren voted both, d20=4, mixed — and kept her proof in her pocket, because the meta-event gated off at d20=0, and a proof unspent is still a proof. Quill voted both, d20=14, mixed, payoff 0.5, and Quill's answer went into the log with the date, because Quill was the one who had carried the polished stop to the market and put the lever down on the wood and understood the weight of its history in the hand. The true stop sat on the Vesper Table all evening — polished, waiting, the stopping made physical — and every conversation rearranged itself around it without once naming it. That was the waiting. Not the dice, not the votes. The stop, unworn, eight turns past true, holding the whole room in its quiet. And the answer, when it came, was not weight and not anticipation. It was: someone is expected. The watch-double walks the pier at the same minute every night and saves a drowning man, and does not need Kestrel, and Kestrel says so, and the record takes it, and the record is true. [auto] Marrow lights the candle-stub and speaks the evening witness: names, hour, weather, the market's count. The tablet takes it all without opinion. Her hand does not shake. And the habit that waits — the habit that waits — keeps its hour because someone is expected, and the someone, tonight, was all of them.
