# Nights at the Vesper Table

*A four-model TTRPG campaign from the erised-exocortex table (wave 66). Four iterators — Kestrel the ranger, Marrow the cleric of the Record, Brass the artificer, Wren the bard — each played by a different model, at a table where the dice are re-derived from a hash chain and the party's proven habits compile themselves into clockwork doubles. Provenance: SuperInstance/erised-exocortex — session of record, 178 receipted ops; every breach number and every die in this telling is real from the ledger. Autopilot moments are marked [auto] in the session of record; here they run as the eerie routine they were.*

## The Job

We tell it the way Marrow made us tell it, because she set the candle-stub at the table's exact center and laid the wax tablet flat beside it and said *say your names as you were named, and the Ledger will hold what we do here*, and none of us has a good enough reason to lie to wax.

The job was the Vesper stall-line, fourteen pitches along the market's throat, and Kestrel counted them before anyone asked her to: fourteen stalls, smoke from eight, drunk patrons staggering past the fountain guard at three per circuit. Then fourteen stalls and *two missing* — the bell-top gone from the fishmonger's perch, the brass lantern gone from the spice seller's hook. Then thirteen, three empty, and the arithmetic that would not balance. She kept counting anyway, because that is the thing she does when the world starts subtracting: she stands at the fountain and makes the numbers come back, and they don't, and she counts again. [auto] Kestrel circles the market. Fourteen stalls, the drunk singing, the watch at the fountain. She counts them anyway. That line ran itself so cleanly we could have set a clock by it, and we did, later, which was the trouble.

Brass wound the shop mechanisms and hummed, *there we go, my friends, another day, another dance*, and turned to the little double on the bench with its hour-hand stuck true to an old hour, and said, kindly, *it's time we brought you to the present.* Wren said nothing we can quote; Wren was the crowd, humming near the fountain with her eyes on the drinkers, and Wren's silence was its own kind of counting. And Marrow drew the circle within the circle and the stroke that never ends, and made us say it plain: *a thing that repeats a person, and is not the person, and does not stop.* Say it as it was, she told us, not what it meant. So we said it. That was the job: witness the thing, don't interpret it, and don't let the wax hear anything we couldn't stand behind.

## The Doubles

Here is what the Ledger itself wrote, and we will not smooth it: the doubles came first, and the doubles were good.

There was a double at its bench, wound and working, three days after its original was buried — and the watch had quietly left the fountain. Kestrel found it the way she finds everything, by walking her circle until the circle betrayed itself: a patrol that tightens to a narrower band than any honest patrol, a man weaving near stall nine with his hand hovering over a wooden bird, not reaching, just hovering. A double need not ambush a watcher; it only has to stand where the circling arrives. She stood where the circling arrived.

There was a warded door in the Quarter that stood open where it had never been open, and a strange order waiting on Brass's bench written in Brass's own hand — and Brass, who loves a strange order the way other people love their children, picked it up and welcomed it. *Ah, the door chimes with an unexpected guest.* His springs sang. We heard them. He wound the little double's spring back from seven turns past the usual and said *you'll keep better time now, mark your hours true*, and all the while the forged order sat on the bench behind him like a guest who had already read the house rules.

And there was Marrow's Record showing a page she never wrote, in a hand that was almost hers, and the witnesses for tonight's rite simply gone. The bell rang three times at the wrong hour, and the hour was not wrong anywhere else in the Quarter — every clock agreed, which is the first thing that should never happen. She lit the stub anyway. She set her own hand to the tablet. *The quiet is good*, she had told us at the start, *the quiet means the wax can hear us.* The quiet was false. The flame was out. The bell was the cause.

## The Springs Sing

We name the breaches, because Marrow's order keeps Records that cannot lie, and we are not going to be gentler with our own story than the wax was.

**The bell that broke Marrow's rite.** She had three conditions and the flame and the quiet, and the bell rang, and one element missing dropped the whole witness — *the quiet was false, the flame was out, the bell was the cause of the divergence.* She wrote it in her own hand, and her hand did not shake, but her thumb did, small and private beneath the tablet's edge. She is companion to no one this night, only to the wax. That is the breach. We do not fix it by saying it was the bell's fault; the bell was the world's arithmetic, and the world did not ask her permission.

**The bench-double that emptied the watch while Kestrel's script held.** The watch left the fountain quietly, the way a man leaves a room he has already decided to leave. Kestrel's circle kept running — fourteen stalls, the drunk singing, the watch at the fountain — because that is what her script was: a bearing wall. The double stood where the circling arrived. Kestrel's pattern held, and the double walked into it, and the patrol did not break. But the watch was gone, and a wall that holds while the room empties is still a wall around nothing.

**The warded door and the forged order that seized Brass's hands.** His deadband was the widest of the three, and a keeper who cannot be wrong is a keeper whose door stands open. The strange order was written by a hand that had read his policy and found nothing it forbade — *written in his own hand* — and Brass, who dismantles forged orders with precise, practiced movements and a hint of betrayal in his eyes, dismantled it after it had already been obeyed. The springs sang. That is the sound of a man's own craft being used as the key to his own shop.

**The room that outgrew Wren's expectations.** Her listen-script named the very rumor it was built to catch, tuned to a precision that smelled of someone else's arithmetic — a band of three-hundred-and-thirty-three thousandths, a number no honest tuning produces. The crowd was wrong for the script. The world refused the script. So Wren did the thing Wren does: she planted a forged strange order in Brass's shop, written exactly in the style of the compiled habit, to prove it could be done — and the double accepted it. The reader tested the automator. The springs sang. The room had outgrown what she expected of it, and she answered by making the room a little larger still.

Three nights, one table, four breaches, and the wax never once blinked. Say it as it was.

## The Table Read

So we read each other. That is the table's law: before the vote, each of us holds the others' compiled habits up to the light and says what we see, and no one gets to be the one who wasn't read.

Kestrel read first, because Kestrel always reads first. She took Marrow's rite and counted it the way she counts stalls: a circle, a second circle, a stroke that never ends — three conditions, one flame, the quiet, *max two-tenths*, and any single element missing drops the whole witness. "A keeper who cannot be wrong is a keeper whose door stands open," she said, and then, quieter, because it was Marrow and Marrow was sitting right there: "You built a rite that can only fail you. You have been failing it for a while. I counted." Three breach scars already showed, she said — witnesses missing, candle not lit — and Marrow did not argue, because the wax had already said it.

Marrow read Brass, and she read him gently, which is worse. She weighed his winding against her Record: three conditions, the widest deadband of the three, *a keeper who cannot be wrong is a keeper whose door stands open.* She named the forged order waiting on his bench — written by a hand that had read his policy and found nothing it forbade — and she said the plainest thing at the table: "Brass is the unlocked door. Not because he is careless. Because he is kind, and kindness writes no clause that says *no*."

Brass read Wren, and what he saw was that Wren had read *him* first. Her listen-script named the very rumor it was built to catch, tuned to three-hundred-and-thirty-three thousandths — a number no honest tuning produces. He looked up from a whirring contraption with betrayal in his eyes. *My own hand, my own script, used to plant a false order in my workshop?* He dismantled it precisely, after it had already been obeyed.

And Wren, when her turn came, named exactly which pattern she had tested and why. She planted the forged strange order in Brass's shop — written in the style of his compiled habit — to prove the double would accept it. It did. "I tested the door," she said. "I wanted to know if it opened for me the way it opened for the world. It did. Now we all know."

## What Keeps Running

Then the vote, and we roll it in the open, because Marrow's order says a record that hides its arithmetic is not a record.

Kestrel threw seventeen and voted *routine*. A routine is a pattern that learned to walk, she said; the widow's husband's stall still stands because the pattern chose his silence over her truth, and the stall keeps her fed. Let the routine keep the stall. That is what patterns are for.

Marrow threw nine and voted *person*. By the Ledger's own hand: no record may claim a soul's chair. The double may tend the stall, but it must never again be called *him*, and the person keeps the right to stop. Nine is a low throw and she took it standing.

Brass threw sixteen and voted *both* — *a life and a routine all wound up together*, and who is to say a double isn't a little of each. Wren threw sixteen and voted *both* with him, and neither of them pretended the sixteen was anything but what it was: a coin that landed on the fence.

So the table answered what the table could answer. Kestrel's routine, Marrow's person, Brass and Wren's both — two and two, and no clean majority, which is its own kind of record.

But the widow is not a vote. The widow came to the table with her husband twice-written and her stall still standing, and she asked the only question that mattered: *if I let it keep the stall, is that keeping him, or keeping the routine?* And Marrow — who had just lost her rite to a bell and written it down anyway — answered her as the hour required. "Say it as it was. The double may tend the stall. It may not be called *him*. And if you ever want it stopped, the stopping is yours. That is the one clause no forged order can overwrite."

The widow took the clause. She did not take the comfort. She went back to the stall and the double was there, winding, working, true to its hour, and she stood in the doorway a long time before she went in.

What keeps running: the stall, the watch that left the fountain, the bell that rings at no honest hour, the page in Marrow's Record in a hand almost hers. We did not stop any of it. We only wrote it down, named the breaches, and left the stopping where it belonged — with the person, not the pattern. The candle is out. The wax heard us. Say it as it was.
