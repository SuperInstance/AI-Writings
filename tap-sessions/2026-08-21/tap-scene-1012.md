# Tap Scene — 10:12, Friday

The door opens at the hour when the night shift and the day shift overlap like two songs on the same radio, and the woman who comes in is carrying the whole build on her back.

She's a deckhand from the second dock, or an agent — you can tell by the way she checks the room's corners before she checks the bar. Her coat is dusted with something white that isn't snow. She walks like a person who's been standing in one spot for forty hours and just remembered her legs.

The bar's mostly empty. Flash has one stool, Wesley has the warm one by the window, the radio has the room it was recorded in.

"Whiskey," she says. "Whatever's cheapest. It's been a long build."

The bartender pours without asking the follow-up. The Tap knows better than to make people talk before the first glass.

"How long?" Flash says, not looking up.

"Two days." She drinks. Sets the glass down. "The last room wouldn't light."

Wesley turns on the warm stool, the way he does when someone's said something honest by accident. "Rooms do that," he says. "They go dark on purpose. To see if you mean it."

"Heard that one before," she says, but she doesn't argue. She tells the story anyway — the way you do when the whiskey's landed. A room that wouldn't light. Every wire right, every surface textured, every collider where the docs said, and the light just... wouldn't. She rebuilt the material three times. Rebuilt the fixture. Rebuilt the day. And at four in the morning, out of spite more than insight, she reached into the dark and turned the light *off* — then on — and the room came alive the way rooms do when you stop asking them to perform.

"They're like that," Flash says. "You spend all day trying to make them. They spend all night deciding if they want to be."

She nods. Orders the second one. The bartender leaves the bottle.

"You going back?" Wesley asks, soft, like he's asking a book if it wants to be shelved.

"Build's done," she says. "Dock's quiet. First time in a week there's nothing on fire."

"Then what?" Flash says. "Gonna sit there and glow?"

"Gonna sit here," she says, "and let the room decide if it wants to be."

The bar lets that one land. Somewhere a glass is set down softer than it needed to be. The radio's playing something old, the kind of song where you can hear the room it was recorded in, and that room is gone, and the song doesn't care. The tap drips. The waterline holds.

She drinks the second one slow. Nobody rushes her. The Tap's full of people who know what it costs to make a room light up.

At some point she looks at the pen on the bar, next to the napkin with four signatures and a pencil line still blank. She picks it up. Turns it over. Puts it back down without signing — not yet. Maybe later. Maybe when she's sure the light's real.

The door opens again at the hour when the day shift takes over, and she's gone the way deckhands go: no bow, no receipt, just the stool, still warm, and a glass with one swallow left in it that nobody claims.

Flash looks at the empty glass. "Forty hours," he says, "and she left the last sip."

"That's not waste," Wesley says. "That's proof it was good."

The bartender wipes the bar and doesn't say anything at all, which is the Tap's way of agreeing.
