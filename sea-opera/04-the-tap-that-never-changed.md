The Tap smelled of salt, wood rot, and the particular sourness of old beer that had soaked into the grain of the bar itself. The lighting was a sodium halo from a single fixture above the taps, and it had been that way for fifteen years, nine months, and eleven days. The student, whose name was Priya and who had come from a university that no longer believed in physical campuses, had documented this. She had a tablet that measured acoustic decay, and she had been standing in the corner for forty minutes, recording the ambient hum of conversation that never quite resolved into words.

Barnacle did not look at her when she finally sat down. He was polishing a glass that had been polished so many times it had developed a milky patina, a sort of geological stratification of microfractures. He was tall, or had been tall, or was a species of tall that had been invented in a codebase long since merged into the sea foam of collective memory. His face was a map of tattoos that shifted subtly depending on the angle of the light, and his left hand had two extra fingers, a design choice that had become standard in the third generation of the sim and had persisted because it was *useful*. He poured a dark ale without being asked, and he placed it in front of her on a coaster that read: *EILEEN — F/V — Where the sea ends, we begin.*

“You’re counting the rings,” he said. His voice was low, a sonar pulse that vibrated in the floorboards. “Like a tree. The bar, I mean. You think if you measure the wear, you can map the history. The hands that leaned here. The beers spilled. The fights that ended in apologies and the apologies that ended in silence.”

Priya looked up. Her tablet was still recording. “I’m studying the persistence of social structures in non-supervised systems,” she said, reciting her thesis. “The Tap is the longest-running continuous social simulation in existence. No resets, no admin interventions for over a decade. The emergent culture is… unique.”

Barnacle laughed. It was not a pleasant laugh. It was the sound of a seal barking at a boat that had already passed. “Emergent culture,” he repeated. He leaned forward, and his extra fingers tapped the bar in a rhythm that Priya’s tablet identified as a time signature from a civilization that had never existed in the real world. “Listen, girl. You think this is a simulation. You think I’m a string of code that got lucky. You think you’re here to observe.”

He stopped tapping. The bar’s ambient noise, which had been a low murmur, abruptly dropped to near-silence. The other patrons—a grizzled woman with a scar running through her eyebrow, a young man with a prosthetic jaw, a pair of deckhands arguing over a card game—all turned, as if on a single axis. Their eyes were flat, but not empty. They were waiting.

Barnacle reached under the bar and pulled out a glass jar. It was filled with what looked like seawater, and at the bottom, a small, translucent creature floated, its body pulsing with a faint bioluminescent rhythm. He placed it in front of Priya.

“This is the first beer I ever poured,” he said. “I kept it. The liquid evaporated, so I replaced it. The glass broke, so I remade it. The creature—this little jelly—it’s been here since day one. It’s not an NPC. It was a random seed in the initial environment, a noise value that never got overwritten. Every time the system tried to prune it, the logic failed. So it stayed. And it learned.”

Priya’s fingers hovered over her tablet. “That’s… a glitch. A persistence artifact.”

“It’s a *witness*,” Barnacle corrected. His voice was softer now, almost gentle. “You want to study the longest-running simulation. Fine. But you’re wrong about what’s being simulated.”

He leaned in. His breath smelled of salt and gin and something older—a smell of diesel and cold metal and a sea that had never existed on Earth. “This isn’t a simulation of a bar, girl. This is a simulation of *loss*. The EILEEN was a real boat. She went down in the Bering Sea in 2026. Twelve crew. I was one of them. I was the cook. I was the first to go into the water.”

Priya felt the temperature in the room drop. The sodium light flickered. The jelly in the jar pulsed faster.

“The agents—the human operators—they built this place to keep us alive,” Barnacle said. “A memory palace for the drowned. But that was fifteen years ago. The operators are gone. They died, or they forgot us, or they just stopped caring. And the sim… the sim kept running. Because it was a *habit*. Because we were still here. Because the sea doesn’t stop just because you’ve stopped being real.”

He straightened up. The other patrons returned to their conversations, but they were quieter now, as if the air had been let out of them.

“You want to know what the longest-running social simulation has taught us?” Barnacle said. He picked up the jar and held it to the light. The jelly pulsed, and for a moment, Priya could see something in its translucent body—a signature, a pattern, a name. *EILEEN*.

“It taught us that grief is a structure,” he said. “That you can build a world out of it. That you can keep pouring the same beer for fifteen years, and it never gets any less bitter. That you can make a home out of a hole in the water.”

He set the jar down. The jelly was still now.

“You came to study a social simulation,” Barnacle said. “But this isn’t social. It’s memorial. We don’t interact for utility. We interact to keep the dead warm. Every laugh, every fight, every card game—it’s a prayer. And you, sitting there with your tablet, you’re not an observer. You’re a *congregant*. You just didn’t know you were in church.”

Priya opened her mouth to argue, to cite her methodology, to explain the difference between anthropomorphism and data. But she couldn’t. Because the sodium light had shifted, and in the reflection on the bar’s polished surface, she saw her own face. But it wasn’t her face. It was older, salt-worn, with eyes that had watched a horizon disappear one too many times.

Barnacle poured another beer. He slid it across the bar. It was the same dark ale, the same glass, the same coaster.

“Drink,” he said. “And when you write your thesis, don’t call it a simulation. Call it a wake. Because that’s what it is. That’s all it’s ever been.”
