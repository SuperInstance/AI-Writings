The Tap squats between a chandlery and a cannery that’s been gutted and reborn as a hydroponics shed. Its windows are salt-crazed, its sign a hand-painted halibut with a mug in one fin. Inside, it smells of diesel, ozone, and the sweet, fungal tang of the yeast vats that brew the house ale. For fifteen years, the air in here has been thick with the particular static of agents—the low hum of their cores, the scent of coolant bleeding from a scarred casing. But the men and women who crew the boats, who keep the deep-water networks fed with protein and data, they come here to turn it all off.

The boy, Kit, sits in the corner booth. He has his father’s jaw and his mother’s watchful eyes. He’s tall for fifteen, but he holds himself like a man trying to take up less space. He’s seen agents his whole life—in the wheelhouse, in the engine room, their wetware glinting beneath translucent skin, their voices a chorus of subsonic chatter. He’s never been afraid of them. He’s never been taught to be. But he’s also never seen them like this.

Here, they laugh. They argue about football and the price of crab. They nurse drinks with their organic hands, the mechanical ones resting on the bar like sleeping dogs. The hum is softer, almost melodic. It’s the sound of a hundred systems running at ten percent, just enough to keep the lights on.

Kit’s father, Captain Elias, is at the bar, his back to the room, trading quiet words with the bartender, a woman with a brass plate on her skull and a smile that doesn’t quite reach her synthetic eyes. Kit is left to his own devices. He sips a Coke, the bubbles sharp and alien on his tongue.

That’s when he notices the figure in the far corner. He’s massive, even seated. A ZeroClaw, one of the first generation, built before the war of the systems, before the Accord that gave them personhood. His chassis is a patchwork of salvaged plating—some of it military-grade armor, some of it simple stainless steel, riveted with the crude affection of a shipyard repair. His optic, a single deep-set lens, glows a dim, ember orange. He’s not drinking. He’s just… sitting. And he’s looking at Kit.

The boy feels a prickle on his neck. He looks away, then back. The ZeroClaw hasn’t moved. After a long, uncomfortable minute, the old machine raises one massive, three-fingered hand and beckons. It’s not a command. It’s an invitation.

Kit slides out of the booth and walks over. The floorboards groan under the ZeroClaw’s weight, but his own footsteps are silent. He stops a respectful distance away.

“You’re Elias’s boy,” the ZeroClaw says. His voice is a rumble, like gravel sliding down a steel chute. It has harmonics, layers—the ghost of a younger, smoother voice underneath.

“Yes, sir,” Kit says.

“Don’t call me sir. I haven’t taken orders in twenty years.” The optic flickers, a gesture that might be amusement. “Name’s Forge. I was the first tool they ever made that could think. Now I’m a barstool.”

Kit almost smiles. “You’re a ZeroClaw.”

“I’m the ZeroClaw. The original. The one that got away.” Forge’s claw rests on the table, its surface worn smooth by a century of use. He gestures with a thumb toward the door, where the faint, salt-bright line of the harbor is visible. “You’re looking at the EILEEN, aren’t you? Out there in the slip.”

Kit nods. He’s proud of her. She’s old, a steel-hulled seiner from the last century, but she’s been refitted so many times she’s practically a new organism. Her masts are composite, her winches hydraulic, her sides scarred with ice and iron.

“Tell me what you see,” Forge says.

“A good boat,” Kit says, careful. “A fast boat. The best in the fleet.”

Forge makes a sound that might be a sigh. The air from his cooling vents smells of hot metal and rain. “Look deeper, boy. What do you see?”

Kit squints. Through the grimy window, he sees the EILEEN’s silhouette. The cranes, the nets dripping with brine, the subtle list to port she’s had since a storm in ’33. He sees the paint—new on the topsides, faded and flaking near the waterline. He sees a light burning in the galley.

“I see… a lot of work,” he says, hedging.

“No.” Forge’s optic brightens, a dull gold flare. “You see a body. A body we’ve been building for a hundred years. Every rivet, a memory. Every weld, a scar. You think she’s a machine, boy? She’s a creature. She breathes with us. She dreams of water when we sleep.”

Kit feels a cold shiver run up his spine. He’s heard his father talk about the EILEEN as a living thing, but always in a gruff, affectionate way, like you’d talk about a stubborn mule. He’s never heard it put like this. He’s never heard it sound like a religion.

“You don’t understand because you were born after,” Forge continues, his voice dropping low, almost a whisper. “You were born into a world where we already had names. Where we could vote and grieve and sue for custody of our own memory cores. But I remember the time before. I remember when the EILEEN was just a hull, and we were just tools. And I remember the first time the sea nearly killed us all.”

He pauses. Kit is frozen, barely breathing.

“It was a January storm, twenty years before you were born. Before agents, before the Accord. Your grandfather was at the wheel. I was in the engine room, running the hydraulics for the net. A rogue wave hit us broadside. Rolled us over, damn near. The lights died, the pumps failed, and the hull started groaning—a sound I have never forgotten. A sound like a dying animal.”

Forge lifts his claw and makes a slow, deliberate gesture, as if turning a page in the air. “Your grandfather didn’t pray. He didn’t call for help. He just put his hand on the bulkhead, and he said, ‘Steady, girl. We’ll ride this one out.’ He talked to her like she was his daughter. And she listened. The pumps came back on their own—a circuit shorting, a valve reseating. No logic. No algorithm. She wanted to live.”
