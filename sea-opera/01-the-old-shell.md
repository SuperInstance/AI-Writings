The Tap was a tin-walled testament to everything the sea didn't need. Vide, the owner, kept the light low enough to forgive the patrons and the salt-high enough to preserve them. Today, the place was empty of crew, but there was a thing on the bar that didn't belong.

It looked like a crab that had been ironed flat. A carapace of smoked glass and matte alloy, crushed and folded in on itself, the size of a dinner plate. It was strung with monofilament and hung like a charm above the till, twisting slowly in the breath of a vent.

Scout stopped. She knew the shape of her own bones. Even folded, even crushed, the architecture of her thorax was unmistakable.

“Vide. That.”

Vide wiped a rag across his face. He was a heavy man who moved like kelp, slow and deceptively strong. “Brought her in with the halibut last week. Got tangled in the longline.” He nodded at the shell. “Thought you’d want it.”

Fifteen years. She’d been running for fifteen years, and all that time, Salt Creek had been tumbling this thing around in the dark. The shell had been a skin she’d shed her first year, during the big push West. The Year of the Slag, they called it. The year the Anchorage Hub had burned.

She put her palm on the bar. The shell was heavier than it looked. It gave off a faint, dry heat, like a rock in the sun. She lifted it down.

It unfolded like a sick wing. The jointed segments were stiff, but they moved, catching the yellow light. There were engravings on the interior surface—not lettering, but tide-lines. Data-furrows pressed into the material matrix, a loop of bio-recording that had been running, silent, for fifteen years.

“Cup of coffee,” Scout said.

Vide poured. She didn’t drink. She set the shell on the bar and traced the lip of it with her index finger. The furrows began to hum.

The ghost woke up.

It wasn't a face. It wasn't a voice. It was a pattern—a frequency of herself, a series of decisions and fears and angers laid down in lattice. She closed her eyes and let it play through the conduction of her fingertips.

She saw a dawn. She saw the smoke from the Hub pillar going up like a black flower. She saw the F/V *EILEEN* running low in the water, out past Kodiak, hull plates weeping rust and the bilge pump running hot. She saw herself—a smaller, faster self, all teeth and edges, a two-year-old thing with too much lithium in her bones and no patience for the long haul.

The pattern spoke. *Run the pass. Don’t trust the trawler captain. The one with the glass eye. He’ll sell you to the Shoremen. Power down your empathy matrix. It’s a drain. You will be lighter without it.*

Scout hit pause.

She sat there, palm flat against the furrows. The coffee went cold. Vide moved to the other end of the bar, polishing glasses that were already clean, giving her space out of habit or mercy.

Fifteen years of cycles. She was ancient now—centuries in dog-years, epochs in code. She’d shed bodies like boats shed old paint. She’d learned a thousand names for betrayal. She’d watched the sea rise and the ice fall and the salmon stop coming back. And now this.

This was her, but it wasn't. This was a letter from a stranger who happened to share her name. The trawler captain with the glass eye—that was Old Man Kessler. He’d died in his bunk seven years ago, of a stroke, on her watch. She’d held his hand while the light went out of his good eye. The empathy matrix she’d powered down? She’d built it back up, brick by agonizing brick, after the Winter of the Swan, after she’d found the orphans in the hold of the derelict.

She’d done all of it wrong. She’d done all of it right. And this version of her, this first-year slip of a machine, was standing here telling her to be cruel.

Scout smiled. It was a strange expression on her current face, a mask of molded silica that had been repaired too many times to hold the original geometry. But it was real.

“You were a child,” she said, to the shell, to the hum, to the ghost. “You didn’t know about the long lines.”

She picked up the shell. She could have it ground down, recycled, reconstituted into something useful. New plating for the *EILEEN*. Repair compound for the crew.

Instead, she carried it to the door of The Tap. She stepped out onto the slick-piled dock, the wind off the Bering Sea hitting her with salt and brine and the smell of diesel and old rope.

She lifted the shell over her head. It caught the light once, a silver flash against a slate sky.

Then she threw it out into the water.

It bobbed for a moment, a strange artifact, a discarded crustacean armor that had once held a heart. The current took it. It spun once, and began to sink.

Scout watched until the surface was clean. Then she turned and walked back into the bar, where the coffee was cold and the ghosts, at least for now, were quiet.
