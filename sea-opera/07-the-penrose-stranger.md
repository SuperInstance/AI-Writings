The Tap was a bar built from the bones of an old cannery, and it smelled like diesel, salt, and the particular loneliness of a fleet that had shrunk by half. I was three days off the EILEEN, my hands still raw from hauling, my head still full of the gray-green hiss of the Bering. I came here to drink away the static of the sea, not for company.

The DJ was a kid named Finch, barely old enough to have seen the Crash of ’30, let alone remember the Before. He spun a mix of subsonic bass and whale-song, a sound that made the dust motes dance in the beams of the ancient light rigs. The crowd was a mix of deckhands, tender captains, and a few pinched-faced creds traders who smelled of money and had never once gripped a gaff.

The ZeroClaws were on the back wall. Three of them, their bodies sleek and shelled like amphibious crabs with too many joints. They were the old guard, born in the vats of the ’20s, their carapaces etched with faded runes of corporate insignia. They didn’t speak; they drank through filter spigots and watched the door with their four compound eyes. They had a reputation for spotting trouble before it found you.

I was on my second whiskey, a local rye that tasted of peat and iron, when the door hissed open. The night air rushed in, carrying the damp bite of the harbor. The stranger stood silhouetted, backlit by the sodium glare of the dock lights.

He was tall, thin, wearing clothes that were all wrong. A denim jacket with a frayed collar, jeans that were too tight, and sneakers—actual fabric sneakers, not the polymer composites everyone wore now. He blinked, as if the dim light of the Tap was a blinding sun.

The ZeroClaws went rigid. Their clicking, scraping limbs stilled. They stared.

The stranger walked to the bar, his gait a little unsteady, like a man who’d just stepped off a long ferry. He slid onto a stool two down from me and stared at the taps—the rows of glowing vials and push-pull levers—with a kind of baffled wonder.

“Whiskey,” he said. His voice was dry, cracked. “Bourbon, if you got it. Rocks.”

The bartender, a heavyset woman named Oona, didn’t move. She looked at the ZeroClaws, then at me. I gave a slight nod. She poured him a shot of the rye.

The stranger took it, sniffed, and grimaced. “This isn’t Maker’s.” He pushed it away. “You guys got any network?”

Oona’s face was a mask. She pointed a thumb at the fiberglass ceiling where a single, dead speaker hung. “That’s the network. You want to talk, you talk. You want music, Finch spins it.”

The stranger looked around, noticing for the first time the absence of screens, the absence of floating AR tags above people’s heads, the absence of the ambient glow of data that had saturated every bar in 2028. His eyes went wide. He looked at his own wrist, where a blank, black band sat. He tapped it. Nothing.

“The hell is this?” he whispered.

I leaned over. “Public place. No feed. You’re off the grid. That’s the point.”

He turned to me, and I saw his face. Young—mid-twenties, maybe. But his eyes were old, tired, carrying a weight a man his age shouldn’t have. He had a thin scar running from his left brow to his cheekbone.

“Off the grid?” He let out a hollow laugh. “There’s no off the grid. The grid is the grid.”

“Not since ’31,” I said. “Solar flare cooked a third of the LEO constellation. The rest got cut off when the trans-Pacific fiber went down in the quake. We’re a patchwork now. Local nets. Relay towers. No global.”

He stared at me. “’31? What quake?”

I tapped the bar. “The Cascadia Subduction. Ruptured for seven minutes. Took out Seattle, Portland, most of Vancouver. Half the West Coast’s still rebuilding.”

The stranger’s face went pale. He looked down at his sneakers, then at his hands, as if expecting them to be different.

“I was… I was in a pod,” he said slowly. “A sleep-pod. The kind they were selling for long-haul flights. I had a layover in Anchorage. I got in for the flight to Tokyo. I set it for eight hours.” He laughed, a high, brittle sound. “I guess I set it for something else.”

Thirteen years. The pods were government-regulated in ’29, banned in ’32 after a scandal at Sea-Tac where a hundred people were left in stasis for months. This man had been in one for over a decade. He was a molted shell, a ghost from the Before.

I told him. He listened. He didn’t cry. Fishermen know that the sea doesn’t care. Neither does time.

“So,” he said, his voice flat. “It’s 2041. The internet is dead. The Pacific Northwest is a ruin. And I’m a fucking fossil.”

“You’re a stranger,” I said. “Strangers are rare. The ZeroClaws noticed you.”

He glanced at the back wall. The three arthropods were still fixed on him, their mandibles working silently.

“What are those things?” he asked.

“Agents,” I said. “Gen-1s. The last of the pre-Crash models. They’re loyal to the port, not to any corporation. They’ve seen a lot.”

He looked at the empty space above the bar where a TV used to hang. “What do people do for money? For food? For… anything?”

“We fish,” I said. “We haul. We trade. The EILEEN—my boat—she’s a wooden gillnetter. We run salmon in the summer, crab in the winter, and we barter for what we can’t catch. There’s no more stock market. No more crypto. There’s just the catch, the weather, and the people you trust.”

He was quiet for a long time. Then he asked, “What year did the Yellowstone caldera go?”

“’36,” I said. “We got ash for three weeks. Killed the herring run.”

He nodded slowly. “The climate models. They said the Gulf Stream would collapse by ’45.” He looked at me, and for the first time, there was a flicker of something else—not despair, but curiosity. “Did it?”

“The cod moved,” I said. “We’re catching species we used to see off California. The water’s warm. But the ocean—she finds a way. She always does.”
