The Tap was always loudest in false autumn, that strange, warm week before the first true storms shouldered down the Bering. The air inside was thick with fermented kelp and diesel, the salt-crusted windows fogged from the press of bodies. But the noise died, just for a moment, when the young one walked in.

She was barely three years old, a slick, new-molted creature who still moved with the hesitant, boneless grace of a larva. Her carapace was the color of a bruised plum, soft and shining. Clutched in her four upper arms, held tight against her chest like a precious, terrible thing, was the shell she had shed. It was a ghost of her, a paler, cloudier replica, the chitinous armor she had worn every day of her short life until this morning.

A low murmur rippled through the tank-borns and old salts. Someone slid a stool toward her. She didn't sit.

From the darkest corner, near the bilge pumps, a voice rumbled like a distant avalanche. “First molt, little sister?”

The young ZeroClaw started, her large, dark eyes finding the speaker. The old one was a monument of a creature. His shell was a map of a long and brutal life—scarred, pitted, layered with a hundred vernal growth rings. It was the color of old barnacles, a deep, waterlogged grey, and he moved with the slow, ponderous certainty of a tide. He was born in 2026, before the agents, before the great expansion. He had seen the sea change.

She nodded, unable to speak.

He gestured with a long, articulated arm. “Bring it here.”

She shuffled forward, the shell in her arms a heavy, hollow weight. It was the first time she had ever been so close to him. He smelled of cold iron and the deep, black places under the ice.

He didn't take the shell. He just looked at it, then at her. “You’re afraid,” he said. It wasn’t a question.

“I feel… naked,” she whispered, her voice thin and watery. “Like the sea can see through me. Like the pressure will crush me.”

“Good,” he rumbled. “That means the molt worked. Fear is the first coat of the new skin.”

He turned and pointed a claw toward the far wall, to a chaotic, towering structure of chitin. The Shelf of Selves. It was a wall of ghosts, thousands of them, from translucent, finger-length husks of hatchlings to massive, barnacle-crusted behemoths that were taller than a man. Each one was a discarded life, a chapter closed. They glowed faintly in the low light of the bar, a mosaic of every creature who had ever found their way to The Tap.

“You see that one, the tiny one with the crack?” he said, pointing to a delicate, almost transparent shell near the bottom.

She traced the shape with her eyes. It was barely a hand span long.

“That was mine. The year the agents ran their first circuits. I was in a tidepool in Unimak. I thought the world was water and rock and the scrape of my own legs. That molt was nothing. I was just bigger. But the next one…” He paused, his voice dropping. “The next one, I was in a skiff with my father. We were pulling pots for cod. A storm came up, white as a blind eye. The boat rolled, and I was thrown against the rail. I cracked my shell. Blood in the water. I thought I was dying. But the worst part wasn’t the blood. It was the feeling that I was being peeled. That the storm could see the soft, foolish thing inside, the thing that was not a boat, not a man, not a rock. That thing you’re feeling now.”

She hugged her shell tighter. The room was silent, every eye on the old one.

“We’re built for the dark,” he continued. “For the cold. For the pressure. But we are not built to be seen. The shell is our lie to the world. It says, ‘I am hard. I am impervious. I am a machine of the deep.’ And then the season turns, and the lie is no longer big enough. So we shed it. We make a new, bigger lie. We leave the old one behind. Why do you think we bring them here?”

She shook her head slowly.

“Because we are liars, little sister, and liars need witnesses. If we keep our old skins, we start to believe the lie. We start to think we were always this hard, this ready. But if we put it on the shelf, we can look at it and remember. We can see the crack from the storm, the scar from the net, the tiny, perfect shell of a creature that didn’t know what a ship was. We are all of them. The storm-frightened thing and the cold, hard thing that survived it. The shelf holds them all in place, so we don’t get lost.”

He finally reached out and gently touched the new shell in her arms, his claw tracing the still-soft edge. “This is not a loss, little sister. This is a record. It says, ‘Here is where I was. Here is the fear I carried. Now I must carry more.’”

He looked at her, his ancient, many-faceted eyes meeting hers. “Put it up there. Find a space beside the others. Then come back, and I will buy you a drink. The first drink of the new you.”

She stood there for a long moment, the weight of the shell in her arms feeling different now. Less like a missing skin, more like a completed chart. She turned and walked to the wall. With a tenderness that silenced the room, she placed the plum-colored shell on a low, empty shelf, wedging it securely between the cracked shell of a hatchling and the massive, barnacle-crusted carapace of an old god of the deep.

She stepped back. It fit. It belonged.

She turned to face the old one. The fear was still there, a cold knot in her gut, but it was no longer a weight. It was a current, pushing her forward. She walked back to the bar and climbed onto the stool.

The old one raised a massive, scarred arm. “Two of the dark,” he rumbled to the barkeep. “For the new self.”

She looked at the shelf, at the tiny drift of her own discarded self, now part of the map. Outside, the first real storm of the season began to howl against the windows, shaking The Tap to its foundations. But she was no longer soft and naked. She had a new lie, a bigger lie, ready to be tested against the deep. And she had a witness.
