The hull was a living thing beneath Kaimana’s belly, its timber groaning in a language older than her own. Fourteen nights out from Hawai’i, and the star path was stitched into her bones — Hōkūle’a rising, then falling, a zenith marker she could find blind. Her hand, splayed flat on the damp wood, felt the sea’s breath: the long, slow heave from the southeast, the cross-swell that rolled in from the north like a memory of a storm that had died days ago. It was all there, in the body’s registry. Her father had taught her to read it the way others read a face — the flicker of a mouth, the tension of a brow.

But tonight, the face lied.

She pressed her cheek to the hull, eyes closed, tasting salt on her lips. The canoe rose, fell, rose — and then, between one breath and the next, the rhythm stuttered. A pulse that should have been there was not. The southeast swell, the one that had carried them like a mother’s arm for two weeks, had gone thin. Threadbare. The north cross-swell pushed harder, its angle wrong, its timing off by a half-heartbeat. Kaimana’s fingers curled into the wood.

No chant described this. She knew them all — the songs of the thousand-year fleet, the genealogies of waves that mapped every island between the Marquesas and the dawn. Her grandfather had sung them to her in the long house, his voice cracking like dry bark, and she had stored them in a place deeper than memory. But this was not in the song. This was a deadband — a gap in the pattern, a silence where the ocean’s voice should have been. The stars above were unchanged, their fixed points indifferent. The wind was steady. Only the sea knew.

She opened her eyes and sat up. The crew watched her from the shadows of the hull — twelve weathered faces, patient as stone. They did not ask. A wayfinder did not speak until she had something to say. Kaimana looked at the sky, at the familiar sprawl of the Milky Way, and felt the old knowledge — the reflexive, automatic knowing — fail her. It was like a breath catching. Like a word stuck on the tongue.

*Think*, she told herself. *Think like the ancestors thought before the songs were made.*

She lay down again, but this time she did not let her body fall into its practiced rhythm. She forced her mind to separate the sensations. The hull was not a map; it was a story being told in real time, and she had been reading only the parts she knew. The deadband was not an absence — it was a revision. She pressed her ear to the wood, feeling the cross-swell’s angle. East. No, northeast. It was pushing them toward something. What did the sea carry on its back that the stars could not see?

A memory surfaced — not a chant, but an old man’s aside. Her grandfather, mending a net, had muttered about the time the ocean shifted near a place called Rarotonga, a reef that reached out like a hand hidden beneath the water. The swell, he said, would bend around it, and a navigator who didn’t know that would think the sea had gone mad.

Rarotonga. The island of the long reef. It was not on her star path. It was not supposed to exist anywhere near their course.

Kaimana stood, legs braced against the gentle roll. The crew’s eyes were on her now, and she felt the weight of their trust like a stone in her chest. She looked up at the stars — Hōkūle’a still held its place, but the canoe had drifted. The cross-swell had been carrying them for hours, a current they had not felt because they had not thought to feel it. The deadband was not a violation of the rhythm. It was a doorway.

“Bring us two points west,” she said, her voice steady. “There is a reef beneath the water. We will follow its pulse until the old song resumes.”

No one questioned her. The paddles dipped, and the canoe turned, and Kaimana lay flat once more, her hand on the hull, her mind open. The sea was not broken. It was just speaking a dialect she had never learned — until now. She would learn it. She would carry it home, and her daughters would sing it, and the next time someone felt the deadband, they would not be afraid.

The swell returned at dawn — the old rhythm, the familiar heave — and Kaimana smiled against the wood. The sea had lied, but only to teach her the truth. The star path was not the only map. The ocean itself was the registry, and she had only just learned to read its margins.
