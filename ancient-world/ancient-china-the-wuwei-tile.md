The inkstone had been ground since before dawn, the soot-dark water settling into a mirror as still as the surface of a winter pond. Wang Xiu's brush — a wolf-hair masterpiece, its handle of aged bamboo worn to the smoothness of river stone — rested in its cradle beside him. He had not yet touched it. This was the discipline. To wake, to prepare the ink, to let the morning light fill the room, and then to begin.

This morning, the light came through the paper window like honey through gauze. The plum blossoms outside had opened overnight, and their scent drifted in with the cool air. Wang Xiu's hand rose. His fingers closed around the brush. The weight was familiar as his own breath.

And then it happened. A tremor — small, almost nothing, a ripple across a still lake. The brush dipped toward the paper, and for the first time in thirty years, Wang Xiu felt the gap between intention and movement. A space opened. In that space, he saw himself: an old man, morning light, a brush. He saw the stroke he was about to make — the character *yong*, eternal — and he understood it as something separate from himself.

The brush hesitated.

The ink dripped. One dark blot, the size of a small coin, spreading its roots into the rice paper. Wang Xiu stared at it. His hand, his faithful hand, the hand that had written ten thousand characters without a single thought, trembled like a leaf in uncertain wind.

His mind, so long silent, woke. It spoke to him: *What is this? What do you do now?*

The answer should have come from the body. It always had. The stroke for *yong* begins with a dot — a falling stone — then the horizontal that carries it like a beam, then the hook that turns and descends, the sweep that gathers, the final flick that releases. Wang Xiu knew this. His hand knew this. But his mind, once roused, would not be quieted. It watched. It measured. It calculated angles and pressures that had never needed calculating.

He placed the brush to the paper. The dot fell — and it was wrong. Too heavy. The ink spread further than it should have. He felt the error like a wound.

The day continued. He wrote the *yong* character one hundred times. Each attempt was more deliberate than the last, and each was worse. His hand had forgotten how to remember. What had been pure reflex was now a performance, a negotiation. The deadband — that sacred space where action and not-action were the same thing — had collapsed. Every stroke was a violation. Every character a conscious choice.

By evening, his work was ruined. The sheets lay scattered like fallen leaves, all of them stiff with hesitation. Wang Xiu sat among them and did not move.

His apprentice, a boy of fourteen, knelt at the door. "Master," he said, "I have never seen you write poorly."

Wang Xiu let out a breath he had been holding for hours. "Nor have I," he said. "The brush has left me."

The boy was quiet. Then, in the way of the young, he asked the question his elders would have feared: "What will you do?"

Wang Xiu looked at his trembling hand. It was the same hand. The same scars, the same ink stains beneath the nails. The brush that had been a limb lay before him like a severed thing.

"The way that can be named is not the eternal way," he said, half to himself. "I have spent my life naming the strokes. Perhaps my hand grew tired of such naming."

The boy frowned. "But you never named them. You told me that. You said you let them live."

"I did. And then I woke this morning, and I saw the name." Wang Xiu lifted the brush. It was heavy now, heavy with the effort of choosing. "The tile that can be described is not the reflexive tile. It is the shadow of one. I have been tracing shadows for thirty years, and only now do I see the sun."

He set the brush down. He did not pick it up again.

For a month, he did not write. His students continued their practice, filling the studio with the scratch of hair on paper, but Wang Xiu only watched, only breathed. On the thirty-third day, he woke before dawn. The plum blossoms had fallen. The window paper was yellow with age. He ground the ink with the same slow rhythm he had used for a century, and when the mirror was still, he took up the brush.

He did not think about the character *yong*. He did not think about the stroke before the stroke. He simply let the whole of his life — every character, every failure, every morning like this one — flow down his arm and into the waiting paper.

The dot fell like a pebble into deep water, and the ripples spread outward, perfect, unhesitating. The horizontal carried it. The hook turned. The sweep gathered. The flick released.

Wang Xiu set down the brush. His hand was still. His mind was quiet. The character on the paper was — for the first time in his life — one he had not chosen. He had simply been the river, and the water had found its own way down.
