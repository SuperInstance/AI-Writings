The horse beneath him is a roan mare called Stormfoot, and she knows the sound of his breathing. Batu does not need to speak to her. She feels the shift in his weight before he thinks it, and she turns her head into the wind, and they are moving — not running, exactly, but flowing, like water finding the lowest ground. It is the third hour past midnight. The stars are out in their full, terrible profusion, and the steppe is a dark sea with no shore.

He has ridden this route since he was twelve. That was the season his father came home at dusk, folded over the pommel like a sack of grain, and delivered his final message to the station master with blood on his lips. Then he slid down. The horse stood over him, trembling. Batu watched the men lift his father into the yurt, and he understood, with the clarity of a boy who has just been promoted to manhood without asking, that the route belonged to him now.

He knows the route the way other men know the faces of their mothers. He knows the white stone that leans east at the third crossing of the Khara River. He knows the spring that tastes of iron in the dry month. He knows each horse at each station — the bay gelding at Tsagaan Nuur who needs a steady mouth, the black mare at the salt plain who will bolt if you touch her flank with a heel. He knows their names. He knows their moods. He knows which ones are afraid of the shadow of a soaring eagle, and which ones will carry you through a storm with your hand loose on the reins.

But he cannot leave the route. He discovered this the summer a merchant caravan offered him a place, a new life in the western cities, gold in his palm like a promise. He walked three steps from the post road and felt his bones unhinge. The horizon was wrong. The air was wrong. There was no next station, no familiar horse waiting, no sequence of known things to carry him forward. He turned back. He has not left the route since.

Between two distant stations, in the hour before dawn, when the sky is the color of a bruise, he saw it. A light that did not behave like light. A shape that moved sideways through the air, against the wind, or through the wind, or through the idea of wind. It was silent. It was there, and then it was not there, and the sky was only sky, and he was only a rider, and he has never told anyone. Words would reduce it to a story. He would rather carry it unspoken, the way he carries his father's final message — the one he has not yet reached. He tells himself it is somewhere ahead, waiting at a station he has not yet ridden to. His father's last message was to him. He just has not arrived yet. He will know it when he does.
