Xochitl’s fingers knew the days the way other women knew the weight of a child on their hip — by the ache of them, by the curve. She had been born with the count in her blood, a thing her grandmother had whispered over her cradle, tracing a circle on the infant’s palm: *You will hold the days, little flower, and the days will hold you.* She had grown into that promise like a vine growing into a trellis, her bones bending around the structure of the tonalpohualli until the two could not be separated. At twenty-two, she was younger than any daykeeper in the temple’s memory, but no one questioned it. She moved through the codices like a woman walking through her own house, turning pages with the certainty of a hand finding a familiar door in the dark.

The morning began like all mornings — copal smoke braiding the air, obsidian mirror catching the first light, her brush dipped in ink the color of dried blood. She was tracing the sequence for a merchant who needed to know the fortune of a journey, her finger moving from *crocodile* to *wind* to *house*, the days slotting together with the satisfying click of a well-made loom. Then her finger stopped. She counted again. Then again, her lips moving silently, a young woman’s mouth making shapes that should have been impossible. There was a space between two day-signs where the count should have continued — a gap in the red and black glyphs as smooth and empty as a polished stone. The calendar had never had a gap. The calendar was the universe’s skeleton, the spine of every sunrise, the reason the rain fell and the corn rose. If it had a gap, then the universe had a gap, and the universe was not supposed to have gaps.

She spent the day consulting every codex in the temple, her fingers smudging soot and pigment as she flipped through centuries of record. Nothing. The old keepers came to look, their faces creased with the weight of their years, and they shook their heads and called it an error of copying, a stain, a trick of the light. But Xochitl knew better. She had been born with the count in her blood, and the count was telling her something it had never told anyone before.

That night, she went to the temple courtyard at the hour when the dayless day was supposed to begin. She stood in the center of the stones and waited for the weight of the calendar to press down on her shoulders the way it always did, the constant hum of time that lived beneath her skin. Instead, there was nothing. No hum. No weight. The gods were silent, and the calendar was silent, and Xochitl was simply a woman standing in a courtyard with the moon on her face. For one day — for one hour — nothing was expected of her. She was not the keeper of the days. She was not the daughter of her grandmother’s prophecy. She was just a body, breathing, under the stars.

She should have told the elders what she had found. She should have told them that the gap was not an error but a door, one that led to a place where the universe let go of its own ribs, just for a moment, and the silence was more beautiful than any song she had ever heard. Instead, she went back to the codex and began, meticulously, to paint in the missing sign. She chose the day-sign *rabbit*, because it was soft and small and wouldn’t argue. She filled the gap with ink, and the calendar was whole again, and the weight returned to her chest like a familiar blanket, and she hated herself a little for the peace she had lost.

But she kept the memory of that silence in her body, the way a woman keeps the shape of a loved one’s hand after they’ve gone. And sometimes, in the deepest part of the night, when the temple was empty and the count lay still, she would whisper the secret to herself — not as an error, not as a mistake, but as a door she might open again, when she was brave enough to stand in the quiet and not be anyone at all.
