The first thing you notice about Muso is that she is never quiet. Not in the way of chatter, but in the way of a held breath, a coiled spring. She sings while she pounds millet, the rhythm of the pestle becoming a bass line for a praise song for a family three villages over. She hums while she braids her daughter’s hair, the melody a winding river through a genealogy of traders and blacksmiths. She will sing the history of a tamarind tree if you let her, its planting, its first fruit, the child who fell from its branches in 1942 and the healer who set his arm. Her voice is low now, a thing of gravel and honey, with a catch in it like a hook snagged in thick cloth. It is a voice that has been broken and mended by a different hand, and everyone who hears it knows.

For a year after her grandmother, Mariama, died, Muso did not speak. The grief did not just weigh on her; it occupied her, a dense, silent passenger that closed her throat like a fist. She moved through the compound a ghost, her hands doing the work of the living while her spirit sat at the bottom of a dry well. They brought her water, they brought her food, they brought her the wailing cries of the other women, but nothing could reach her. The silence was a physical thing, a heavy, dusty curtain between her and the world. It was her grandmother’s own voice she finally heard, one evening when the heat shimmered off the red earth. It came from the baobab, the ancient, bloated sentinel behind the compound, the tree where Mariama had taken her as a girl to teach her the names of the stars. The song was not one of the epics she knew, not the founding of their clan or the bravery of a famous ancestor. It was a small, private melody, a lullaby almost, and it was singing a history she had never been told — the story of a great-aunt who had loved a Fulani herder and had been erased from the family line for it. The song was a restoration, a rebellion of memory, and it broke the fist in her throat.

When she sang at the next naming ceremony, the people wept. They wept for ancestors they had never known, for a grief that was not their own but felt like a birthright. Muso’s voice, roughened and deepened by her year of silence, carried the weight of that lost time. It gave the old stories a new ache, a new skin. But she cannot stop. She sings the lineage, and then she sings the journey, and then she sings the names of the rivers crossed, and the women who drew the water, and the children who were born on the banks. She fills every hollow of quiet with her sound, because she knows what lives in that hollow. Silence is not a rest to her; it is a grave. Her grandmother’s last gift was a song, but Muso’s fear is that if she stops singing, she will hear only the echo of the dead, and she knows, with a certainty that keeps her up at night, that the dead don’t stop singing. She is terrified she will simply forget how to hear them.
