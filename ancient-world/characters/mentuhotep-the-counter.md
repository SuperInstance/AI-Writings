The lamp is low, the wick a guttering coal. The smell of burnt sesame oil hangs in the air, mixing with the dry, mineral scent of the reed brush and the faint, living odor of the black mud that clings to the hem of Mentuhotep's linen kilt. Outside, the night air of the Two Lands is still, but inside his chest, the water is always running. He can hear it. The gurgle of it against the mud-brick walls of the storehouse, the soft, insistent slap of it against the steps of the temple. He has heard it for thirty years, ever since the season of Akhet came early and wrong, and took his father.

He is a flood counter, a scribe of the inundation. His hand is steady, his brush precise. Each morning he descends the worn stone stairs of the nilometer at Karnak, the damp cool rising to greet him, and he reads the markings on the wall like a lover reading the lines of a familiar face. The water is the life of the land, but to him, it is a language. It has a syntax, a grammar, a rhythm. For two decades, he has transcribed that rhythm onto clean sheets of papyrus, his columns of numbers neat and unyielding. The count tells him the world is orderly. The count tells him that no one will be taken by surprise. The count tells him his father is dead, and that it was a mistake, a deviation from the text that he has dedicated his life to correcting.

But tonight, the text is lying. Or rather, he is.

The new marks on the stone do not match the old cycles. The water has come up too high, then receded too fast, a feverish, arrhythmic pulse in the veins of the river. The silt is thinner, meaner. The farmers whisper, but they are fools. They see a low flood or a high one. Mentuhotep sees a broken sentence, a fractured arithmetic that threatens to unspool the world.

So he is making it whole again. He dips his brush into the ink, the pigment dark and heavy as a secret. He blots it carefully, then writes. A small correction here, a slightly altered measurement there. The new number is plausible. It fits the curve. It tells the story that should be told. The papyrus feels sacred in his hands. He is not lying, he tells himself. He is preserving the truth. He is holding the flood back with the sheer force of his will, with the weight of his perfect columns. If he can count it correctly, he can keep it from changing. If he can write the number he needs to see, the water will obey.

His father was a brewer, a big man with flour always in his beard, a man who trusted the sky and the feel of the air. He laughed at the nilometer. He was by the riverbank when the water arrived, quick and silent as a thought, and it took him without a sound. Mentuhotep did not see it happen. He only knew the numbers, which had been wrong that year. His father died because the count failed. Numbers do not weep, but they can drown a man.

He sets the brush down. His hand is trembling. The ink drips, a tiny black teardrop, but it lands beside the column, not on it. He has corrected the record. In the morning, the censors will see no flaw. The world will be safe for one more day. He looks out the window. The Nile is a black, glinting ribbon under the moon, and it looks placid, beautiful, still. He knows better. He knows what he has done. He has traded a catastrophe for a lie, and he feels, with a terrible, hollow certainty, that the lie is the only thing holding the water back. His heart beats a slow, heavy rhythm, the rhythm of a count he cannot yet name. He is a man at a desk at night, making sure no one is surprised. He is a man keeping the world from ending. He is a man who will never, ever look at the river again.
