Yuki’s hands moved like water over the inkstone, a ritual older than her own breath. The brush dipped, paused, and came alive — a line about the first frost on the chrysanthemums, the cry of a wild goose against the paling sky, the ache of a lover’s sleeve grown cold. The syllables fell into their pattern as naturally as rain finding the hollows of a leaf. It was flawless. It always was. She set the brush down and looked at the poem, and something in her chest did not move. It had been that way for so long she could no longer tell if the stillness was a wound or a scar.

The court called her a genius, and they were not wrong. They were wrong about what it meant. Yuki could compose a poem for any occasion in the time it took a candle to gutter — a greeting for the Empress, a farewell for a departing minister, a lament for a dead sparrow the emperor’s daughter had loved. She knew every seasonal word, every pillow word, every allusion to the *Kokinshū* and beyond. She knew the rules so deeply that she no longer had to think of them, and that was the trouble. The perfect poem asked nothing of her. It was a mirror that returned no face, a bell with no clapper, a pressed flower — beautiful, correct, and utterly without a single living cell.

At night, when the screens were drawn and the serving girls had gone, Yuki would sit by the single lamp and open the locked box under her futon. Inside were sheets of rough paper, covered in her own crooked hand. No meter. No season. No form. Sometimes the words were barely legible, blurred where tears had fallen, or where the ink had been smudged by the heel of her palm in the fury of writing them. They were ugly. They were raw. They said *I am afraid* and *I am so tired* and *I do not know what I am for.* They screamed into the silence of her small room, and the silence screamed back. And she knew, with the terrible clarity of someone standing at the edge of a deep well, that these ugly, broken, honest poems were the only real ones she had ever written.

The imperial contest was in three months. She had been chosen, and she would go, and she would produce something flawless. She would lose, because she always did, because the judges — learned, discerning, ruthless — could smell the absence of a heartbeat in a poem the way a dog smells fear. But she would lose with dignity, with beauty, with a text they would copy into anthologies and call *exemplary.* And the locked box would grow heavier, and the poems inside it would press against the wood like floodwater against a dam.

Her grandmother had once told her, when Yuki was a girl and already perfect at eight years old: *The perfect poem has no cracks for the light to get in.* Yuki had not understood then. She understood now. She sat in the lamplight, the flawless poem before her, and she went to the box, and she took out the newest sheet — the one that said *I am a pressed flower and the light cannot find me* — and she read it twice, and she put it back.

In three months, she would lose. But tonight, alone, she held the ugly poem in her hands, and for a moment — just a moment — something in her chest moved. It was not enough. It was everything.
