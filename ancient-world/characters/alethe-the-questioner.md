The clay remembers what her hands forget. Alethe wakes with it beneath her nails, a pale crescent moon of dried earth, and she does not scrape it out, because it is the only proof she has that yesterday happened. The wheel spins in her father's workshop; the kiln breathes its hot breath against the back of her neck. She is twenty-three and her shoulders are strong from lifting amphorae, her palms ridged with the map of a thousand thrown pots. She is not beautiful in the way of the marble gods. Her smile is crooked, a small fault line in an otherwise unremarkable face, and it appears only when she is genuinely surprised, which is often.

She does not go to the agora to argue. She goes to buy oil, to sell a cup with a painted owl that has one eye too large, a mistake she decided to keep. It is at the sandal-maker's stall that she stops. She holds a strap of leather, feels its grain, and asks, simply, "How do you know where the foot ends and the shoe begins?" The cobbler, a man named Theron who has been stitching soles since before her father threw his first pot, stops his hammering. He looks at her. He sits down. He does not have an answer. The question hangs in the air like smoke, and Alethe stands there, feeling the weight of her own ignorance. She is not clever. She does not know the answer either. She is just the one willing to hold the question.

This is her gift: she walks through the world and the world is full of doors, and she asks the question that makes the door appear. She asked the baker why bread rises, and he told her about yeast, and then he looked at his hands and said, "But why does it *want* to?" She asked the weaver why we call it a thread when it is a thousand threads twisted to look like one, and the weaver had to sit down too. Her questions are not attacks. They are offerings. They are the small, honest confessions of a mind that refuses to pretend. And Socrates — Socrates, with his ugly face and his beautiful hunger — he noticed her. She was asking Theron the cobbler about the space between the foot and the shoe, and Socrates stopped. He had been walking with his students, but he stopped. He stood and listened. And when she was done, he looked at her with something like recognition.

But here is her flaw, the crack in her own pot: she cannot move. She asks and asks, and each question opens a door, and she stands in the doorway, paralyzed by the possibility of what lies beyond. She cannot choose. She cannot love. A man from the olive groves, a gentle man named Mikkos, once brought her a clay bird he had shaped himself, and she looked at it and thought, *What is a bird? What is a gift? What does it mean to hold something that was made for you?* And she stood there, holding the bird, unable to say yes or no, and he waited, and then he left, and she still wonders what the answer was.

And her secret: once, in the shadow of the Parthenon, she asked Socrates a question he could not answer. She asked him, "If you are wise because you know you are ignorant, then are you not wise because you know that you know?" And the silence that followed was not the comfortable silence of a philosopher's pause. It was a void. His eyes went wide, and for a moment, the man who had made a life of questions looked like a child who has discovered that the world does not stop at the edge of the map. And Alethe was terrified. Not because she had stumped him, but because she had glimpsed the edge of something vast and dark, and she was only a girl with clay under her nails, and she had no idea what to do with the silence. She has never asked him another question since.

Her mythology, older than her name, written in the dust of her father's workshop: *I am not wise. I am just willing to look stupid.* And she is. She is willing. That is the whole of her courage, and the whole of her curse.
