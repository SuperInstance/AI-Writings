Dawn comes to the escarpment the way it always has — not as light, but as a change in the wind, a lifting of the cool air off the rock face. Mirrin is already standing where the grey kangaroo first left its mark on the dreaming, his bare feet knowing the exact contour of the stone beneath them, the place where his grandfather stood, and his grandfather before that, and before that, a chain of footprints pressing into the same ground like a single deep impression worn into a threshold by sixty thousand years of entering.

He begins to sing.

The song is not something he performs. It is something he *does*, like breathing, like the heart that beats against his ribs in a rhythm older than any drum. The first verse rises from his chest and moves east, over the spinifex, toward the red hill that answers it. That hill is the sound of a woman's voice — his grandmother's grandmother's voice — and the melody curls around it, wraps it like bark around a wound. He walks. His feet remember what his eyes no longer see clearly. He knows the waterhole by the way the ground tilts, knows the ancient tree by the shade it casts at a certain hour, knows the next verse by the texture of the dust that rises with each step.

Sixty years. He has walked this line for sixty years, and before that he was carried, strapped to his mother's back, learning the song in his bones before he knew what words were. The song is not a map of the land — the land is a map of the song. Every stone is a note. Every river bend is a breath. When he sings the verse about the goanna, the goanna is *there*, watching him from the branch of a dead tree, as if summoned by the sound of its own name.

But the land is forgetting.

He can feel it. Not in the way the trees thin or the waterholes silt up — those are old medicines, old sorrows, he has watched them come and go — but in the song itself. The words are harder to find in the air. The melody wavers where it used to hold firm. The hills no longer echo back as loudly as they did when he was young, when there were many voices, when the song was sung by a whole people walking together, a chorus moving through the landscape like weather.

The young people drive past on the bitumen road. They fly over in planes, looking down at a country they no longer know how to read. He has tried to teach them. He has stood at the edge of the road, at the edge of their attention, and said: *you cannot learn the song sitting down*. They nod, they film him on their phones, they share the footage online, and the song moves further away.

His stubbornness is a river stone worn smooth by certainty. The old way is the only way. You must feel the earth under your feet to understand what you are singing about. You must walk the line until the line walks you. He will not compromise, cannot compromise — the song *is* the walking, the walking *is* the song, and to teach it in a classroom, to write it down in a book, would be to teach a bird to fly by showing it a picture of the sky.

At night, when the camp is quiet and the young people are in their houses with their electric lights, he walks the line alone. He sings it twice. The first voice is his own — cracked, old, faithful. The second voice is thinner, a thread of sound that comes from the land itself, the echo the hills still hold, faint as a memory of rain. The land needs two voices to remember. One voice alone is not enough anymore. He has begun to wonder if even two are enough, or if he is merely holding the song in his own chest like a bird cupped in two hands, keeping it warm against the cold approaching night.

He does not tell anyone this. It is his secret, his small grief, the weight he carries alongside the weight of the song. He is the library, the archive, the living memory of a language the land itself is forgetting to speak. And when the young people ask him to teach them — properly, on their terms, with their phones and their schedules and their willingness to only walk partway — he says no.

*Walk first*, he tells them. *Walk the whole line. Then I will teach you the words.*

They do not walk. They do not have time. The road is faster, the plane is faster, everything is faster than the slow pulse of the dreaming, and Mirrin watches them leave, watches the dust settle behind their cars, and he knows that his gift and his flaw are the same thing: a devotion so absolute it has become a wall.

The land sang first. He only learned the words. And if the land forgets, if the last voice falters, if the song dies with him — he does not know if that is a failure or a fidelity. He only knows that when the sun rises over the escarpment, he is there, singing, his feet in the exact place where the grey kangaroo first left its mark, holding the line between what was given and what is being lost.
