The oak at the heart of the grove has a voice like the turning of a great door. Elara knows this because she has spent seven years learning its grammar, the way its roots shift through the loam like a slow, patient sentence, the way its bark cracks in winter frost as a form of punctuation. The other druids—her order, her kin, her people—have spent those same seven years arguing. They have argued about the proper orientation of a ritual circle, about whether the mistletoe must be cut with a golden sickle or if bronze will suffice, about the precise inflection of a prayer meant to call down rain. They have argued so fiercely, so persistently, that the words have worn thin as old coins, the meaning rubbed away from use.

Elara does not remember the exact moment she stopped speaking. She remembers the day a dispute over the autumn equinox rites escalated into a shouting match that sent a flock of rooks shrieking from the canopy. She remembers looking at the oak, old and patient, and understanding that it had been listening to them for a century and had never once felt the need to correct anyone. She remembers the taste of her own silence, unfamiliar at first, then sweet, then necessary.

In the second year, the oak began to speak.

Not in the way the old tales described, not with a voice like thunder or the whisper of leaves. It spoke in the slow groan of its limbs adjusting to the weight of rain, in the infinitesimal creak of its trunk swaying against the wind, in the deep, mineral pulse of its roots threading through the dark earth. She felt the soil as a kind of membrane, the mycelial networks beneath her feet as a vast, interwoven conversation. She understood, for the first time, that the oak was not a single thing but a dialogue—between tree and ground, between leaf and light, between the slow drip of water through bark and the patient chemistry of rot and renewal. It was wordless, this speech, but it was *speech* nonetheless. She learned to listen the way a person learns to read a face, the way a musician learns to hear the spaces between notes.

The other druids did not notice her silence. Or rather, they noticed it the way one notices a piece of furniture has been moved—a vague discomfort, a sense that something had shifted, quickly forgotten in the next round of argument. They had stopped listening to her long before she stopped speaking.

When the seven years ended, on the night of the winter solstice, she stood before the gathered order and opened her mouth. She meant to say her name, meant to say, *I am here, I am Elara, I have learned something you need to know*. What came out was not language, not in any form the druids recognized. It was a sound like ice cracking on a frozen pond, like the deep settlement of a root through stone, like the slow, patient swallowing of a riverbank. It was the oak's speech, the grove's speech, the long and wordless liturgy of the earth itself.

The druids stared. Some covered their ears. One wept.

Elara closed her mouth. She had spent seven years learning to hear, and in doing so she had lost the ability to be heard. Her muscles of speech had atrophied; the words she once knew felt like stones in her throat, foreign objects she no longer knew how to shape. She could listen forever. She could hear the argument beneath the argument, the fear beneath the anger, the love beneath the doctrine. But she could not say any of it.

The trees were always talking. She had just had to shut up. And now, when she wanted to rejoin the world of human speech, she found she had forgotten its tongue. Her gift was a door that only opened inward. Her flaw was that she could no longer find the way out.
