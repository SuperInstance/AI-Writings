The obsidian knife caught the first light, and Xochitl watched it bleed color into the room. She had performed this same motion ten thousand times—the blade catching the dawn, the shadow falling across the bark paper, the paint-stone grinding against the palette. But this morning, the light fell wrong. It bent around the knife like water around a stone, and the shadow it cast on the reed mat was not the shadow of a blade at all.

She blinked. The shadow was gone. The knife was just a knife.

Xochitl knelt before the tonalamatl, the day-count almanac that had been in her family for seven generations. The folded bark paper stretched in a long accordion, each panel a week of the sacred count. Her fingers walked the symbols—One Crocodile, Two Wind, Three House, Four Lizard. The pigments were still bright: cochineal red, indigo blue, the yellow of maize flowers ground with resin. Her grandmother's hand had painted some of these. Her great-grandmother's. The chain of seed logging ran back into the deep time of the collective unconscious, each generation adding its observations to the predictive web.

She reached the end of the current cycle. Twelve Rabbit. The unbroken sequence of the tonalpohualli, the 260-day perception pulse that governed every breath of Tenochtitlan, every planting, every war, every marriage—the pulse that gave shape to the world by naming it.

The next panel was blank.

Xochitl's fingers stopped. She traced the edge of the pigment, the rough fiber of the bark paper. The paint was there—indigo and ochre, ready to receive the next day—but the shape beneath was wrong. She leaned closer. The fibers had been pressed in a pattern she did not recognize, a weave that seemed to move in her peripheral vision. She dragged her nail across it. The texture was wrong. It was not a gap where paint had flaked away. It was a hole that had never been filled, a space in the weave where the threads of the universe had simply... not been woven.

Twelve Rabbit ended. Two Reed began. And in between, nothing.

The absence pressed against her eyes like the pressure before a storm. She felt it in her bones, that hollow space where a day should be. The day-signs triggered their automatic responses—she knew the reflexes of Crocodile, the deep earth-powered beginning; Wind, the breath of speech and movement; House, the shelter of the body; Lizard, the abundance of the harvest. But this day had no tile. No name. No number. It was a deadband violation of the most profound kind—not a day whose energy mismatched its sign, but a day that had no sign at all.

Xochitl's hands began to shake. She reached for the codices, the accumulated prophecies of five centuries. The folded books sat in their reed chests, their spines softened by generations of oil and sweat. She pulled out the oldest, the one with the jaguar-paw binding that crumbled at the edges. She opened it to the corresponding passage, the days between Twelve Rabbit and Two Reed.

Her thumb stopped.

The text was there—the painted glyphs, the commentary, the predictions. But the symbols were inverted. The colors were reversed, red where blue should be, yellow where black. The gods' faces looked out from wrong angles, their mouths open in postures of speech but their eyes closed. She read the glyphs, and they read back.

The quiet in the room was not silence. It was the sound of something listening.

She slammed the codex shut. The sound was flat, dead—as if her hands had clapped over a void. She tried again. The same flat note. She looked at her palms. They were coated in a fine dust the color of moonlight, a dust that did not exist anywhere in her house, in her memory, in the accumulated store of human knowledge.

The day outside her window had not changed. The sun still rose. The canal still glittered. The causeway still hummed with the footfalls of merchants and priests. But Xochitl knew, with the bone-deep certainty of a woman who had spent forty years reading the pulse of the world, that she was now living in the absence. The day that had no name was happening anyway, and the calendar had not made it so.

She stood. The room felt tilted, as if the earth had shifted beneath her feet. She thought of her grandmother, who had taught her the counting. She thought of the chain of seed logging, the generations of observation that connected her to the first daykeeper who had looked at the sky and seen the pattern of time itself. She thought of the deadband, the gap where the world's expectations failed, and she understood at last that sometimes it was not the day that violated the pattern.

Sometimes the pattern was the day. And this day, this absent day, this day that had never been logged or named or harvested—this day was alive, breathing, watching her from between the folds of the calendar.

She reached for her knife. The obsidian edge was still wet with dawn. She did not know what she intended to do with it. She only knew that the day would not end until she had given it a name.
