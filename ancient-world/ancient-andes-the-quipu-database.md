The alpaca’s wool was damp against Tika’s fingers as she drew the final strand through her hand. The beast shifted its weight, patient, chewing cud that smelled of wet earth and ichu grass. Above them, the sky was a bruise of slate and silver, the peaks of the Andes holding up the clouds like a god’s table. It had rained twice this week. The maize in the terraces below the village stood green and tall, but the soil drank greedily, and the priests had begun to murmur.

She did not murmur. She counted.

The quipu lay across her lap like a patient child, its primary cord thick and ochre, dyed with the root of the achiote bush. From it hung a forest of pendant strings—some the red of sunset, some the white of llama bone, some the deep blue of the lake at dawn. Each knot she tied was a word; each twist, a breath. Her fingers moved with the memory of her grandmother’s grandmother, the knowledge pressed into her skin like the stone of the terraces against the mountain.

*Rainfall on the western slope: nine units.* She tied a figure-eight knot in the blue strand. *Chasqui arrival from Cusco: fourth hour, before the sun crested.* A long knot on a white string. *Llama births: seven. Deaths: two.* Red strings, knotted in a cluster near the main cord.

The chasqui had come at dawn, his chest heaving, the message quipu wrapped in a cloth of vicuña wool. He had dropped to one knee before her, his sandals cracked, his face a mask of dust and exhaustion. He had run ninety miles from the royal road, up and over the pass where the wind screamed like a condor’s cry. She had taken the message, unwound its knots with her eyes, and recorded it. A tax census. A festival date. A warning that the northern bridge was washed out.

The relay was the empire’s heartbeat. Twenty-five thousand miles of stone and packed earth, hairpin turns cut into the living rock, handrails carved where the cliff fell away to nothing. The qhapaq ñan did not merely connect villages; it connected memory. Every runner, every post, every relay was a pulse sent down the arteries of the world. She had stood at the edge of the road as a child, watching the dust rise in the distance, and her grandfather had said: *The road is a loom, Tika. The runners are the shuttle. And you, little one, are the weaver of the pattern.*

She had not understood then. She understood now.

Night fell, and the village gathered at the edge of the ceremonial plaza. Torches of tar and ichu grass spit sparks into the black. The priests wore jaguar pelts and masks of hammered gold, their eyes gleaming from the sockets. But it was not the sun they watched tonight. It was the Pleiades—the little stars, the seed-gatherers, the seven sisters who danced in the southern sky.

The High Priest raised his arms. The crowd went silent. Even the dogs hushed, their ears pricked.

Tika looked up. The stars were there, but they were faint. Dim. As if a veil of dust had been thrown across the sky. She felt the cold crawl up her spine like a spider.

*The rains will be late,* she thought. *The rains will be late, and the potato planting must be delayed.*

She returned to her hut, the quipu heavy in her hands. The archive was a shelf of clay jars, each one sealed with wax and painted with the symbol of the year. She broke the seal on the oldest jar—the one her grandmother had told her never to open unless the sky demanded it. Inside, the cords were brittle, the colors faded to ghosts. She unrolled the largest quipu, its primary cord thick with age, and began to read.

Her fingers moved over the knots. Rainfall. Llama counts. The arrival of chasqui at the fourth hour. A bridge washed out in the north.

She stopped.

The pattern was identical. Knot for knot, color for color, string for string. The same rainfall, the same births, the same deaths. Two hundred years ago, another quipucamayoc had recorded the same days, the same sky, the same dimming of the seven sisters.

She had heard the story as a child. The Great Famine. The years of cold and hunger, when the terraces lay fallow and the people ate the bark of trees. The empire had prepared—had stored grain in the qolqa warehouses, had moved herds to lower pastures, had rationed the maize so that no one starved. They had survived because they had read the pattern.

The question now hung in the air like the smoke of a dying fire.

Would they prepare this time?

Tika knelt on the earthen floor, the old quipu and the new quipu spread before her like the wings of a great bird. She ran her fingers over the knots again, tracing the same sequence, the same rhythm, the same breath of the world returning to itself. The stars were dim. The rains would delay. The pattern was repeating.

She stood. She wrapped the new quipu in a cloth of vicuña wool. She opened the door of her hut and stepped into the cold, vast night. The road lay before her, pale as bone in the starlight, running south to Cusco.

A chasqui would come at dawn. She would be ready.
