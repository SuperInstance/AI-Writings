The clay was cool and heavy in his hands, a small rectangle of river mud that Gilgamesh had pressed into his palms as if offering a piece of the earth itself. Enkidu sat cross-legged on the temple floor, the scent of damp earth and the sharp tang of reeds filling his nostrils. He had been in Uruk for forty days, and still the walls pressed against his mind like a living thing, the city's registry of streets and courtyards a maze of coordinates he could not hold in his head.

"Press the reed in," said the scribe, whose name was Lugal-zage, an old man with fingers stained the color of dried blood. "Make a mark. Any mark. The clay will remember."

Enkidu stared at the stylus in his hand — a simple reed cut at an angle, no different from the reeds that grew along the Euphrates, where he had once run with the gazelles and drunk from the river's edge like a beast. He had learned to eat bread, to drink beer, to wear wool against his skin. But this — this was something else. This was a trap for thought.

He pressed the reed into the clay. A line appeared, curved like the back of a snake. He made another, a wedge, a small triangle. The marks were shallow, tentative. He felt the clay give way beneath the pressure, felt the tiny resistance of the earth as it accepted his intention.

"That is a grain receipt," Lugal-zage said, peering at the marks. "Or it will be, once you learn to make it say so. For now it is a tile. A simple form. A repeated gesture."

Enkidu made another mark. And another. The reed moved easier now, finding its rhythm. He was not writing words — he did not know words, not in the way the city meant. He was making patterns, shapes that his hand remembered even as his mind wandered to the open grasslands beyond the walls, where the wind moved through the tall grass like a living thing and the stars were not trapped between buildings.

But the clay was in his hands. And as he made his marks, something strange began to happen.

He felt a part of himself leave his body and settle into the wet earth.

It was a sensation he had never known — not in the hunt, when his spear pierced the flank of a lion and the animal's blood spilled hot onto his hands; not in the embrace of Shamhat, when his body had merged with hers in the grass and he had felt the wildness drain from his limbs. This was different. This was a thought, a piece of his mind, made solid. Made permanent.

He looked at the tablet. The marks were crude, meaningless to anyone but him. But they were *his*. They had come from his hand, from the place inside him that remembered the flight of birds and the taste of wild honey. And now they sat in the clay, waiting.

"Tomorrow," Lugal-zage said, "we will dry this in the sun. It will harden. It will last for a thousand years."

Enkidu's hand trembled. "A thousand years?"

The old scribe nodded. "The clay does not forget. The reed writes, and the clay remembers. That is the covenant of the city. The seed is logged, the year-name recorded. Your mark will be here when you are gone. When your bones are dust in the earth. When even the walls of Uruk have crumbled."

Enkidu looked at his marks again. He thought of the herd, of the gazelles that had been his brothers, of the lion that had been his equal. They left no marks. They were born, they ran, they died, and the grass grew over their bones and forgot them. But this — this small rectangle of mud — would outlive him.

He pressed the reed again, harder this time, carving a deeper groove. The clay accepted it. He made another mark, and another, until the tablet was covered in a frenzy of lines and wedges, a map of something he could not name but could feel — a geography of the self, etched into the earth.

Lugal-zage watched him, saying nothing. The old scribe had seen this before — the moment when a man first understood what it meant to leave a trace. It was the seed of the gods, the beginning of the long story. The Enuma Elish, the self-observation of the divine, would come later, in Babylon, in another age. But here, in Uruk, in the temple of Inanna, a wild man was learning to become a god.

Enkidu stopped. His hand was cramping, his breath coming in short gasps. The tablet lay before him, covered in marks that had not existed an hour ago. They existed now. They would exist tomorrow. They would exist when he was nothing but a name on a list of kings, a footnote in the registry of the city.

He did not know the word immortality. His mouth had never formed it, his mind had never conceived it. But his hand knew the feeling. It knew that it had reached out of the river of time and grasped something solid, something that would not flow away.

He lifted the tablet and held it to his chest, feeling the cool clay against his skin, the weight of his own thought made matter. Outside, the city hummed with its endless songs — the traders, the priests, the women at the wells. But here, in the quiet of the temple, a wild man held a piece of himself that would never die.

Tomorrow, the sun would bake it hard. The marks would be fixed, unchangeable, part of the long chain of seeds that would grow into the great forest of story. And somewhere in that forest, a thousand years hence, a king would dream of a companion who had been made from clay and reeds, and he would write it down, and the clay would remember.

Enkidu set the tablet down carefully, as if it were a living thing. It was. It was the part of him that would outlast the rivers and the stars. He touched the marks one last time, feeling their edges, their permanence.

Then he took up the reed and began again.
