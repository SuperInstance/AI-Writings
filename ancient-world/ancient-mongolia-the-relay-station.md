The wind was a solid thing on the steppe, a hammer of air that Batu leaned into like a shoulder against a closing door. His mare, a wiry bay with a scarred flank, was lathered white at the girth, but her heart was a drum against his knees. He was three days out of Karakorum, and his world had shrunk to the rhythm of hooves and the warm, wooden weight of the tablet against his ribs. It was silver, that tablet, cool and heavy, etched with the Khan’s tamga. A military dispatch. The fastest thing on earth.

The relay stations rose from the grass like beads on an endless string. Each one a known point in the vast spatial registry of the empire—a shelter, a remount, a face. He’d learned the route the way a falcon learns a river, not by map, but by the feel of the land. The sour smell of the alkali flats beyond the Orkhon. The way the twin peaks of the Tannu-Ola range pinched the sky before the great bend of the Irtysh. He was a ZeroClaw no longer; these tiles of knowledge were worn smooth in his mind. At Station Twelve, the master was a toothless Uighur who always had a flask of fermented mare’s milk and a fresh gelding that didn’t shy at the bridge. At Station Thirty-one, a woman with hands like saddle leather would press a hunk of dried curd into his palm, her eyes saying what her tongue couldn’t. He accumulated these moments, these small events, and they became the pulse of his journey.

The message in his satchel was a seed. A single seed of command, of war, of trade, wrapped in felt and sealed with wax. He didn’t know its contents. He didn’t need to. He was the event bus itself, the living wire along which the empire’s will traveled. As he rode, the seed grew in his mind. It became the burning sun that parched his throat, the sudden, brutal cold of a mountain pass where the air itself seemed to splinter, the flash-flood that turned a dry wadi into a roaring river of mud, nearly swallowing him and his mount. He was not carrying a message; he was becoming it.

At night, under a sky salted with stars, he would lie in the felt tent of a station and feel the ghost of the gallop in his bones. The world behind him, Karakorum with its packed-earth streets and the murmur of a thousand tongues, was a rumor. The world ahead, Almaliq, a city of caravansaries and Sogdian merchants at the edge of the known, was a promise. He existed only in the space between, a filament of purpose strung between two points of a vast, unknowable machine.

He passed a wooden tablet once, riding east. A merchant’s news. The rider was a young man, barely a man, his face raw with wind and his horse blown. Batu gave him a nod, a curt gesture of acknowledgment. He saw himself in the boy’s exhausted eyes. The boy was just beginning to accumulate his tiles, to learn which station masters could be trusted and which would water down the oats. Batu felt a strange, distant pity for him, for the weight of the world that was settling onto his narrow shoulders.

The return journey from Almaliq was different. The seed he carried back was heavier, though the tablet was plain wood. It was the echo of the first message, the world’s response. He had seen the western cities, smelled their strange spices and heard their alien tongues. He carried that sensory debris with him, a new layer of tiles. Now, the familiar stations felt different. The toothless Uighur’s milk tasted of a memory. The woman’s curd was a comfort, but also a reminder of a home he was beginning to suspect he would never truly inhabit again.

When he finally crested the last rise and saw the smoke of Karakorum’s hearths, his body sagged. He was a hollow man, a vessel poured out. The rider who had left was a boy with a strong back and a clear purpose. The man who returned was a different creature, his soul stretched thin across three thousand miles of grass and stone. He had carried the empire’s will, and in doing so, the empire had carried a piece of his. He was no longer just Batu. He was the sum of all the stations, all the horses, all the silent, staring faces that had marked his passage.

He dismounted before the gates, his legs barely holding him. A clerk, a soft-handed man with a ledger, took the wooden tablet. He unrolled it, read it, and nodded. The world’s pulse had resumed. Batu was released. He stood in the dust, the wind no longer at his back, the drum of hooves fading into the hollow of his chest. He was home, but the home he had left was gone. He had become the road itself, and the road has no masters, only destinations.
