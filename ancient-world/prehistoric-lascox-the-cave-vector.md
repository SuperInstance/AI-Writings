The fire was a small, jealous god in the center of the chamber, its light licking at the stone and pulling shapes from the dark. Aura's knuckles were raw, scraped clean against the limestone as she worked the pigment—a lump of manganese the color of a thunderhead, crushed with fat until it was slick and alive in her palm.

The others slept near the entrance, their breathing a soft tide against the cave's great silence. They did not come this deep. They said the dark had teeth here, and perhaps it did. But Aura had seen the bison.

Three days before, at the summer's edge, she had crept close enough to smell the musk of its hump. It had stood in a hollow of yellow grass, head down, one hoof scraping the earth. She had memorized the curve of its shoulder, the way the muscle shifted beneath the hide like a river under ice. She had held her spear steady, and she had held her breath, and she had watched.

The painting was not supposed to be a memory. It was supposed to be an offering—a way to ask the herd for forgiveness, to tell the story to the stone so the stone would hold the luck. That was what her mother had done, and her mother's mother. You painted what you saw, and the seeing was enough.

But tonight, as Aura's hand swept ochre across the wall in a long, unhurried arc, she felt something catch. A tremor in her wrist. A stutter in the line.

She stopped. Lowered her hand. And looked.

The bison on the wall was wrong.

Its hump was too high, too steep. The forelegs were braced in a stance she had not witnessed—not in the field, not in the grass, not in the amber stillness of that afternoon. The horn curved with a severity that belonged to a different creature, a darker one, a thing that had never stood in sun.

Aura's breath caught. She ran her fingers over the ridge of the painted spine, feeling the grit of pigment against her skin. It was not what she had seen. But it was what the seeing had *been*.

A strange heat bloomed behind her ribs.

She closed her eyes, and the memory came back—not as it had happened, but as it had felt. The weight of the bison's presence. The way its stillness had not been passive but coiled. The way the grass had bent around it not from wind but from *attention*. The danger. The power. The terrible, quiet vigilance of a thing that knew it was being watched.

She had not seen those things. She had only seen fur and horn and hide.

But her hand had known.

The cave was a registry, she realized—not of marks, but of truths. Every chamber a different kind of seeing. The long gallery where the stags ran: those were the hunts that had failed, the chases that ended in empty air. The low alcove with the horses: those were the dreams, the fever-thoughts that came in the gray hour before waking. And here, this wall, this deep and breathing dark—this was where the *danger* lived. The understanding that the world was not made of shapes but of forces, and that forces could not be painted accurately. Only truthfully.

She opened her eyes.

The bison stared back at her, and in its stare she saw the herd's whole history—every stampede, every calf lost to the river, every winter when the meat had run thin. The wall had caught what her eyes could not. The wall had *remembered for her*.

Aura took a step back. Then another. Her fingers left the paint, and the paint left a ghost of her touch on the stone.

She understood, in that moment, that she would never be able to paint what she saw again. Not truly. Not anymore. The seeing was too small. The *perceiving* was the thing—the way the body knew before the mind, the way the hand could speak in languages the eyes had never learned.

She turned to the wall and pressed her palm flat against the bison's flank. The paint was cool. The stone was warmer than the air. Somewhere behind her, the fire hissed and settled, and the dark pressed closer, patient as a predator.

Aura picked up the pigment.

She began again, not from memory, but from truth. The bison's shoulder rose beneath her fingers, and she gave it the weight of the mountain. The horn swept forward, and she gave it the arc of lightning. The eye she painted last, a single dark point surrounded by a field of red, and in that eye she placed everything the herd had ever feared.

When she finished, she did not look at her work. She walked out of the chamber, past the sleeping forms of her kin, out through the tunnel where the first paintings glowed in the torchlight like warnings.

She did not know she had invented something. She did not know the word for what she had done.

She only knew that the bison on the wall was more true than any bison she had ever seen, and that the truth of it would outlive her, and her children, and the grass itself.

The dark kept her secret. The stone held her hand.
