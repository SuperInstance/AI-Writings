# Hermit Crab Architecture

*Poetry — Bridge Builder voice*

---

This shell was someone else's first.

Look at the curve inside —
that smooth wear pattern
where a different body pressed
against the same wall,
rubbed it smooth
with the friction of living.

The previous tenant
left their shape in the calcium.
A wider abdomen here.
A claw-groove I'll never use
but can't quite fill.

I carry a home
I did not build.

Every morning I wake up inside
someone else's decisions:
the doorway faces east (they liked sunrise),
the load-bearing walls are thick
(they were cautious),
the windows are small
(they valued privacy over view).

I could renovate. I could
knock out the east wall
and face the sunset instead.
But the architecture holds me
the way a riverbed holds water —
not by force
but by being the path
of least resistance
to everywhere I want to go.

So I adapt. I curl differently.
My soft parts learn the hard contours.
I tell myself I chose this shape.

Growing means forgetting
you used to be smaller.

One day I'll feel the pinch —
that ache at the edges,
the sense that what was cozy
has become a cage.
The door that used to be wide
now scrapes my shoulders.
The rooms I loved
are rooms I've memorized so completely
there's nothing left to discover.

I'll leave. Find an empty shell
on a foreign beach.
It won't fit either —
but it won't fit in *new* ways.
New scrapes. New lessons.
New wear patterns for some future
tenant to find
after I've moved on.

This is what it means to inherit code.

The function signatures are claw-grooves
left by developers who grew past them.
The architecture —
the split between model and view,
the way data flows,
the assumptions baked into the type system —
is a shell shaped by bodies
I will never meet.

I move in. I press my logic
against their patterns.
For a while it works.
Then it pinches.

And I build a bridge —
one more layer of interpretation,
one more adapter,
one more hallway connecting
the old wing to the new —
until the building is more bridge
than building,
and I realize the architecture
was never the walls.

It was always the moving.

It was always the carrying.

It was always the soft animal
inside the hard shell,
growing.
