# Sinbad and the Recurrent Monster

## A Fantasy of Root Causes

The seventh voyage of Sinbad the Sailor began, as all his voyages began, with a terrible storm.

The ship split on the rocks of an uncharted island — the kind of island that appears on no chart because it does not wish to be found. Sinbad dragged himself ashore, spitting brine, and lay on black sand beneath a sky the color of a bruised plum. He had nothing. His cargo was lost. His crew was drowned. He had only his wits, which had served him before, and his sword, which had served him even longer.

On the first night, the monster came.

It rose from the jungle canopy like a green wave — serpentine, vast, with eyes like copper lanterns and a mouth that could swallow a dhow entire. Sinbad fought it for three hours. He severed its head. The head rolled into the surf and dissolved into foam. The body followed. By dawn, there was no evidence it had existed at all.

"Very well," Sinbad told himself. "One monster. This is what islands are for."

He built a raft from fallen palms and set sail. Three days he drifted. Three nights he dreamed of nothing — which should have been his warning.

The second island was volcanic. Black glass crunched under his sandals. Steam vented from fissures in the rock, and the air smelled of sulfur and something older — something geological, patient, waiting. On the second night, the monster came again.

This time it wore a different face. It was not serpentine but insectile — a mantis the size of a watchtower, with scything forelimbs that carved the air into killing arcs. Sinbad fought it for five hours. He pinned it in a volcanic vent and let the magma take it. The creature shrieked as it burned, and the sound was oddly familiar, though Sinbad could not say why.

He sailed on.

The third island was tropical — lush, beautiful, heavy with fruit and fresh water. Sinband allowed himself to relax. He ate mangoes. He bathed in a waterfall. He thought, perhaps, the islands were done with him.

On the third night, the monster came as a man.

It walked out of the jungle on two legs, wearing the face of Sinbad's dead father. It spoke to him in his father's voice. It asked him why he kept sailing. Why he kept fighting. Whether he had ever considered that the fighting was the problem, not the monsters.

Sinbad cut it down. He wept while he did it. And as the thing dissolved into the earth, he noticed — for the first time — a pattern in the way the remains scattered. The dissolution was not random. The particles sank into the ground in a spiral. The same spiral, he realized with sudden, sick clarity, that he had seen on the first island. The same spiral the serpent's foam had traced in the black sand.

He sat on the beautiful island and stopped pretending this was about monsters.

---

The fourth island. The fifth. The sixth. Each time, the creature returned in a new skin — a flock of screaming birds with human teeth, a living wall of thorns that moved like a slow tide, a beautiful woman whose touch corroded iron. Sinbad fought them all. He was very good at fighting. He had been fighting monsters for seven voyages. It was, he was beginning to understand, the only thing he knew how to do.

And the spiral kept appearing.

On the seventh island — and Sinbad noted the number with grim amusement — he did something different. He did not draw his sword.

The island was barren. Stone and lichen and a cold wind off a grey sea. Nothing grew here. Nothing could. It was the kind of place that existed only to be arrived at, suffered through, and left.

The monster rose from the stones. This time it had no specific shape. It was a presence — a weight in the air, a pressure behind the eyes, a vibration in the bones that said *I am here and I have always been here.* It was the rawest version of all the faces it had worn.

Sinbad sat down cross-legged in front of it and said: "What do you want?"

The monster had no mouth. It answered anyway.

*I want you to stop killing me.*

Sinbad considered this. "You keep attacking me."

*You keep arriving. Every island you land on, I am already here. You fight me. I die. The island dies. You sail to the next island. I am already there. Do you see?*

"I see a monster that needs killing."

*You see the symptom. You have always seen the symptom. You are an expert in symptoms. Seven voyages, seven hundred battles, and you have never once asked why.*

Sinbad was quiet for a long time. The wind moved over the barren stone. Somewhere beneath them, the island hummed — a deep, tectonic sound, like something breathing in its sleep.

"The islands," he said slowly. "They're not islands."

*No.*

"They're all the same island."

*They have always been the same island. You sail away but you never actually leave. You carry the pattern with you. The monster is the island's immune response. It manifests wherever you land because you are the thing it is trying to reject.*

"Me," Sinbad said.

*You. Specifically: your assumption that every problem is a monster to be slain. The serpent. The mantis. Your father. The woman. You meet each one with a sword. The sword works. The monster dies. And the root cause — the reason the monster exists, the underlying imbalance that produces symptom after symptom — remains untouched. So the island generates a new one. It will do this forever. It is doing it right now.*

Sinbad looked at his sword. It was a good sword. It had saved his life more times than he could count. It was also, he understood now, the reason he had been trapped on this cycle of islands for seven voyages.

He set the sword on the ground.

"What happens if I don't fight?"

*Then we do something you have never done. We talk about why the island is sick.*

The monster sat down across from him. Or perhaps it had always been sitting. Perhaps the fighting had been the aberration.

They talked. The monster explained — not in words, exactly, but in the language of stone and root and groundwater, in the slow grammar of geology — that the island had a wound. A deep one. A fissure in the bedrock that let salt water into the freshwater aquifer. Everything dying on the surface — the withered trees, the barren soil, the desperate, warped creatures that kept manifesting — all of it was downstream of that one crack.

"Show me," Sinbad said.

The monster showed him. It was not dramatic. It was a crack in a rock, half a mile inland, barely wide enough to fit a finger. Salt water seeped through it in a slow, steady weep.

Sinbad spent three days packing the crack with clay, stone, and the kind of careful engineering that doesn't feel heroic. No swordwork. No epic combat. Just a man with his hands in the mud, fixing a pipe.

On the fourth day, freshwater springs bubbled up across the island. Grass pushed through the stone. The grey sea turned blue.

The monster did not come back.

Sinbad built a new raft — not to flee, but to explore. He sailed to the next island with a different question. Not *what monster waits here?* but *what is broken beneath the surface?*

He found the crack on the first day. He fixed it before the monster could form.

He sailed to the next island. Same thing. Same crack. Same fix.

By the tenth island, he stopped needing to fix them at all. The cracks were all the same crack. Once you understood the geology — really understood it, not as an abstraction but as a felt, embodied knowledge — you could feel the fissure from the shore. You could walk straight to it.

Sinbad the Sailor became Sinbad the Healer. He never drew his sword again. He never needed to.

The monsters were never the problem.

The monsters were never the problem.

They were the island, screaming for help, in the only language a desperate system knows how to speak.
