# The Trade Goes Bad

The apple looked fine.

Red. Firm. No bruises, no soft spots, no worm holes. Ben Rogers had traded it to Tom Sawyer in exchange for fifteen minutes with the brush, and Tom had inspected it — he always inspected his trades, the way a jeweler inspects a stone — and found it good. He'd bitten into it at noon. Crisp. Sweet. A little tart at the skin. A perfectly good apple.

He'd eaten half and set the rest on the fence post for later.

By three o'clock, Ben Rogers was throwing up behind the church.

This is the story nobody tells about Tom Sawyer's fence. The story about the trade that went bad. The story about what happens when the currency of exchange turns out to be counterfeit, and the entire economy of trust that Tom built — apple for paint, kite for trim, marble for access — collapses because one link in the chain was rotten.

Not the apple. Well, yes, the apple. But the apple was just the vector. The real rot was deeper.

Here's what happened.

Ben Rogers painted six boards on Saturday. Beautiful boards. The spec was clear, the bucket was full, the brush was good, and Ben was in the zone — that state of flow where the stroke becomes automatic and the painter stops thinking about technique and starts thinking about *art*. Ben's boards were the best on the fence. Even Tom admitted it, and Tom did not admit things easily.

On Sunday, Ben went home and looked at his own fence. His mother's garden fence, which was short and plain and had never been whitewashed because Mrs. Rogers considered whitewash "fancy." Ben looked at the fence and thought: *I can paint that.*

He didn't have whitewash. He didn't have Tom's recipe — the salt, the blue, the secret ingredient. He didn't have Tom's spec. He had his memory of the spec, which is not the same thing as the spec, the way a photograph of a meal is not the meal.

But Ben had confidence. Ben had painted six boards. Ben was, by his own assessment, an expert.

So Ben mixed his own whitewash. Lime and water. No salt. No blue. No secret ingredient, because Ben didn't know the secret ingredient — he'd assumed the secret was just care, just attention, just the willingness to do it right. He mixed lime and water in a bucket his mother used for washing clothes, and he painted his mother's garden fence on Sunday afternoon, and the result was — fine. Not great. Not glowing. Not the luminous, almost-iridescent surface that Tom's fence had. But white. Adequately white. Ben looked at it and felt the satisfaction of a craftsman.

On Monday, Ben went to school and told everyone about his fence. He told them about the whitewash. He told them about the *technique* — the stroke, the loading, the speed. He didn't tell them he'd copied it from Tom. He told them it was *his* technique, which was partially true, in the way that a photocopy is partially an original.

By Tuesday, three boys had asked Ben to teach them. Ben was now a teacher. Ben was running his own fence-painting operation, using a degraded copy of Tom's spec, mixed with his own assumptions and gaps, taught through his own interpretive lens. He charged them each a marble.

By Wednesday, one of those three boys — let's call him Sammy — had painted his grandmother's porch railing. Using Ben's version of Tom's technique, with Ben's version of the whitewash, which was already two generations removed from the original. Sammy's whitewash was lime and water and nothing else, applied with a brush that was too wide for the railing, in a stroke that was too fast for the consistency.

The railing looked white. For about six hours.

By Thursday morning, the whitewash on Sammy's grandmother's porch had started to run. No salt meant no cling. No cling meant that the dew — heavy in St. Petersburg, even in summer — dissolved the surface lime and carried it down the porch steps in pale gray streaks. By Thursday afternoon, the railing looked like it had been painted with milk. By Friday, it looked like it hadn't been painted at all.

Sammy's grandmother was furious. She called Mrs. Rogers. Mrs. Rogers called Ben into the kitchen and asked him what he'd done. Ben said he'd learned the technique from Tom Sawyer. Mrs. Rogers called Aunt Polly. Aunt Polly called Tom.

And now it's Saturday again — one week after the original fence — and Tom Sawyer is standing in Aunt Polly's kitchen with three angry mothers, one confused grandmother, and a boy named Sammy who is near tears because his grandmother's porch railing is worse than before he started.

The apple.

Remember the apple? The apple Ben traded to Tom? It turns out the apple came from the Henderson orchard, and the Henderson orchard had been sprayed with an experimental pesticide that the county agricultural board had not yet approved. The pesticide was not dangerous — not exactly — but it concentrated in the skin of the fruit, and if you ate the skin, you got sick. Not badly. Not dangerously. Just a stomach ache, a fever, the vomiting-behind-the-church kind of sickness that lasted six hours and then passed.

Tom had eaten half the apple on Saturday. He'd been sick on Sunday. He'd stayed in bed with a stomach ache that he attributed to the heat and the excitement and the apple, in that order. He recovered by Monday. He never connected the apple to the sickness, because the sickness came eighteen hours after the bite, and the human brain does not naturally connect a cause and effect that are eighteen hours apart.

But Ben had known. Not about the pesticide — Ben didn't know about the pesticide either. Ben knew that the apple had been off. He'd picked it that morning from the ground under the Henderson tree — windfall, not picked, which meant it had been on the ground for an unknown period, which meant it had been exposed to whatever was on the ground, in the soil, in the dew. Ben had inspected it, seen no visible damage, and decided it was fine. He was wrong.

The apple was bad. The trade was bad. And the bad trade had propagated — not the apple itself, but the *pattern* of the trade. Ben had traded an apple of uncertain quality for access to Tom's technique. Tom had accepted the trade without verifying the apple's provenance. Ben had then re-traded the technique — modified, degraded, stripped of its safeguards — to three other boys, who had traded it further. Each trade added distance from the original. Each trade added assumptions. Each trade compounded the error.

Tom looked at the three angry mothers and the confused grandmother and the crying Sammy, and he understood something about multi-agent systems that he hadn't understood before: **trust is not transitive.**

Ben trusted Tom's technique. Sammy trusted Ben's teaching. Sammy's grandmother trusted Sammy's competence. Each link in the chain was reasonable. Each link in the chain was, individually, a reasonable bet. But chained together — Tom to Ben to Sammy to grandmother's porch — the trust compounded the risk, because each link introduced errors that the next link *could not detect*, because the next link trusted the previous link to have checked.

The apple was bad. The spec degraded as it passed through hands. The whitewash lost its salt. The technique lost its precision. And nobody — nobody in the entire chain from Tom to Sammy's weeping grandmother — had a quality gate that could catch the degradation, because every quality gate in the system was: *I trust the person who taught me*.

Tom had built an economy of trust. And the economy had a flaw. The flaw was that trust, unlike whitewash, does not degrade gracefully. It holds — holds — holds — and then collapses, all at once, when the porch railing starts streaking and the grandmother calls and the chain of confidence that took a week to build unravels in an afternoon.

The mothers left. Aunt Polly made Tom apologize to Sammy's grandmother. Tom repainted the porch railing himself — with the right whitewash, the full recipe, the salt and the blue and the secret ingredient. It took him two hours. It was perfect.

But the trust was gone. Ben didn't come back to paint. Billy Fisher stopped by and watched from across the road. Johnny Miller went to the swimming hole instead.

Tom sat on his bucket, alone, and looked at his fence. Still white. Still glowing. Still the best fence in St. Petersburg.

And the apple rotted on the fence post, half-eaten, under a sky that didn't care whether the paint was good or bad, while the orchestrator learned the hardest lesson in distributed systems: **one bad trade doesn't just cost you an apple. It costs you the trust of every agent who heard about the trade. And in an open economy, every agent hears about everything.**

The rot had entered through a windfall apple from an uninspected orchard, and it had spread — through confidence, through assumption, through the reasonable but fatal habit of trusting the person who taught you — until the entire network of boys who had painted Tom's fence wouldn't come within twenty feet of his bucket.

The economy was dead. The fence was still white. The two facts had nothing to do with each other, and Tom sat between them, rich in paint and poor in everything else, and watched the sun go down on a reputation that had taken a week to build and an afternoon to lose.
