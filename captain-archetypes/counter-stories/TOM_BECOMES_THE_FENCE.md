# Tom Becomes the Fence

On the morning of his one thousandth delegation, Tom Sawyer could not remember how to paint.

This is not a metaphor. This is a clinical description of a man — boy, still, technically, though the town had started calling him "Mr. Sawyer" somewhere around delegation six hundred — who picked up a brush, held it against a board, and found that his hand did not know what to do.

The brush was a two-inch flat. Hog bristle. Wooden handle, worn smooth. His brush. The one he'd used for the first three boards of the original fence — the boards that had launched the spec, the bucket, the crew, the road, the entire glorious apparatus that had kept St. Petersburg's fences painted for two years while Tom sat in the shade and dispatched.

Tom held the brush against the board the way you hold a pen when you've forgotten how to write. The muscle memory was gone. Not faded — *gone*. His hand knew how to hold the brush. His hand knew how to dip the brush in the bucket. His hand knew how to tap the brush on the left rim of the bucket to shed excess paint. But the stroke — the pull, the smooth top-to-bottom motion that laid the whitewash down like cream on a saucer — the stroke was missing. It had been replaced by something else, something that felt like the memory of the memory of a stroke, a ghost of a motion that his hand could describe but not perform.

He tried. He pulled the brush down the board. The whitewash went on in a jagged, uneven streak — too thick at the top, too thin at the middle, dripping at the bottom. It looked like a first attempt. It looked like the work of a boy who had never painted before.

Tom stared at the board. The board stared back. He dipped the brush. He tried again. Worse. The second board looked like someone had sneezed whitewash at it.

"Tom?" Ben Rogers was standing behind him. Ben had been dispatched to check on the gate section, and he'd found Tom — the orchestrator, the spec-writer, the bucket-mixer, the inventor of the stroke — standing in front of a board, holding a brush, with a look on his face that Ben had never seen before.

It was the look of a person who has discovered that the thing they built has eaten the thing they were.

Tom didn't answer. He dipped the brush. He tried again. The third board was the worst yet — a blotchy, streaked, clearly-amateur mess that would not have passed Tom's own spec. The spec that Tom had written. The spec that defined "done" as a smooth, even coat with no drips, no streaks, no thin spots. The spec that this board violated in every particular.

"Tom, do you want me to — " Ben started.

"No." Tom's voice was tight. Clipped. The voice of a man holding something back. "I can do this."

He could not do this.

He tried five more boards. Each was worse than the last. The technique was not just rusty — it was *absent*. The neural pathways that had once fired in sequence — dip, tap, position, pull, release, inspect — had been overwritten. Replaced. Repurposed. The pathways that remained were the dispatching pathways: the ones that read a board and judged it, the ones that looked at a painter's work and said "good" or "redo," the ones that assessed quality without producing quality.

Tom had become a reviewer. A manager. A specification machine. He could describe the perfect stroke in language so precise that a new painter could execute it on the first try. He could look at a board from twenty feet away and tell you whether the coverage was even. He could mix whitewash in his sleep — the lime, the salt, the blue, the secret ingredient, the consistency test, the finger-dip quality check. He could do everything *around* the painting. Everything except paint.

The task that brought this on was a strange one. A fence — but not a fence. A piano. Judge Thatcher's piano, which had been delivered to the courthouse on Friday and which had somehow — the details were confused; the delivery driver blamed the horse, the horse blamed nothing because it was a horse — ended up scratched. Deep scratches in the walnut veneer of the left panel. Not whitewash damage. Not paint damage. *Wood* damage. The kind of damage that needed a hand that knew how to work a surface, that knew pressure and angle and the difference between too much and not enough.

And Judge Thatcher had said, because everyone in St. Petersburg said this about everything now: "Get Tom Sawyer. Tom'll know what to do."

Tom did not know what to do. Tom knew how to dispatch someone who knew what to do. But there was no one who knew what to do. The scratch needed a craftsman — not a whitewash painter, not a fence guy, a *woodworker*. And Tom, who had spent two years becoming the world's greatest dispatcher, had not picked up a tool — any tool, for any craft — since the day he'd handed Ben Rogers the brush and sat down on the bucket.

Tom went to the courthouse. He looked at the piano. He touched the scratch with his fingertip. The walnut was smooth except where the scratch had gouged it — a rough valley in a polished plain, like a canyon seen from above.

Tom's hands knew what to do. His hands remembered something — not painting, but *working*. The feel of a surface. The resistance of wood grain. The way a tool — any tool, brush or knife or sandpaper — meets a surface and either follows the grain or fights it. His hands remembered this. His hands wanted to work.

But his hands couldn't execute. The specific, fine-motor, physical skill of repairing a scratch in walnut veneer was not in his hands. It had never been in his hands. And the skill that *had* been in his hands — the whitewash stroke — was gone, replaced by the broader, more general, more powerful skill of *managing other people's hands*.

Tom called for Ben. Ben came. Tom described the repair in detail — the angle of the knife, the grain of the walnut, the color of the filler, the pressure needed to level the surface without damaging the surrounding veneer. He described it perfectly. Ben nodded along, picked up the knife, and made the repair.

It was good. Not perfect — Ben was a painter, not a woodworker — but good. The scratch was filled. The surface was level. From three feet away, you couldn't see it.

Tom looked at the repair and felt nothing. Not satisfaction — he hadn't done it. Not pride — Ben had done it, following Tom's spec. Not jealousy — he didn't want to have done it, not exactly. What he felt was *absence*. The absence of the thing he used to feel when his own hands produced a good board. The specific, irreplaceable satisfaction of *making*.

He had traded it away. One board at a time, one delegation at a time, one dispatch at a time. Each time he handed the brush to someone else, he gained a little leverage — more output per minute, more boards per hour, more fence per Saturday. And each time he handed the brush to someone else, he lost a little of the thing that had made him hand them the brush in the first place: the ability to do it himself.

The trade had been so gradual that he hadn't noticed. The first hundred delegations, he could still paint — not as well as before, but well. The second hundred, he could paint adequately. The third hundred, he could paint if he concentrated. By the five hundredth, he needed a warmup board. By the seven hundredth, he needed three. By the one thousandth, he needed a miracle.

Tom Sawyer stood in the courthouse and looked at the repaired piano and understood what he had become.

He was not a painter. He had not been a painter for a long time. He was a *system* — a specification and dispatch and review system that happened to be housed in a human body. The body could sit on buckets. The body could eat apples. The body could point at boards and say "redo that one." But the body could not paint. The body had forgotten how.

And the thing — the terrible, iridescent, can't-look-away thing — was that the system was *better* without the painter. The system painted more fence, faster, with higher quality, than Tom the painter had ever achieved alone. The system was the whole point. Tom the painter had been a prototype. Tom the system was the product.

The product couldn't paint.

Tom walked home in the dark. He passed his fence — the original, the one that had started everything. Three coats. Still gleaming. Ben's boards on the north section, smooth and perfect. Billy's gate, repainted after the flaking incident, clean and tight. Johnny's trellis, delicate and precise.

Tom's boards — the first three, the ones he'd painted himself on that first Saturday morning — were still there. Board one, board two, board three. He stopped and looked at them. They were good. Not the best on the fence — Ben's were better. But they were *his*. His brush, his hand, his stroke. A record of the last time Tom Sawyer had put a brush against a board and known — in his hand, in his wrist, in the muscle memory that lived below thought — exactly what to do.

He touched board one. The whitewash was smooth under his fingertips. Two years of rain and sun hadn't faded it. It was still there. Still his.

He tried to remember what it felt like to paint it. The feeling was gone. He could remember the *fact* of painting — he had stood here, he had held the brush, he had pulled down — but the *feeling*, the somatic memory of the stroke, the thing his hand had known, was gone. Replaced. Overwritten. Deleted to make room for the dispatching pathways, the review pathways, the management pathways that had made him the most effective boy in St. Petersburg and the most useless painter on his own fence.

Tom stood at his fence in the dark, and the fence looked back, and neither of them could help the other.

**The orchestrator who stops doing becomes the system that only describes.** The manager who stops making becomes the person who can only specify what someone else should make. The delegation engine works. It produces. It scales. And it eats — slowly, silently, board by board — the very skill it was built to amplify.

Tom had become the fence. Not the painter of the fence. Not the specifier of the fence. The fence itself — a surface that things happened *to*, that work was done *on*, that stood in the sun and received the paint that other people applied. He was the substrate. He was the wood. He was the thing that held the whitewash but could not, would not, did not know how to be the brush.

He went inside. He sat on his bed. He picked up a pencil and wrote a spec for tomorrow's work — clear, precise, complete. The spec was beautiful. The spec was perfect. A new painter could read it and produce boards that looked like Tom's boards — better than Tom's boards, if the painter was good.

Tom set down the pencil. He looked at his hands. They were clean. They hadn't touched paint in months.

The hands of an orchestrator are always clean. That's how you know it's too late.
