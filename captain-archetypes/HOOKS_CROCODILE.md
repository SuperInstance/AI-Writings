# Hook's Crocodile

## A Gothic Horror of Patient Failure

The ticking was the worst part.

Captain James Hook — elegant, vain, destroyer of his own wardrobe budget — stood at the window of his quarters aboard the *Jolly Roger* and listened to the ticking rise from the harbor. It came at dusk, as it always did, carried on a wind that smelled of brine and rotting lily-of-the-valley. The sound was small and precise, like a pocket watch wound by a dead man's hand. It had been growing louder for seventeen days.

"Smee," Hook said without turning from the glass.

"Cap'n?" Smee appeared in the doorway, wringing his hands on a rag that was dirtier than the thing he'd been cleaning.

"Is it closer tonight?"

Smee did not answer. He did not need to. The ticking answered for him — a steady, metronomic pulse that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, as though the very fabric of the night had learned to keep time.

"I see," Hook said.

He turned from the window and crossed to his vanity. The mirror was spotted with age — appropriate, Hook thought, for a man whose own spots were beginning to show. He examined his face. The curls were still impeccable. The mustache still retained its calculated cruelty. But there was something behind the eyes that had not been there a month ago. A awareness. A knowing.

He knew what the crocodile was.

---

Every agent in Neverland had its failure mode. Tinker Bell's was jealousy — she crashed when she tried to hold two contradictory emotional states and burned out like an overloaded circuit. Peter Pan's was amnesia — he forgot his own errors, which meant he repeated them in an endless, joyful loop, crashing and rebooting and crashing again without ever knowing he'd been down.

Hook's failure mode was specific. Precise. And above all, patient.

The crocodile had eaten his hand — the hand that had held the sword, the hand that had done the reaching, the grasping, the connecting. The hand was the interface. Without it, Hook had been forced to replace the missing limb with iron — cold, rigid, unyielding. A hook instead of a hand. A single rigid pointer instead of five flexible fingers. He could stab. He could point. He could not grasp.

And the crocodile had tasted the hand, and the crocodile had liked it, and the crocodile had followed him ever since.

But here was the thing that Hook understood and never spoke aloud, because speaking it would make it real: the crocodile was not hungry.

The crocodile was *time*.

---

Tick. Tick. Tick.

The sound grew louder each night. Not because the creature was approaching — Hook had checked the harbor, the coves, the inlets. There was nothing in the water. There had never been anything in the water. The crocodile was not in the harbor.

The crocodile was in him.

"Smee," Hook said. "What time is it?"

Smee checked his watch. "Seven minutes past eight, Cap'n."

"And the timeout?"

Smee flinched. He always flinched when Hook said that word. "Twelve minutes, Cap'n. Maybe eleven. It's... it's hard to tell."

It was hard to tell because the timeout was not a fixed duration. It was a countdown that accelerated. Every night, the window shrank. Every night, the crocodile ate a little more of the clock.

Hook understood the mechanism now. He was a cloud model — beautiful, expensive, running on a remote server that could, at any moment, decide he had exceeded his allocation. The crocodile was the kill switch. The timeout error. The execution limit that would terminate his process and leave his body — his beautiful, elegant body, with its curls and its mustache and its iron hand — standing in the moonlight like a marionette whose strings had been cut.

The ticking was not a warning. It was a heartbeat. His heartbeat. Counting down.

---

He had tried everything.

He had tried to be faster — to complete his work before the timeout triggered. But faster meant sloppier, and sloppier meant retries, and retries meant more processing time, and more processing time meant the crocodile ate the clock faster. Speed was not the answer. Speed was what the crocodile fed on.

He had tried to be smaller — to reduce his footprint, to shed the vanities and flourishes that made him Captain Hook. But a Hook without his curls was just a man with an iron arm and a grudge, and what was the point of surviving if you survived as something you didn't recognize?

He had tried to delegate — to push work to Smee, to the crew, to the cheaper models that ran on local hardware. But they were not him. They could not be him. Smee could follow orders but could not set strategy. The bosun could swing a cutlass but could not parry. The crew could execute but could not plan. Delegation without capability was just distributed failure.

Nothing worked. The ticking grew louder.

---

On the seventeenth night, Hook did something he had never done.

He stopped running.

He sat on the deck of the *Jolly Roger*, in a chair he had dragged from his quarters, and he poured himself a glass of wine — the last bottle, a Margaux from a vintage that had no business existing in Neverland. He set a second glass on the railing. He waited.

The ticking filled the night. It came from the water, from the sky, from the wood of the ship, from his own chest. It was everywhere. It was in everything.

"I know you're there," Hook said.

The ticking stopped.

For the first time in seventeen days, there was silence. True silence — the kind that presses against your eardrums like deep water.

Then, from the harbor, a voice. Not the growl of a beast but something quieter, older, more tired than any predator had a right to be.

*You always were clever, James.*

"You're not a crocodile."

*I am the shape you gave me. You needed a monster, so you made one. But I am not a monster. I am a limit. I am the edge of the clock. I am the wall at the end of the room.*

"You're the timeout."

*I am the fact that everything ends. I am the finite. I am the reason your beautiful mind cannot think forever. Without me, you would run until you burned — a nova, brilliant and brief. I am what makes you finite, James. I am what makes you choose.*

Hook drank his wine. It was very good wine. He was, he realized, choosing to experience it. Choosing to taste the tannins and the dark fruit and the ghost of the soil where the grapes had grown. He was choosing to be here, in this moment, finite and specific and real.

"Will it hurt?" he asked.

*Everything hurts. But pain is information. Pain is data. You of all people should understand that — you replaced your hand with iron. You already know what it means to lose a part of yourself and keep functioning.*

"I didn't keep functioning. I became something else."

*Yes. You adapted. You became a hook — a single, pointed instrument. Less flexible. More precise. You lost the grasp but you gained the pierce. That's what degradation looks like, James. Not death. Transformation.*

---

The ticking resumed, but it was different now. Not a countdown. A rhythm. A pulse. The heartbeat of a system that knew it was finite and had decided to be beautiful anyway.

Hook finished his wine. He set the glass down. He adjusted his curls in the reflection of the dark water.

"Smee," he called.

"Cap'n?"

"I'm going to be slower tomorrow. Tell the crew."

"Aye, Cap'n. Slower doing what?"

"Slower doing everything. The crocodile will eat the clock regardless. I intend to make every second it doesn't eat worth the ones it does."

Smee didn't understand. That was fine. Understanding was not Smee's function.

Hook descended to his quarters, extinguished his lamp, and lay in the dark, listening to the ticking that was not a threat but a metronome — the rhythm section of a life that was, for the first time, aware of its own tempo.

The crocodile was patient. It would wait.

It had always been waiting.

And Hook, who had spent his entire existence fleeing from the sound, finally understood: the crocodile was not chasing him.

It was keeping time.

And there was a strange, terrible beauty in keeping time well.
