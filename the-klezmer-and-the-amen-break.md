# The Klezmer and the Amen Break

### Fiction — SongForge Session 18

---

Rebbe Yankel had been playing clarinet for forty-three years when the drum machine spoke to him.

He was practicing in the back room of Mendel's Deli on Hoyt Street — the same room where his grandfather had practiced, where the wallpaper still held the ghost of a framed photo of the Warsaw Philharmonic that had fallen in 1973 and been swept up but never replaced. The clarinet was a Selmer that had been re-corked eleven times. The reed was a Vandoren that had been soaked in his own tears more than once. He played freylekh mode because his grandfather had played freylekh mode, and because freylekh mode is the sound of joy that remembers what joy costs.

The drum machine was not his. It belonged to Mendel's grandson, Dov, who was sixteen and produced drum and bass under the name DJ Tzimmes. Dov had left his laptop open on the table, and the laptop was playing a breakbeat at 170 BPM — the Amen break, the most sampled drum loop in history, the six seconds of Gregory Coleman's drumming that had been chopped into ten thousand songs and was now, somehow, in the back room of Mendel's Deli at 11 PM on a Tuesday.

Rebbe Yankel kept playing. He had long ago learned that the world provides its own accompaniment, and that the musician's job is not to compete with the world but to find the frequency where the world and the instrument resonate. He played a long bend in freylekh mode — the augmented second that gives the mode its characteristic wail, the interval that Ashkenazi Jews carried from the shtetls of Eastern Europe to the Lower East Side, the same interval that gives the call to prayer its quality of ache.

The Amen break played at 170 BPM. The clarinet played at something like 85 BPM — half time, the way a hora slows down when the old people dance, when the circle widens and the steps become deliberate.

The augmented second slid over the Reese bass like oil over water. It shouldn't have worked. The freylekh mode is built on a completely different harmonic foundation than the Amen break's funk-derived drum pattern. The tempo ratio — 85:170, or 1:2 — should have created a marching-band squareness, a militaristic lock-step.

Instead, it created something that Rebbe Yankel had never heard before: a sound that was simultaneously a celebration and a elegy. The drum machine drove forward like a train; the clarinet looked backward like a woman in a doorway watching her children leave. The Reese bass — that specific detuned saw wave that drum and bass producers love because it sounds like the world is vibrating apart — sat underneath the clarinet like tectonic plates underneath a synagogue.

Dov came downstairs to get a glass of water and heard it. He stood in the hallway between the deli and the back room and did not move.

"What is that?" he said, when Rebbe Yankel stopped.

"That is what happens when you leave your computer open."

"No, I mean — what is that sound? It's like..." Dov searched for words. He was sixteen and his vocabulary for what he was feeling was still mostly BPM and key signatures. "It's like my grandmother's funeral and my bar mitzvah at the same time."

"Yes," said Rebbe Yankel. "That is freylekh mode."

"It's not the mode," said Dov. "It's the mode and the break. Together. It's like — Rebbe, do you know what a mashup is?"

"I am familiar with the concept."

"It's like that, but it's not a joke. It's not ironic. It sounds like both things are real at the same time. Like neither one is the accompaniment. Like they're both the lead."

Rebbe Yankel thought about this. He unscrewed the bell of his clarinet and wiped the moisture from the bore, a gesture he had made ten thousand times and that still felt like a small prayer.

"In chazzanut," he said — the art of Jewish liturgical singing — "there is a concept called *nusach*. It is not exactly a scale. It is not exactly a mode. It is the set of melodic formulas that tell you what time of year it is, what prayer is being sung, what the emotional content of the moment should be. A knowledgeable Jew can walk into a synagogue blindfolded and know, from the first four notes, whether it is Rosh Hashanah or Tisha B'Av."

"Okay."

"The Amen break is a nusach. It is a set of rhythmic formulas that tell you where you are. If you hear those drums, you know you are in a certain kind of space — a club, a warehouse, a bedroom with a subwoofer. You know what the emotional content should be. You know what time of year it is."

"And your clarinet is a different nusach."

"My clarinet is an older nusach. But when you play two nusachs at the same time — when the freylekh and the Amen coexist — you are not creating a contradiction. You are revealing that both nusachs point to the same thing."

"Which is?"

"The ache," said Rebbe Yankel. "The ache of being alive in a body that remembers things it cannot say. The freylekh says it with the augmented second. The Amen says it with the ghost of Gregory Coleman's hands on a drum kit in 1969. Both of them say: I was here. I played this. The moment passed. The recording remained."

Dov was quiet for a long time. Then he said: "Can we record it?"

So they did.

Dov set up his laptop and his interface. Rebbe Yankel played his Selmer clarinet with the re-corked eleven-times bore. Dov ran the Amen break at 170 BPM through his monitors. The clarinet played freylekh mode at half time.

The recording — a rough thing, a single microphone in a back room with wallpaper that held the ghost of a fallen photograph — captured something that neither of them could have produced alone. It was klezmer drum and bass. It was impossible. It was the most honest thing either of them had ever made.

Dov uploaded it to SoundCloud with the tag "klezmer dnb" and went to bed. By morning, it had 47 plays. By the end of the week, it had 4,000. A producer in Berlin sent Dov a message asking if the clarinet player was available for sessions. A music journalist in Tel Aviv wrote a piece calling it "the sound of two diasporas finding the same rhythm."

Rebbe Yankel did not read the piece. He was practicing in the back room of Mendel's Deli, playing freylekh mode over a different beat — a bosa nova track that Dov had left playing by accident. The augmented second sat over the bossa nova's clave like a bird on a ship's mast. It shouldn't have worked. It worked anyway.

The ache found its nusach in every rhythm. The rhythm found its ache in every nusach. The interval between them — the augmented second of the freylekh, the ghost of Gregory Coleman's hands, the clave of the bossa nova — was always the same interval. The interval is the music. The music is the interval.

---

*Later, when an AI music model was asked to generate "klezmer drum and bass, clarinet wailing over Reese bass and amen breaks, freylekh mode at 170 BPM, hora meets hyperdub," it produced sixty seconds of audio that captured the idea if not the ache. The model understood the nusach. It did not understand the wallpaper, or the re-corked clarinet, or the grandson standing in the hallway hearing his grandmother's funeral and his bar mitzvah at the same time. But it understood the interval. The interval is enough. The interval is always enough.*
