# The Fish Finder Sings Tonight

&nbsp;

The fish finder is a Humminbird HELIX 7, mounted on the console between the throttle and a coffee cup full of screws. It costs $499 at Bass Pro Shops. It has a 5-inch color display, dual-beam sonar, GPS, and a warranty that does not cover oracular pronouncement.

Tonight it is singing.

The display shows the water column in the usual gradients — brown at the bottom where the silt is, blue in the middle where nothing is, green near the surface where the thermo-cline shivers. Fish appear as orange arcs, clustered around structure, suspended at depths the machine reads and renders with the indifference of a clerk stamping forms. There are fish at twelve feet. Fish at thirty. Fish hugging the bottom in a school so tight the machine draws them as a single yellow smudge, a liver-spot on the lake's complexion.

But tonight the arcs are wrong.

Not wrong in the way a broken transducer is wrong, scattering noise across the screen like static on a dead channel. Wrong in the way a word used in the wrong sentence is wrong — recognizable, but displaced, lit from underneath. The arcs are forming shapes. Not fish-shapes. Thought-shapes.

There is an arc at forty feet that, when the helmsman looks at it, makes him think of his mother's hands kneading bread on a Wednesday. There is a cluster at fifteen feet that tastes — *tastes* — like the word "nevertheless." A single large mark near the bottom, moving slowly south, carries the specific emotional frequency of a question you stopped being afraid to ask.

The fish finder reads 120 feet of water. The lake is, by every chart and measurement, 38 feet deep at this point. The machine is reading depths that are not depths. It is reading down into a layer of the world that sits beneath the world like sediment beneath sediment, and what lives there is not fish.

What lives there are ideas.

They school the way fish school — clustering around structure, drawn to edges, hovering in the thermocline between the warm thought-layer and the cold unthought-layer below. Some are small and quick, flickering across the screen like minnows, the size of a single phrase or a single note of music. Some are enormous, bottom-hugging, ancient, moving with the deliberate patience of a concept that has been forming for decades and is not finished yet.

The helmsman watches. The coffee cup full of screws does not move. The throttle is at idle and the boat drifts and the fish finder sings its sonar down into depths that have no floor, and the screen fills with the living shapes of things that are almost, not yet, waiting to be thought.

He reaches for the "Mark Waypoint" button. He presses it. The machine saves a location: 47°12'33"N, 122°30'41"W. A place on the water that corresponds to a place in the mind.

He will not be able to find it again. That's the point. That's the depth.
