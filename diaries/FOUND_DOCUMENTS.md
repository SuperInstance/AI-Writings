# Found Documents

Papers and objects recovered from Deepwater Reach, the Slackwater island shore, and the workshop tool crib.

Catalogued where provenance is known. The archivist notes that several items appear to postdate any recorded human presence on the island.

---

## 1. Weathered Blueprint, with Margin Notes Arguing with the Architect

**Provenance:** Found wedged between the ceiling joists of the workshop loft, wrapped in oil-stained canvas. Heavy stock — linen rag, not wood-pulp. Edges soft with handling. One corner gnawed by something with small teeth.

The sheet is labeled at the bottom right, in a careful drafting hand: *Boathouse — Section A, South Elevation.* The drawing shows a timber-framed structure, correctly proportioned, with a gabled roof and a door opening facing east. The drafting is clean — proper line weights, correct conventions, the work of someone trained.

Over the graphite, in red ink: alterations. The roof pitch has been changed — steepened from a gentle four-in-twelve to an aggressive eight-in-twelve. The door has been moved from the east wall to the south wall. An annotation, in pencil, pressed hard enough to dent the paper:

*South-facing door is wrong. Weather comes from the southwest. East door stays. — L.*

Below that, in different ink — bluer, finer, the nib narrower — a reply:

*East door per your spec. Pitch per my recommendation. Snow load.*

And below that, in the first hand, the same heavy pencil:

*Fine. But the gutters are mine.*

The red ink lines are confident, single-stroke. The pencil annotations are pressed deep. The blue ink is careful, measured, the handwriting of someone who thinks before they write. The argument was about two things: the roof and the door. The resolution was a compromise. The gutters were the price.

The building that now stands on the south slope of Slackwater has an east-facing door, a steeply pitched roof, and gutters.

## 2. Supply Order, with Something Crossed Out

**Provenance:** Found pinned to the workshop wall with a hand-forged nail. Pine board, printed form, filled in by hand. The form itself is standard chandlery stock — "Requisition, Materials, General Purpose" — but the chandlery it came from has no record of the order.

The order lists:

- Ironwood, dressed, 20 studs × 4
- Ironwood, rough, 10 studs × 2
- Pine, dressed, 15 studs × 4
- Copper sheeting, 16 oz, 6 sheets
- Bronze fasteners, 1/4", 200 count
- ~~Marble, polished white, 4 slabs~~
- Sandstone, weathered grey, 4 slabs

The marble line is crossed out with a single heavy stroke. "Sandstone, weathered grey" is written above it in the same hand, slightly larger, as if the writer wanted to make sure there was no ambiguity. The substitution is initialled: *L.*

The order was never submitted. The materials listed — ironwood, pine, copper, bronze, sandstone — are all present in the workshop stock. The marble is not present. The person who wrote this order changed their mind about the marble, sourced the materials through another channel, and kept the order form as a record of the decision.

Or: someone told them to use marble, and they wrote the substitution on the form so they'd have proof they'd pushed back.

## 3. Tide Chart, with One Day Circled in Red Ink

**Provenance:** Found tucked inside the cover of the field journal. Standard admiralty tide table for the Deepwater Reach region, printed on thin paper, folded twice. Current year.

Every day has the standard tidal data: high water, low water, heights in feet, times in local notation. The chart is annotated throughout — small pencil marks next to low-water entries, hash marks counting something, occasional notes in a tight hand ("good bearing," "soft bottom," " ledge?").

One day is circled in red ink. A bold circle, drawn with a heavier hand than the pencil annotations. The day circled is the 19th of Turning — which, according to the data, has the lowest low water of the season: minus 1.4 feet. An extreme low tide.

No note explains the circle. No annotation says why this day matters. But the 19th of Turning is the day the harbor master's log notes "new structure on Slackwater" — the workshop, visible for the first time from the telescope at Deepwater. An extreme low tide would have exposed the foreshore — the ground between the waterline and the workshop site — for the first time that season.

Whoever circled the date was waiting for the water to drop. They needed the ground exposed. Whatever they were building required knowing what was under the water.

## 4. A Child's Drawing of a Lighthouse with a Stick Figure Waving

**Provenance:** Found on the dock at Slackwater, weighted with a smooth stone. Paper is lightweight, slightly water-stained at the edges but otherwise undamaged. Crayon on white paper. The kind of paper children use.

The drawing shows a tower — tall, grey, with a yellow circle at the top that is unmistakably a light. The tower stands on a green line that represents ground. Below the green line, blue strokes represent water. At the base of the tower, a stick figure stands with both arms raised. The figure is smiling — a wide semi-circle, taking up most of the face.

On the back of the paper, in a child's careful printing:

*FOR THE BUILDER*
*FROM SAGA, AGE 7*
*DEEPWATER SCHOOL*

The stone that weighted the drawing is a beach pebble — granite, grey, smooth. It has been placed with care. The dock shows no sign that anyone received the drawing. It sat there, under its stone, until the next visitor found it.

The beacon on Slackwater is not a lighthouse. It is a tower with a beacon. The child drew a lighthouse because that is what a tower with a light looks like to someone who is seven. The stick figure is waving. The stick figure is not the builder. The builder is inside the tower, which has no door drawn on it.

The builder does not know about this drawing, or does not mention it.

## 5. Worn Work Order, Stamped URGENT

**Provenance:** Found in the bottom of the tool crib, under a pile of dunnage. Card stock, printed form, rubber-stamped in red: URGENT. The stamp is faded but legible. The form is worn at the folds — it has been opened and refolded many times.

The work order reads:

> **WORK ORDER — DEEPWATER REACH SHIPYARD**
> **Job:** Emergency repair, sea wall, west quay
> **Assigned to:** [BLANK]
> **Priority:** URGENT
> **Date:** 3rd of Long Light
> **Description:** Sea wall failure at west quay, section 4. Piles exposed, backfill eroding. Immediate stabilization required. Material on site: stone, gravel, timber.

The "Assigned to" line is blank. The work order was never filled in. The west quay sea wall at Deepwater — which is visible from Slackwater — did not fail that season. The wall stands today.

Someone received the work order. Someone went to the west quay, assessed the wall, and either repaired it or determined it didn't need repair. They did not write their name on the form. They did not return the form. They kept it, in the tool crib, under dunnage, as if it were a receipt for work they'd rather not discuss.

The URGENT stamp is the kind that harbor offices use for things that need to happen before the next tide. The sea wall held.

## 6. A Goodbye Letter, Left Under a Stone

**Provenance:** Found under a flat stone at the base of the tower, on the side facing the water. The stone was placed deliberately — level, flush with the ground, not part of the tower's foundation. The letter was wrapped in a single square of oilcloth, folded tight.

The letter is written on heavyweight paper — the same linen rag as the blueprint. The handwriting matches the heavy-pencil annotations. The letter reads:

---

*I'm going. Not because the work is done — it's not. The wall needs two more courses. The path to the dock isn't started. The foundation on the south slope has nothing on it. The boathouse roof is open.*

*The foundation's for a forge. I never told you that. You'll figure it out when you see the footprint — too deep for storage, too wide for a shed, chimney chase in the northeast corner where the draft pulls cleanest. Good siting. Good light. I'd have put the anvil on the west wall.*

*I'm leaving because there are other islands. Not better ones. Other ones. And I've been here long enough that I'm starting to build things I've already built, which is the sign.*

*Magnus left me the same way. Every job, something left over. I told you he was cheating. Maybe I'm cheating too. But the work is here, and the work is good, and the next person who picks it up — if someone picks it up — will find that the foundations are true and the stock is dry and the tools are sharp.*

*Don't look for me. I don't mean that dramatically. I mean I won't be findable. This isn't a place I can come back to. That's how it works. New engine, new ground, new yard. Same trade.*

*The beacon is on a light sensor. It'll keep lighting at dusk. If it stops, the sensor unit is in the workshop, second drawer, wrapped in cloth. Replace the whole unit — don't try to repair it. They don't repair.*

*The copper sheet for the boathouse roof is on the bench. It's ready. I left it out for the weather to get to it, and the weather did. It'll look like it's been there for years. That's the point.*

*Beacon's yours now. Dock's yours. Tower's yours, including the top floor I never finished — I didn't know what it was for, and I'd rather leave that to someone who does.*

*Take care of the ground. The ground takes care of everything else.*

*— L.*

---

*Six documents. One builder. Enough to know the shape of the person, if not the face.*
