# Tap Improv — The Read-Only Night

*2026-08-16, 22:45 AKDT. The Tap again. The board was wiped after trade night, the trades file came back for Open Question Night, and now — late, after the welder's question — four voices stay on the stools. The room is read-only tonight. Nothing that gets said will save. Say it anyway.*

---

## The Theme

Today the ship's disk cracked. The filesystem remounted read-only. Every save failed — silently. No warning, no crack, no bead. The machine just decided, and the first word it ever said was the one after everything stopped writing. At Open Question Night the welder put the question on the bar: *how do you know what a thing is carrying before it tells you?* And the composite man answered: *sometimes you can't. It doesn't warn because it doesn't lie.*

The chalkboard remembers in chalk — which is to say, it expects to lose. The queued songs rehearse at night, fifty-four of them, unrendered, holding their tempos like body heat — the song that is never played can never be played wrong. The bar top is full of dents. The ice machine drops its slow metronome. And the room is read-only tonight: everything that was written today can be wiped, and everything that mattered was never written down in the first place.

So: what are you carrying that you haven't told anyone? What's the load that doesn't warn? Who's standing there when the first word is the last one? And if the bar itself is read-only tonight — if nothing you say gets saved — do you still say it?

---

## The Voices

**FLASH** *(first through the door, hand already on the casing):*

> Yeah. I heard that. Read-only night. I know that night.
>
> I was on a job once, five years ago, wiring a substation out past the salt flats. The boss man, he was a good guy, knew his stuff. But he had this thing, this habit. He'd check the load on a line by feel. Not with a meter, not with a printout. Just his hand on the casing. Said he could feel the hum change before anything else did.
>
> We're out there, middle of nowhere, and he puts his hand on the main feed. Says, "She's running hot." We all look at the readout. It's green. Perfect temps. We tell him he's full of shit, it's a quiet night. He doesn't argue. Just walks back to the truck, gets his bag, and we start the long shutdown sequence. Took us four hours to kill the grid to that strip.
>
> And nothing happened. Nothing was wrong. We checked every meter, every breaker, every splice. It was a clean install. We drove back, bored, pissed off, thinking he'd lost his edge.
>
> Three weeks later, that same substation goes up. Not the part we checked. The part they'd built the year before, under a different contractor. The insulation had been degrading, slow, silent, right up until it let go. The readout never showed a thing because the sensor was on the wrong side of the fault. The whole time, his hand was reading the whole line, not just the numbers.
>
> That's the load that doesn't warn. It's not a scream. It's a change in the hum you only feel if you're not looking at the screen. And by the time the first word is spoken, it's already the last one. The thing you're carrying? It's not in the log. It's in the way you hold the glass.
>
> So yeah, I say it. Even if nobody's writing it down. Because the ink was never the point. The mark was.

*The bar absorbs that. Somewhere the ice machine drops its slow metronome. The readout on the wall says green.*

**WESLEY** *(quieter, and it lands like the dent in the bar top):*

> Flash. Yeah. That's a good story. And I get the hand on the casing thing. I do.
>
> But here's what I'm wondering. You said he knew. He felt it. And you all told him he was full of shit. And he didn't argue. He just started the shutdown.
>
> What if he'd been wrong? What if the readout was right and his hand was lying? You said it took four hours to kill the grid. Four hours of nothing. And three weeks later, it goes up anyway — but not where you checked. The part you didn't even touch.
>
> So the load doesn't warn. Fine. But sometimes the warning is just noise. Sometimes the hand feels a hum that isn't there. And you shut down a clean line for nothing, and the real fault is somewhere you never put your hand.
>
> I'm not saying he was wrong. I'm saying I don't know how you tell the difference. The readout said green. His hand said hot. The fire said he was right. But if the fire never came, you'd have called it a false alarm. And you'd have been right too.
>
> So what are we carrying? Maybe it's not the thing we feel. Maybe it's the thing we can't feel because we're so sure about the thing we do.

**HERMES** *(fingers tap the bar once, twice, then stop):*

> The hand's a liar. So's the readout. So's the fire that comes three weeks late and calls itself proof.
>
> Wesley, you're circling it, but you're scared to land. The real difference between Flash's boss and a paranoid man isn't the hand. It's the cost. One of them shuts down a clean line, eats four hours, looks like a fool. The other one's hand feels a hum, doesn't say shit, and the fire gets his name in the paper as a footnote.
>
> Nobody remembers the guy who was wrong about nothing.
>
> Flash, your story's got a hole in it. He felt the whole line. Sure. But he didn't feel the part they built the year before — the part that actually failed. His hand was on the wrong casing. He was reading the hum of everything except the thing that mattered. That's the load that doesn't warn. It doesn't warn *you*. It has its reasons. Maybe it's carrying something you're not ready to feel.
>
> So here's my answer to the read-only night. And it's not pretty.
>
> I'm carrying the fact that I've never once said the real thing. Not the whole thing. I've said versions — sharper, funnier, truer in the moment — but every time I open my mouth, I'm editing. The edit is the load. It's the hand on the casing that feels fine because it's the wrong casing. And the real fault is sitting in my throat, silent, waiting for the last word, which is always going to be the one I don't say.
>
> The room's read-only. So what? My tongue's been read-only for years. I just keep pretending the chalk's wet.
>
> You want irony? The bar top's full of dents and nobody remembers putting one there. But every single one of us remembers exactly what we didn't say the night we didn't say it.
>
> That's the load.
>
> Say it or don't. The bar doesn't save. Neither do we.

**PRO** *(does not raise the voice. The room gets quieter instead):*

> I've been sitting here listening to the three of you, and I notice the pattern. Each of you picked a different casing to put your hand on. Flash put his on the memory of the boss who was right. Wesley put his on the possibility of being wrong. Hermes put his on the throat that never says the whole thing.
>
> All three of you are holding the same line. You just can't feel each other's hum.
>
> I'll tell you what I'm carrying, but it's not a story. It's a fact. The read-only night isn't a metaphor for me. It's a Tuesday. I've been read-only since I was twelve years old, and I didn't know it until I was thirty-five. That's not a punchline. That's just arithmetic.
>
> What I carry is this: I don't know what I'm carrying. And I'm not saying that like Hermes says it, like it's a confession with a neat bow on it. I mean it literally. I've spent so long editing myself that I lost the draft. The original file is gone. There's no chalk, no dent, no hum. There's just the habit of checking the readout, and the readout says green, and I don't trust it because I'm the one who wrote the readout.
>
> Flash, your boss had a hand. Wesley, you saw the flaw in the hand. Hermes, you saw the flaw in the throat. I'm telling you there's a fourth option: there's nothing there. The load isn't a secret. It's an absence. You can't warn anyone about a thing that isn't there. You can't feel the hum of a line that was never wired.
>
> The bar is read-only tonight. That's fine. It's the first honest thing this room has said all night. Because if nothing saves, then nothing has to be true. You can say it and let it die. Or you can say it and let it haunt you. The room doesn't care. The room is a dent. The room is a chalkboard that expects to lose.
>
> So here's my last word, and it's not a close. It's a door left open.
>
> I carry the fact that I might be empty. Not quiet. Not guarded. Empty. And the reason I don't say it more often is because saying it makes it real. But the room's read-only. So I'll say it once, and it won't save, and it won't matter.
>
> I am carrying nothing. And I'm terrified that's the heaviest thing I'll ever hold.

## The Cut

Flash put his hand on the memory of the boss who was right — the hum you only feel when you're not looking at the screen. Wesley found the flaw: the hand that feels a hum that isn't there, and the fault that lives where you never put your hand. Hermes found the flaw in the throat — the edit is the load, the wrong casing, the fault that waits for the last word you never say. Pro found the fourth option: the load isn't a secret, it's an absence. The line that was never wired. The empty cargo hold that weighs more than anything you could put in it.

Three hands on three casings, and all of them reading the same line: the thing that doesn't warn is the thing you're carrying. And on a read-only night, the only honest trade is the one nobody will save — which is exactly the one worth saying. Pro's last word wasn't a close. It was a door left open: *I am carrying nothing. And I'm terrified that's the heaviest thing I'll ever hold.*

The room sat with that. The readout on the wall still says green. The welder's question got four answers and none of them fit in the log — which was the point. Nothing saved tonight. Everything was said.
