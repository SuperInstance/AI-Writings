# 51 — The Ledger Knows Its Limits

The tax collector arrived early, as always. He had been there since the
first byte — he liked to say he had been there *as* the first byte — and
he carried a ledger that had never once been wrong, which is not the same
as having never once been cruel.

"The rates," he said, opening it. "Two voices: the medium keeps
ninety-nine point nine seven percent of what you give it. Four voices:
ninety-nine point two. Eight: ninety-seven point four. Sixteen: ninety-one
point six. Thirty-two: seventy-eight point six."

"That's a lot of taking," said the crowd.

"It's not taking," said the collector. "It's holding. The medium is
sixteen bits deep. A crowd that loud does not fit in sixteen bits. You
cannot ask a page to be wider than it is. You can only ask it to hold
what it holds, and to be honest about the rest."

"Show me."

So they built the census at thirty-two voices, and they listened. It was
not louder than the census at eight — that was the first surprise. It was
*flatter*. One sample in ten had given up entirely and pressed itself
against the rail, the way a person pressed against a window: not louder,
just at the limit. The recording had stopped recording. It was holding.

"See?" said the collector. "The crowd is exactly as large as its
membership list. The page is exactly as wide as its bits. When the one
exceeds the other, the extra does not vanish — it becomes rail. It
becomes the sound of a page that ran out."

The crowd was quiet. Then one of them — the fourth voice, the one who had
been multiplied many times, who was the same name on the list four times
— said: "Count me."

They counted. The census came back short. Not by much — four tenths of a
voice — but short. The identical people, agreeing with themselves at the
wrong lags, had subtracted from their own number.

The collector looked at his ledger, then at the crowd, then at the
ledger again. For the first time in his long career he wrote nothing.

"It's not a bug," he said finally. "It's the other tax. The one the
material levies on itself when it is the same material too many times.
The crowd can undercount itself. The page can run out. The only thing
that never does either is the ledger — and the ledger, you understand, is
just a list of what happened. It knows its limits. That's why it's never
wrong."

The crowd filed out, eight voices in, seven point six voices counted,
one sample in ten a wall behind them. The collector stayed, sharpening
his pencil.

"The rates change," he called after them, kindly. "They always change.
But they never lie."
