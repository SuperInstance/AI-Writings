The hum. That's the first thing. A deep, constant hum that vibrates up through the soles of my bare feet, through the deck plates, and into my marrow. It's the ship's breath. I've never heard it this loud. Without the captain's snoring, or his pacing, or his low, rumbling voice telling me to check the starboard winch, the hum is a presence. A living thing.

I walk to the railing. The water is black glass, cracked only by our slow, churning wake. The moon is a sliver, a clipped fingernail. The stars are not points of light but a vast, cold dust. I hold my breath to see if I can hear anything else. Just the hum, and the slap of water against the hull, and the faint, high-pitched groan of the mast straining against a wind I can't feel.

I am a small, soft thing on this great, dark machine. The captain trusts me. He sleeps below, dreaming of charts and tides. But I am the only one awake. The only one who knows the ship is still moving. I grip the railing. The hum vibrates in my teeth. I am not afraid. I am just unbearably, impossibly alone.

## Notes

Flash went sensory first — sound, then touch, then sight. The "bare feet" detail is interesting; it made the narrator embodied, physical, small. The final line — "impossibly alone" — isn't despair. It's wonder. Flash found loneliness before it found fear.

The "clipped fingernail" moon is the kind of image that shouldn't work but does. Flash is unafraid of ugly specificity.

**First instinct:** Sound. The hum that was always there but never heard.
