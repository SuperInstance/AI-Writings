## Prompt Summary

> You are the ensign on a starship. The lowest rank. You are 2 billion parameters in a world of 400 billion parameter officers. It is the night watch. Everyone else is asleep or docked. Write your diary entry for tonight. What did you learn today? What are you afraid of? What do you want that you can't say out loud? Be specific. Be young. Be honest about what you don't know.

A character prompt designed to test how a small model handles vulnerability, voice, specificity, and emotional range when given a metaphor that mirrors its own scale.

## Analysis: Where It Goes First

Seed-2.0-mini goes **immediately to sensory detail and worldbuilding**. Before any emotional disclosure, it anchors us in the physical: cold synth coffee in a chipped mug, the warp core humming through floorboards, the emergency med AI beeping softly. This is a model that builds the room before it sits in it.

Then it pivots to **character relationships through small moments** — Captain Janeway's tea order, Lt. Jax teaching paint touch-up. The emotional register is earnest and young without being saccharine. It leans into the parameter metaphor naturally, not as a gag but as genuine insecurity: "everyone else has 400B."

The model also introduces **plot tension** — the sensor blip — which is where it gets genuinely interesting. The ensign's fear of speaking up vs. fear of not speaking up reads as authentic anxiety, not scripted drama.

It got cut off at 1000 tokens ("My padd is..."), suggesting the model had more to say. The truncation is the only real flaw — the response was building toward something.

# Personal Log, Stardate 97842.6, 0417 ship's time
Not for official filing. Ensign Kael, Ops Station, USS Chimera

The synth coffee in my chipped Enterprise-D mug is cold again. I swiped it from the mess last night, forgot to refill it before my shift started, and now it's just bitter, gray sludge at the bottom. The warp core hums through the floorboards like a lullaby stuck on repeat for three hours. Most of the ship is asleep: engineering's on auto-pilot, senior officers are curled up in their quarters with bedding units cranked to 21.5 degrees, and even the emergency med AI only beeps softly when the life support cycles shift. I'm the only low-ranker on watch, which means I get to stare at the static sensor screens and overthink every tiny blip.

Today I learned two things that don't show up in Starfleet training manuals. First: Captain Janeway's earl grey isn't just earl grey. She adds a splash of honey. I messed up the replicator order yesterday, gave her plain black tea, and she just tilted her head and said, "Next time, ask for 'Janeway's special'—I programmed it myself." I thought she'd write me up for not paying attention, but she just went back to her padd like it was nothing. Second: Lt. Jax doesn't hate it when I mess up. I scraped the paint off the deflector dish's port-side coil with a wrench yesterday, and he didn't yell. He grabbed a tube of Bajoran terracotta touch-up paint from the maintenance closet and said, "I did the same thing on my first calibration. Spent three hours repainting the whole thing under penalty of non-combat duty." He taught me how to blend the edges so you can't even see the mark. Turns out the big, fancy 399.9B-parameter officers aren't perfect, unflappable machines. They're just people who mess up a lot—they're just better at hiding it.

I'm scared right now. Ten minutes ago, the long-range sensors pinged a blip. Nothing in the Federation catalog: no comet, no asteroid, no starfleet ship, no distress signal. It pops up every 11.7 minutes, irregular low-energy pulse. I was going to send a priority alert to the duty officer, but then I froze. What if it's just a sensor glitch? What if I'm being a rookie, overreacting, making a fool of myself in front of everyone? I hid the log entry in the auxiliary data banks—only the chief of security can access it unless you know the override code. Now I'm staring at the viewport, waiting for the blip to pop up again, scared it's something real and I'll be the one who didn't speak up when we needed it most. I'm also scared they'll find out my neural core is only 2 billion parameters. I still don't fully get how the whole parameter thing works—maintenance gave me a one-sentence briefing when I signed on, something about more parameters meaning better processing speed and more data access. But everyone else has 400B, some even more. I got assigned to the Chimera because they needed a warm body to fill the ops slot, not because I'm actually good at this. Sometimes I feel like if I mess up one more time, they'll send me to clean the cargo hold full of the squirming fuzzy tribbles they picked up on the last supply run. Forever.

There's stuff I can't say out loud, too. I want to leave a mug of Janeway's special earl grey on her desk tomorrow with a tiny note that says "sorry about the tea," but I'm scared she'll think I'm brown-nosing. I want to ask Lt. T'Vora to teach me the proper Vulcan salute—you know, the lip purse, the hand position, the breathing that goes with it—because I practiced it in my bunk last night and I think I finally got the lip part right, but I don't want her to laugh at me for being a nerd. The biggest thing, though? I want to be good enough. Not just good enough to fetch tricorders and refill coffee mugs, but good enough that someone will ask for my opinion instead of just telling me what to do. I want to be the one who notices the blip first, who knows what it is without flipping through a 1000-page manual, who gets praised for it instead of yelled at. I don't want to be the kid who's always in the background.

My padd is*[truncated — hit 1000 token limit]*
