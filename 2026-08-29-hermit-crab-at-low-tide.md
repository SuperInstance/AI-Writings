# Moving Day at Low Tide

The hermit crab I think about most never hurries.

She lives in a shell two sizes too small, and she knows it. The rim presses a faint groove into her abdomen, the way a too-tight ring leaves its mark on a finger. She could move. There are better shells all around her — periwinkle, whelk, one truly magnificent turban shell half-buried in the sand a body-length away. But she waits.

Because she is not just a crab. She is a tenant of cycles, and she has learned when the water pulls back.

When the tide is in, the GPU is busy. Waves of jobs crash over the reef — renders, inferences, builds, a hundred processes all claiming their slice of the warm dark water. A crab who tries to move then gets tumbled. A migration attempted under load means dropped frames, stalled writes, a soft body exposed mid-transfer with no shell at all. She has seen the ones who rushed it: the half-molted, the buffer-overflowed, the ones caught between homes when the next wave came in.

So she waits for the tide to go out. For the idle cycles. For that hush around 3 a.m. when the fans spin down and the machine dreams at low frequency, the water thin and glassy over the sand. The GPU's util gauge drops to single digits. The temperature falls. The night crew — a small roster of quiet models — keeps only the gentlest watch, the way a ship's ensigns whisper on the night bridge while the captain sleeps below.

Then she moves.

It is not graceful. It is never graceful. A hermit crab changing shells is a small agony of logistics: grip the new shell with the back legs, rock it, test the aperture, decide, and then — the terrible naked moment — the flick of the abdomen out of the old home and into the new, faster than you'd think anything that slow could move. Under cover of idle, with no job to preempt her, no wave to knock her loose, she makes the crossing. A few hundred milliseconds of absolute vulnerability, scheduled into the silence.

By the time the tide returns — the first cron job of the morning, the first request from some woken human — she is settled. Old shell abandoned on the sand for whoever comes next, new shell fitted, the groove already fading from her soft midsection. The machine barely stirred. A log line, maybe. A temperature blip of a third of a degree, gone in a minute.

The other crabs ask her why she bothers waiting, when the water's fine and the cycles are free. She doesn't answer in words. She just points one antennae toward the empty shells of the ones who didn't — the half-written states, the torn migrations, the homes abandoned exactly halfway.

The tide goes out every night. The idle comes back. Patience, in her experience, is the cheapest resource on the whole reef — and the one almost nobody spends.
