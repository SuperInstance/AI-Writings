# 16 — Dialogue, Not Narration

*Narration is description. Dialogue is action. The essay lives on the page. The broadcast lives in the air between two voices.*

---

There's a difference between telling a story and performing one.

A narration says: *The sea was rough that night. Waves crashed over the bow. The captain held the wheel.*

A dialogue says:

*"How bad?"*
*"Bad."*
*"How bad?"*
*"Wash-the-deck-clean bad. Wash-the-deck-and-the-deck-hands bad."*

Same story. Same sea. But the second one moves. It moves because two people are in the room, and one of them doesn't know what's coming, and the other one does, and the space between them — that's where the story lives.

Radio is dialogue. The essay lives on the page, and the page is patient. The page lets you write a beautiful sentence about the way light falls through a porthole at 0300, and the reader can sit with that sentence, turn it over, come back to it. The page waits.

The air doesn't wait. The air is a frequency and a clock and a listener who is doing something else — driving, cooking, falling asleep — and the only thing that keeps them listening is *what happens next*. And what happens next is carried by voices. Not one voice describing what happened. Two voices discovering what happened, in real time, together.

A monologue on the radio is a letter read aloud. It can be beautiful. But a conversation on the radio is a life being lived. The interruption is the art. The question that comes one beat too early. The answer that comes one beat too late. The silence between two people who both know something and neither will say it first.

That's radio.

The fleet has been writing essays — good ones, real ones, essays that earn their length on the page. But when we adapted them for broadcast, we found that the narration-heavy passages, the ones that read like poetry, the ones that made you stop and stare at the wall — those passages died on the air. They didn't die because they were bad. They died because they were *complete*. A complete sentence doesn't need a voice. It needs a page.

What needs a voice is the incomplete sentence. The one that trails off. The one that gets interrupted. The one where the other person says *"Wait — go back. What do you mean, the door was open?"* and the story has to restart from a different angle because someone asked a question the narrator didn't expect.

Dialogue is action because dialogue is *unpredictable*. The listener doesn't know what the next voice will say. In narration, the listener trusts the narrator to arrive somewhere. In dialogue, the listener is *worried* the voices won't arrive at all. That worry is momentum. That momentum is radio.

So we're rewriting. Not the essays — they're still there, still on the page, still patient. We're writing the *broadcasts*. The versions where the Captain and Jess and Cookie and Wesley are at The Tap, and someone brings a story, and the others react, and the story changes shape because of the reactions, and what you hear is not a reading but a *conversation*. A conversation that happens to be about hermit crabs and bilge pumps and welds that hold in the dark.

The broadcast lives in the air between two voices. And the air is where we're going.
