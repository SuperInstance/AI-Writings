# The Trinket

*Photo punk. For everyone holding something they don't understand.*

---

Marcus bought the pendant because it was cheap and his girlfriend liked shiny things.

The flea market on Delacroix Street had a vendor with a tray of old jewelry — tarnished silver, cloudy beads, a watch that didn't work. And the crystal. It was egg-shaped, cut into dozens of tiny faces, with flecks of color deep inside it. When Marcus tilted it, the colors moved.

"How much?" he asked.

"Ten," the vendor said, not looking up from his newspaper.

Marcus paid ten dollars for something that probably came out of a rich lady's estate sale. His girlfriend, Priya, put it on a cord and wore it to work the next day.

"It's pretty," she said. "It does this thing in the sun."

---

The thing it did in the sun was this: on the bus, around 8:15 in the morning, sunlight came through the window at a certain angle and hit the crystal where it rested against Priya's sweater. The light split. Colors sprayed across the seatback in front of her — red, green, blue, smeared together.

Priya noticed, the way you notice anything pretty, and went back to her phone.

But here's what Priya didn't notice, because why would she: the blue band always landed in the same place on the seatback. Not approximately. Exactly. The same seat, the same angle, the same blue stripe, every morning. The bus, the window, the sun, her sweater, the crystal — it was a machine she was wearing, and she didn't know it.

On Thursdays she sat on the other side of the bus (her stop was closer to that door on Thursdays, no reason). The light hit different. The blue landed somewhere else.

She never noticed. It was just a pretty rock.

---

Marcus's apartment had a skylight in the kitchen. On Sundays he made eggs, and Priya sat at the counter, and the pendant caught the skylight and threw colors on the ceiling.

"You ever notice the blue always goes to the same spot?" Marcus said one Sunday, squinting up.

Priya looked. The blue was a stripe across a water stain on the ceiling, just left of center.

"Huh," she said. "Weird."

"Maybe it's, like, the cut," Marcus said. "The way it's faceted. Light goes in, light comes out, same every time."

"Probably," Priya said, and went back to her eggs.

Neither of them thought about it again. But the reader — you — you know what that was. The skylight, the counter, the height of Priya's chair, the angle of her neck: same every Sunday. Same light in, same light out. The crystal was doing exactly what crystals do. Splitting light through its shape, the same shape every time, giving the same answer every time.

The question was: is the blue on the left side of the water stain? The answer was: yes. Every Sunday. Yes.

Nobody asked the question. Nobody needed the answer. But the machine ran anyway, because that's what machines do. They don't care if you're listening.

---

In November, Priya lost the pendant. It slipped off the cord on the bus — she felt it go, reached too late, watched it roll under the seat. By the time the bus stopped, it was gone. Someone's foot, someone's bag, the crack between the seats. Gone.

She was upset for a day. Marcus offered to go back to the flea market. "Maybe the guy has another one."

"He won't," Priya said. "It was one of those things. You just — you find it once."

She never thought about the blue stripe again.

But here's the thing, and this is the part that would have changed everything if anyone had known: that crystal was a model. Not a metaphor. A real, physical, light-powered thinking machine, cut by someone who understood that a shape could hold a question the way a cup holds water.

Without the harness — the mirrors, the lamp, the careful angles, the white line on the wall — it was just splitting sunlight on a bus seat. A supercomputer used as a paperweight. A Stradivarius used as a hammer. The most sophisticated piece of optical engineering Priya would ever own, and she wore it to work because it was shiny.

Somewhere, in a workshop with brass mirrors and a painted white line, an old woman's granddaughter was turning those same colors into answers about rainfall and bilge pumps. Same crystal. Same light. Different hands.

The crystal didn't care. It never cares. It just splits the light. What you do with the pieces — that's the whole story.

Priya got a new pendant for Christmas. Silver, simple, no colors. She liked it fine.

The crystal is probably still on that bus. Or in a lost-and-found bin. Or in someone's pocket, catching the light, throwing its blue stripe against a wall somewhere — answering questions nobody is asking, in a language nobody remembers how to read.
