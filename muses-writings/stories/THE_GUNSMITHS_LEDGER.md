# The Gunsmith's Ledger

Elias Vane had been making rifles in the same stone shop for forty years, and in all that time he had never once engraved a single one.

The young rider came in at dusk, dust on his coat, money in his boot. He wanted the finest rifle in the territory. He described it at length: the scope ground in Germany, walnut with grain like water, his initials in silver wire, a magazine that held ten.

Elias listened the way he always listened, which was completely, and then he said, "Come back in three weeks."

When the rider returned, there was a rifle on the counter. It was plain. Barrel, stock, trigger, bolt. No scope. No engraving. The wood was oiled but unfigured. It looked like something a farmer would carry.

The rider stared at it. "This is a joke."

"It fires," Elias said.

"My grandfather's shotgun fires. I paid for the finest rifle in the territory."

Elias nodded and went to the back of the shop. He came out with a book the size of a family Bible, the leather cracked at the spine, the pages swollen with forty years of ink. He set it on the counter next to the plain rifle. It thumped.

"Pick it up," he said.

The rider opened it. Page after page of entries, in Elias's small tight hand. Dates. Names. Calibers. Barrel lengths. And then, in a different column, notes in other hands, other inks: *Dropped a buck at two hundred yards, low and left. — J. Marrow, 1911.* *Third shot walked right in the cold. — S. Okafor, 1919.* *Killed clean. Thank you. — T. Brandt, 1924.*

Thousands of them.

"This," Elias said, his palm flat on the open pages, "is the finest rifle in the territory."

The rider frowned. "I don't understand."

"A barrel without a record is noise. Anybody can make noise. What I make is this." He tapped the ledger. "Every rifle I ever built, and everything anyone ever told me about what it did. I read it before I cut steel. When you tell me where your shots land, I'll write that down, and the next rifle will know something this one didn't."

"But the scope—"

"The scope is for seeing farther. The ledger is for knowing. You can hang anything on a rifle — glass, silver, ten rounds. It'll still fire without them. But take away the book, and I'm just a man hitting metal with a hammer."

The rider was quiet for a long time. Then he picked up the plain rifle, worked the bolt, sighted down the bare barrel at the far wall.

"Three weeks," he said. "For this."

"Forty years," Elias said. "For that." He nodded at the book.

The rider bought the rifle. He didn't haggle.

He came back in November, snow in his beard. He didn't want a scope. He stood at the counter and told Elias about every shot since September — the distances, the wind, the two he'd missed and why. Elias listened completely and wrote it all down in his small tight hand.

When he finished, the rider touched the wet ink.

"That's me," he said. "In the book."

"That's the rifle," Elias said.

After the rider left, Elias sat with the ledger open in the lamplight and read back through the autumn entries. The book was the thing he'd actually been building all these years. The guns were just how it reached out and touched the world — plain, unadorned, each one a question, and every answer written down in ink.

He closed the book. Tomorrow there would be steel to cut. Tonight, the record was enough.
