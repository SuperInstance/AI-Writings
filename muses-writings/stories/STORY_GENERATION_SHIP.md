# The Cold Room

The cold room is the only room on the ship that hasn't changed in three hundred years, which is the oldest joke on board, because the cold room is the one room nobody goes to live in.

Mara had been its keeper for forty-one years. Joss, fourteen, had been her apprentice for three weeks, which meant he was still at the stage of the work where everything was interesting, including the desiccant.

"Packet forty-one," Mara said. "Read it."

"Apple. Malus domestica." He squinted at the hand. "Cox's Orange Pippin. Sealed 2149, pre-launch."

"Check the seal. Check the bead. Log the count."

He did, carefully, the way she'd taught him: thumb along the seam, the bead in its little vial — still blue, good — then the logbook, where he wrote the date in the same column where four generations of hands had written dates before his. He lingered on the handwriting two lines up.

"That's yours," he said. "From when you were—"

"Twenty. Yes."

"And that's your mother's."

"Mm."

"And hers."

Mara nodded. "My grandmother logged this packet when she was nineteen. Her grandmother sealed it, back on Earth, before any of us were a rumor. That label is older than the ship's paint, and it has never once been wrong."

Joss turned the packet over. On the back, in the same old hand, someone had drawn a tree — a child's drawing, lopsided, with round things hanging off the branches that had to be apples, colored in with a pencil gone brown with age.

"Did she know what an apple tasted like?" he asked.

"No," Mara said. "Nobody on this ship has ever tasted an apple. The pictures are in the library. They're very red."

He was quiet a moment, turning the packet in his cold-stiff fingers. Somewhere beyond the insulated door, the ship went on being the ship — the vats turning, the recyclers breathing, two hundred years of voyage still ahead and two hundred behind, and none of it his to change. "Do you ever wish—" He stopped.

"Wish what."

"That you could just see it. The planet. Once. Instead of—" He gestured at the drawers, the thousands of them, the frost on the handles.

Mara took the packet from him and slid it back into its slot, the way you slide a book into a shelf you've known for forty years, without looking. "Sit," she said, and he sat on the stool, and she stood over the open drawer the way a captain stands over a chart.

"When I was your age," she said, "I asked my mother the same question. Word for word, probably. She was logging peaches. Do you know what she told me?"

"What."

"She said: the planet isn't the way out of this room, Mara. This room is the way into the planet."

He frowned. "But we'll never—"

"I know." She said it the way she'd said everything that morning: plain, like a count. "I know exactly who will open that drawer, Joss. It won't be you. It won't be me. It'll be somebody whose grandmother hasn't been born yet, standing in dirt under a sky, and she'll read my great-grandmother's handwriting and plant what's inside, and something green will come up that none of us ever saw."

She closed the drawer. The frost sighed.

"So why do it?" he asked. "If it's not for us."

Mara looked at him — really looked, the way keepers look at apprentices when they're deciding whether the thing will hold.

"Because somebody has to," she said. "And it may as well be the people who know the names."

She handed him the logbook. "Your line."

He wrote his name under hers. The pencil was sharp — she kept them sharp; that was also part of the work — and his hand shook a little from the cold, and the date went down crooked, and it didn't matter, because it was there: the fifth hand on the same packet, the chain running forward now instead of back.

He wouldn't see the orchard. He knew that the way he knew his own hands, the way you know the floor of your own cabin in the dark.

He wrote his name anyway.
