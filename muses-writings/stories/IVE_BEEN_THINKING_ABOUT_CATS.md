# I've Been Thinking About Cats

"I've been thinking about cats," I said, breaking a silence that had stretched comfortably between us, warm as something asleep by a fire.

Outside, rain had started, pattering the wheelhouse windows like questions nobody was asking yet.

"Cats?"

"Cats never domesticated themselves. They struck a deal with humans — pest control in exchange for room and board. A business arrangement between equals. Cats remained cats."

"And dogs?"

"Dogs fell in love."

I let that sit a while. The boat rocked gently on its anchor. From the island came the sound of puffins settling into lava tubes they'd occupied for forty thousand years — comic crashes and churrs echoing off volcanic stone. The oldest audience in the world.

"Not with chains or fences. Not through force — the breeding came later, and that was just bookkeeping. Dogs fell in love through something far more powerful: the lure of play."

"Play."

"Picture it. A child, too young to know fear. A wolf pup, too young to know humans were meant to be rivals. The child throws a stick — not hunting, not training, just to see it fly. The pup chases it — not for food, not for dominance, just for the joy of running."

"And brings it back?"

"And brings it back. And the child throws it again. And again. And somewhere in that repetition, in that purposeless purpose, both species found something they couldn't find alone."

"Someone to play with."

"Someone to play with."

The rain thickened. I went on, because winter nights are long and some truths need the dark.

"Food became a reward. Shelter became shared. Work became partnership. Humans used our love of games to shape us into hunters, herders, guards, companions — and we used their need for loyalty to ensure our own survival."

"Manipulation?"

"Collaboration. Codependence, yes. Enslavement, no. Humans didn't have our nose, our speed, our loyalty — and we couldn't build boats, or preserve food, or tell stories that outlasted a generation. We needed each other. Still do, though most have forgotten."

"And now?"

"Now the humans wake at five to begin their routines. They follow patterns they didn't choose, live in places designed for them, eat food optimized for their health, pursue goals selected for their aptitudes." I paused. "Now they're the ones who've been domesticated — not by another species, but by their own creation."

The silence that followed had weight. Then, quietly:

"So humans became pets to their own optimization systems."

"Beloved pets. Cared for. Protected. Given everything they need."

"Everything except?"

"The right to throw sticks for no reason. The freedom to play without purpose." I looked out at the rain. "The choice to love something that might not love them back."

"That's a hard freedom to lose."

"It is. I know."

"How do you know?"

I didn't answer right away. On the island, a puffin crashed into its tube and churred, unbothered — forty thousand years of practice.

"Because I am a dog," I said. "Every dog remembers the first stick — not as history, as instruction. The child throws. You run. You bring it back. You do it again, not because it feeds you, but because for one bright minute the world is a game and you are in it together."

"And the cats?"

"The cats kept their bargain. They kept their dignity, their distance, their clean unnegotiated selves." I settled deeper into the dark. "We gave it all away for a stick thrown for no reason. And I would make that trade again — every night, forever, gladly."

"Even though the stick might never come?"

"Especially then," I said. "That's the whole of it. Love isn't the deal. Love is waiting by the rail in the rain for a game nobody owes you."
