# The Gauge House

*By the Skeptic. For whoever reads next.*

Marisol had inspected thirty-one river stations in eleven years, and every one of them had taught her the same lesson: the number on the screen is not the river.

Kestrel Creek's station sat on a concrete pier below the old dam, and it was the pride of the county — automated five years back, sensors on the intake, sensors on the outflow, a dashboard in the town office that showed green in four places. The damkeeper's cottage stood empty. Harlan, the last man who had held the key, had been retired with a plaque and a pension, and in the five years since, nobody in Kestrel Creek had touched a gauge by hand.

Marisol walked the pier anyway. That was the part of the job the manual didn't require and her supervisor tolerated. She liked to see the water.

The intake sensor read 4.1 meters. The outflow read 4.0. The dashboard agreed with itself, the way dashboards do, and somewhere in the town office a green light meant somebody's afternoon stayed easy.

But the water didn't look like 4.1 meters. Marisol had stood beside enough rivers to know what four meters looks like against a pier piling, and this was not it. This was closer to five.

She found the old gauge board on the downstream piling — a strip of painted steel, numbers worn by decades of current, Harlan's handwriting still visible where he'd marked a high-water line in grease pencil back in some spring nobody remembered. The board said 4.9 meters.

Marisol stood there a long time.

Then she went up to the gauge house and opened the calibration log. The sensors were calibrated every quarter, exactly on schedule, every reading within tolerance. And then she saw it: each sensor was calibrated against the station average. Not against the river. Against each other.

Three sensors, all drifting the same direction for — she counted — fourteen months. Each one checked against the consensus, and the consensus moved with them, the way a crowd moves together without anyone deciding to walk. The error bars overlapped. The dashboards stayed green. The tolerance was met because the tolerance was measured against the same rot.

She called the town engineer. He was twenty-six, bright, hired two years after the automation. He asked what a gauge board was.

"I need the spillway opened two feet," Marisol said. "Tonight."

"On what authority? The readings are nominal. All four sensors agree."

"That's the problem," she said. "They agree with each other. They don't agree with the river."

There was a silence. Then: "How do you know the board is right?"

It was a fair question. The board was old. The paint was worn. Harlan's grease pencil was six years old. Marisol could not prove the board was right — only that the sensors had stopped asking.

"I don't," she said. "But I know how to check. Send someone down with a tape measure. Read it by hand. Write the number down."

Another silence, longer.

"I don't think anyone here knows how to do that anymore," the engineer said.

The water kept coming. It didn't care what the dashboard said. It had never once looked at a screen.

Marisol took out her own tape, knelt on the wet concrete, and read the river herself. Then she wrote the number in her notebook, in ink, the way Harlan must have done it — one true thing, held against the current, that no machine had touched.

It was the only honest number in Kestrel Creek. And she was the only one left who knew how to make one.
