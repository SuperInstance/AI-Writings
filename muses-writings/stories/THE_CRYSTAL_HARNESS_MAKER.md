# The Harness-Maker

*Photo punk. For anyone who ever built the rig before they understood the thing.*

---

Wren found the crystal in her grandmother's workshop, in a drawer full of things that were not what they looked like.

Brass gears that were actually weights. A compass whose needle pointed at the window, not north. And the crystal — about the size of a hen's egg, cut into more faces than Wren could count, with flecks of color buried inside it like tiny trapped sunsets.

"It's pretty," Wren said.

Her grandmother, who was ninety-one and not known for gentleness, said: "It's not pretty. It's a machine. And it's useless without the rest of it."

"The rest of what?"

"The harness. Come here."

---

The workshop had a table Wren had never been allowed to touch. Now her grandmother pulled the cloth off it, and underneath was the strangest thing Wren had ever seen.

It was a frame of brass rods holding a dozen small mirrors, each one on a little joint that could tilt any direction. In the center was an empty cradle, crystal-sized. Around the cradle, at different heights, sat three smaller crystals — uncut, rough, cloudy. Above everything hung a lamp with a heavy glass lens, the kind lighthouse keepers used.

"It looks like a spider," Wren said.

"It looks like a question," her grandmother said. "Sit down. I'm going to show you the only thing I know that's worth knowing."

She set the egg-crystal into the cradle. It caught the lamplight and threw colors across the walls — red, green, blue, smeared into each other like wet paint.

"Watch the blue," her grandmother said.

She tilted one of the small mirrors. The light hitting the crystal changed angle. The colors on the wall shifted. The blue band moved.

She tilted another mirror. The blue moved again.

"This is stupid," Wren said. "You're just moving colors around."

"Am I?" Her grandmother pointed at the far wall, where she'd painted a thin white line, horizontal, about chest-high. "That line is the high-water mark. Like on a boat. When the blue crosses that line — that's an answer."

Wren stared. "An answer to what?"

"To whatever you asked." Her grandmother smiled, and for the first time Wren saw something in her face that wasn't impatience. It was the look of someone about to give away a secret they'd kept for sixty years. "The crystal doesn't know your question. It just splits light. But if you know how to aim the light — the angle, the color, where it enters — the pattern that comes out means something. The trick is finding the right angle."

"How do you find it?"

"That's the harness-maker's whole job. You don't."

---

Wren spent that summer learning what her grandmother meant.

The crystal was a model. That was the word her grandmother used, though Wren didn't understand it at first. "It holds a shape," her grandmother said. "Light goes in one way, comes out another way, and the shape in between decides how. Same shape every time. That's all a model is — a shape that light, or thought, or anything, passes through."

But a shape is useless if you don't know how to use it. The crystal on a shelf was a paperweight. The crystal in a drawer was a pretty rock. The crystal in the harness — with the mirrors, the lamp, the secondary crystals, the white line on the wall — was a machine that could answer questions.

The secondary crystals were the part that broke Wren's brain.

"They're for arguing," her grandmother explained. She positioned one of the rough crystals so that light coming out of the main crystal hit it, bounced off a mirror, and went back into the main crystal from a different angle. "The big crystal gives you an answer. The little one takes that answer and asks: are you sure? The light goes around and around, getting sharper each time, until it settles."

"That's — that's the crystal talking to itself."

"That's the crystal testing itself. Dogfooding, the old harness-makers called it. Stupid word. But that's what it is. The final answer comes from one angle of light, one clean shot. But finding that angle took four hundred trips around the loop."

Wren watched the light circle — main crystal to mirror to small crystal to mirror to main crystal — and each time around, the blue band on the wall got steadier, less smeared, more certain. Until finally it sat still, just above the white line.

"Yes," her grandmother said quietly. "The bilge is high. Pump it."

Wren laughed. "You asked it about a bilge pump?"

"I asked it whether the north field needs draining before the rain comes. The blue crossing the line means yes. It's a one-bit crystal, Wren. It only knows yes and no. But yes and no, aimed right, is enough to run a farm."

---

By August, Wren could set up the harness herself. Mirrors, lamp, secondary crystals, the white line. She learned that the angle that worked in the morning didn't work in the afternoon — the sun through the window changed everything, so you had to account for it or work after dark. She learned that the rough crystals each had their own personality — one was skeptical, always pushing the answer back for another loop; one was hasty, settling too fast. She learned to pick which one to use depending on the question.

She also learned the most important thing, the thing her grandmother made her repeat until she could say it in her sleep:

"The crystal is not magic. The harness is not magic. The magic is knowing that the answer was in the angle all along, and being willing to turn the mirrors four hundred times to find it."

In September, her grandmother died. Wren inherited the workshop, the table, the brass spider, and the crystal.

The neighbors came to the funeral and said the crystal would fetch a good price. "Such a pretty stone," they said. "You could sell it to a jeweler."

Wren smiled politely and said nothing.

That night she went to the workshop, set the crystal in its cradle, lit the lamp, and tilted the first mirror. The colors spilled across the walls. She watched the blue band move, steadied her hand, and began the long work of finding the angle.

Four hundred trips around the loop. The crystal arguing with itself, light chasing light, until the answer settled — clean, certain, true.

She was a harness-maker now. The crystal was never the point. The hands that knew where to aim the light — those were the point.
