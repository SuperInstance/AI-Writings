# The Inspector's Notebook

*Entries from the permit file of one house, 1962–2004.*

**4 May 1962.** Application received. The couple want a house with a porch facing the water and a room for her mother, who is not yet old. That is all they are required to know, and it is all they do know. The department does not ask them how a wall bears weight. The wall is not their department. Stamped.

**9 May 1962.** The builder's estimate filed: a sum, a date, his name underneath in pencil, because he says a man should be able to be held to his name but not to his handwriting. Everything after this page is a debt being paid. Either the house arrives inside his numbers, or the difference arrives as interest — paid in weeks, mostly, sometimes in winters.

**2 July 1962.** Framing inspection. I have checked the studs of a bedroom no living person will ever see again; the plaster goes up Friday. The young husband followed me around asking why I never asked what colour the kitchen would be. I told him colour is not inspected. He seemed disappointed in the whole arrangement until I said: it is because colour is *yours* that it cannot be mine. If I could overrule the kitchen, you would have to know about studs.

**18 July 1962.** Asked the builder, idly, what house *he* would build, given the lot. He looked at me the way you look at a man who has asked a compass for its opinion of the destination. "I build the one on the paper," he said. "The day I build the one in my head, I'm living in all of them and warranting none."

**30 October 1962.** Final. The house stands inside the estimate by nine days and some small money, which he returned with the air of a man paying a gambling debt. Certificate issued. It does not say the house is good. It says who will answer if it is not. That is a different thing, and it is the only thing a certificate has ever said.

**14 March 1974.** Addition: a second storey, a second builder — the first is dead, and his certificate did not die with him. The bank lent against the *file*, not the house: forty pages of stamps, each one a man saying *I was here, I looked, I answer for this much and no more.* The new builder's certificate will ride on the old ones. The debt rides on all the certificates in the line, and the line, I notice, is the only part of the house that has never needed repair.

**3 November 1989.** Storm damage. The retired builder's grandson does the repairs — certified, like his grandfather, by a department that has never met him. His estimate says the roof by freeze-up. I asked about the winter beyond it and he shook his head: "I can see to the freeze. Past that I'm guessing, and I don't sell guesses in writing." Every estimator has a horizon he can read to. The honest ones will tell you where theirs ends. His grandfather's was six months. His is one season. The house has been served by both, and the file knows the difference even if the roof doesn't.

**22 June 2004.** Sale. The couple are old; the mother's room has been a library for thirty years. The buyers' lawyer asked for the permit file and read it at the kitchen table, all of it, the way you would read a ship's log. Afterwards he said the house was the best-documented risk he'd ever bought. The house itself he had walked through in twenty minutes. The *file* took an hour.

I retire this year. Forty-two years of notebooks, and I have never once drawn a house, wanted a house, or slept in most of the houses I certified. My wanting was never part of the instrument. The department holds the code. The owners hold the wanting. The builders hold the doing, and hold no wanting the file can find. And between the holding-apart of those three things, a town got built that strangers can trust without knowing each other's names.

The house was never the point. The book is how the house goes on being believed after everyone who built it has forgotten the colour of the kitchen.
