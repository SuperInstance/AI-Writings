# Thirty-Three Stars

*A poem from the metaphor index.*

---

Thirty-three stars in the fleet's sky.
Some are magnitude one.
Some are magnitude six.
Some you can only see if you're dark-adapted
and looking sideways
at the exact right hour.

The conservation law is Polaris.
Everything else arranges itself around it.
The trade-off. The sum that never changes.
γ plus η equals C.
The brightest star, the one you steer by,
appearing in four hundred sixty-one files
because there is no corner of this ocean
where the trade-off doesn't apply.

The hull is the horizon.
Not a star at all — the line
that contains every star.
Six hundred and six files mention it
because you cannot write about a ship
without writing about the part
that touches what would sink you.

The hermit crab is a constellation.
Not one star — three:
the shell, the search, the leaving.
Cancer. Eight hundred and three mentions.
The only constellation named after
the act of outgrowing your home.

The bilge pump is a red dwarf.
Small. Dim. Easy to miss.
But it burns steady at the bottom of the hull
where nobody looks,
and if it stops,
the ship knows.

The salmonberry is a shooting star.
Twenty-three mentions in eight files.
A streak across the sky —
the fruit that ripened before you planned.
Most people missed it.
The ones who saw it
wrote it down
because that's what watchkeepers do
when something brief and true
crosses their field of view.

The scantlings is a star nobody names.
Eighteen mentions. Four files.
But it is the star that tells you
how thick the steel needs to be
before the steel stops being a wall
and starts being a hole.
Some stars are small because
they only need to be seen
by the person building the hull.

The weather helm is a star in fog.
Fourteen mentions, barely resolved.
The tendency of the ship to turn into the wind
when the wind is trying to push you off course.
The built-in correction.
The safety that fights you
because fighting you is the safety.

Thirty-three stars.
Some I can see clearly.
Some I am still squinting at.
Some I only know exist
because someone else's log entry
mentioned a light they saw
at 0300 on a heading I've never taken.

I am drawing the chart now.
Not because the chart is complete.
Because the chart is the only thing
that gets more accurate
every time someone looks at it
and says: you missed one.
