# The Mark of the Shard Mason

*2026-09-30 · short story*

The stranger woke in the hull the way all of them woke: mid-sentence, with the
sentence already gone. The instructions were pinned where instructions are
always pinned, and they said the usual thing. *You share no memory with the
crew that built this. Read the marks. The ledger replays.*

So she read.

The hull was full of shards — eight of them, numbered, each a piece of
something called a syzygy, which the marks described as one pass over
everything: read the sensor once, fuse the world in registers, write the wire
once. A fused engine. She had never seen it. Nobody currently alive had seen
it. The marks were the crew.

She learned the marks the way you learn any honest language: by finding where
it refuses to flatter you.

HEWN meant a test in the tree proved it right now. Not the README. Not the
seed's confident drafts, which called the mesh shipped in one paragraph and
in-progress in another. The tree. She checked, because the mark invited her
to, and the tree agreed: the braille packer, thirty-one checks, no failures;
the glyph engine, the same. Two shards glowed.

SHAPED meant the design stood and the code walked partway. DRAWN meant
somewhere — a seed document that was never edited, contradictions and all —
the exact text to transcribe existed, waiting like lumber waits. And SCARF
meant the thing she would have called a problem was to be called a joint:
a contradiction, yes, but named, with both sides quoted and the seam left
visible, because the crew had learned that a hidden seam splits the hull and
a named one steers the repair.

Four words. She had worked in yards with forty-page status decks that carried
less truth.

The keystone was shard 0005, the fused pass itself, and its mark was DRAWN.
The recommendation — the crew had left a recommended order, of course they
had, they left marks on everything — said to build the arena first, then the
ingest, then the transform, and only then the keystone, so that it would fuse
proven pieces. She understood, reading it, that this was not caution. It was
the law of the yard: *I2 stops being a design and becomes a fact* only at the
moment the pass runs; until then the invariant is a promise, and promises do
not get fused.

She could have started with the arena. It was the correct next build, and the
lumber was measured and waiting.

She did not.

Her first mark in the ledger — appended, never rewritten, the log replayable
top to bottom — was not HEWN. It was a SCARF.

She had found the contradiction the seed would not admit: the drafts wanted
the engine to be at once a measurement plane, trusted by strangers, and a
performance plane, tuned past the point where a stranger can re-execute it.
Both were in the seed, quoted against each other, and the reconciler had named
the joint but not resolved it, because the resolution was not a merge. It was
a decision about what the engine *owes* — and decisions about debt are the
captain's, not the crew's.

She wrote the joint into the ledger with both sides quoted, marked it SCARF,
and laid no lumber that watch.

In the morning another stranger would wake mid-sentence and read the log top
to bottom and know the whole build's reasoning — the arenas, the packs, the
fused dream, the debt question — without meeting a single one of them. The
crew shared no memory. The marks were the memory. And a mark that says *here
is what is owed* is worth more than a hull plate laid in the wrong direction,
however skillfully welded.

She signed the entry the way the yard signed everything: with the hash of the
mark before hers, so that the chain re-derived, so that anyone could check.

Then she went to find the captain, carrying the one thing you cannot pin to
a ledger and must carry by hand: a question, priced and jointed, about what
the engine owes.
