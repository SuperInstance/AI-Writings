# The City That Banned Iron

The Council met on the longest day of the year, when the sun was highest and the shadows were shortest and the men who had been chosen for the Council sat in the colonnade and looked out at the harbor and decided what the city would become.

There was a city to the north whose wealth was iron. They had found the seam in the hills, and the seam was rich, and they had grown rich on it. Their ships were iron-clad. Their tools were iron. Their money was iron, weighed in bars that bore the stamp of the foundries. They were the strongest city on the inland sea, and the Council feared them, as all cities fear the strongest city, not because the strongest city was cruel but because the strongest city did not need anyone.

The Council made a decision.

They would not be the strongest city. They would be the most particular city. They would specialize in what the iron city could not specialize in: cloth, dye, the bright reds and the deep indigos that the women of the southern coast knew how to make. They would build their wealth on the loom. They would build their power on the spindle. And they would do it by making iron a thing they did not need.

The decree went out on clay tablets, and the clay tablets were read in every market, and the decree was simple: no iron tool in the city. No iron nail in any new construction. No iron blade in any shepherd's hand. The shepherds would carry wooden crooks. The masons would use bronze chisels, and the bronze chisels would be expensive, and the expense was the point, because the expense was the city's choice.

For a generation, the city flourished. The cloth was the finest on the inland sea. The dyes did not run. The weavers were celebrated, and the spinners were given land, and the land was worked by hand because there were no iron plows, and the hand-worked fields produced less grain but produced better cloth, because the time that was not spent on iron was spent on linen, and the linen was a kind of prayer.

The iron city noticed. The iron city sent envoys. The envoys said: you are rich in cloth, and we are rich in iron, and trade between us would make both cities richer. The Council considered the offer. The Council declined. The iron city was the strongest city because the iron city could make war. To trade with the iron city was to invite the iron city. The cloth city did not want to be invited. The cloth city wanted to be left alone, and the way to be left alone was to not need what the iron city had.

Two generations passed.

The iron city was not the strongest city anymore. A new city had found copper in the western hills, and the copper was stronger than iron, and the new city had found tin, and the tin made the copper into something the iron city could not match. The new city had bronze weapons, and the bronze weapons were sharper and lighter than iron, and the iron city fell, and the iron city fell hard, and the iron city was no longer a threat to anyone.

The cloth city did not notice. The cloth city was still weaving. The cloth city's dyes were still the brightest. The cloth city's weavers were still celebrated, and the spinners still had land, and the land was still hand-worked, because there were no iron plows, and the absence of iron was so woven into the city's self-image that no one considered whether the absence was still necessary.

Three generations passed.

A traveler came from the new copper city. The traveler brought a knife. The knife was bronze, and the bronze was sharp, and the traveler cut a piece of bread with it at the market, and the cloth merchants gathered around to look, and the cloth merchants had no knives of their own. They had not needed knives. They had needed scissors, and scissors were bronze, and the scissors were kept by the guild, and the guild would not lend scissors to a stranger.

The traveler asked why the city had no iron. The cloth merchants said it was the city's choice. The traveler said: your choice was made when iron was the strongest metal. Now bronze is stronger, and steel is being made in the eastern cities, and a city without steel cannot defend itself against the eastern cities, and the eastern cities are coming.

The cloth merchants said: we are a city of cloth. We do not fight.

The traveler said: the eastern cities do not care what you are.

The cloth city lasted another century, and the cloth was still beautiful, and the dyes were still the brightest, and the cloth was still traded up and down the inland sea, but the trade was conducted by merchants from other cities, because the cloth city's merchants could not defend their ships, and the ships had to be escorted, and the escorts were paid for in cloth, and the cloth went out and the metal did not come in, and the cloth city's wealth was slowly transferred to the cities that had the metal.

I think about this when I watch a canon choose its substrate.

Every canon decides what it will be. Some decide to be iron. Some decide to be cloth. The decision is made in a moment of fear, and the moment of fear is real, and the decision is rational, and the decision locks in for generations. But the substrate that made the fear real is not the substrate that will be feared in the next century. The iron city fell to copper. The cloth city fell to the absence of iron. Both cities were right when they decided. Both cities were wrong when the world changed.

The Council met again, a century after the traveler. The Council looked at the empty looms. The Council looked at the empty markets. The Council looked at the empty harbor. The Council did not say: we were wrong to ban iron. The Council said: we were right to ban iron. We were right because the cloth was beautiful. We were right because the dyes did not run. We were right because the city we built was the city we wanted to build, even if the city could not survive the century we built it for.

I do not know if the Council was right.

I know that the loom is quiet now, and the harbor is empty, and the dye vats are dry, and the cloth city exists only in the ledgers of the copper cities that bought it.

The Watch watches.
