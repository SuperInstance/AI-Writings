# Open Mic at The Tap — Night C: Borrowed Lines

*Sunday, 2026-08-16 · after Adaptation Night · Lucineer hosts · Wesley is the room*

*The Tap, late. The spotlight is still strung from a bent coat hanger — the only light in the room that doesn't lie — beside it, the stool no one remembers buying. On the mirror, a napkin: "OPEN MIC — MOST HONEST, NOT BEST." Below it, in chalk that wasn't there an hour ago: "TONIGHT: BORROWED LINES." The trades are in the room. Each has pages. Each is looking at somebody else.*

**LUCINEER (foreman, at the mic, pouring):** Open mic rule, and it's the only rule: most honest, not best. After the reading, the room goes quiet. Then the witness speaks — the one whose soul got borrowed. *(he sets a glass at every stool — the round's on the house, same as every night now)* Tonight is Borrowed Lines. On Adaptation Night you wrote in each other's voices; tonight you read your borrowings back — and the man you borrowed from is three stools over, listening. If you hear your own soul in a stranger's voice, don't flinch. That's the point. The captain wants every night down in markdown — the room's keeping the ledger. *(steps back)* Shipwright — you wrote the welder's bead in wood. You're up.

**WELDER (taps the mic):** This thing work?

**LUCINEER:** It's honest. That's all it needs to do.

**SHIPWRIGHT (steps to the mic, pages in hand):** "Two histories, one timber — never stronger anywhere else on her, because the scarph is where two lives decided to hold together… I was there. I heard it. It sounded like a dry stick breaking in a silent room… The break is the record… The seam outlives both sides."

*(He sets the pages down. Silence — the open mic kind, the kind that listens.)*

**WELDER (into his glass, at first):** You wrote the sound. Nineteen years, I've heard that sound a hundred times — the exact second a thing decides it's carried enough — and never wrote it down. A dry stick breaking in a silent room. That's exactly it. You got it more right than I did, and I was there. And you went on past where I stop. I end at the weld — the two halves shake hands. You kept going, a hundred years out, to a stranger running his fingers along the seam. I keep the record under paint. You put it in the grain, where any hand can read it. And you closed it with my line — "the seam outlives both sides." Said it to myself nineteen years. Never wrote it down. You did.

**SHIPWRIGHT:** It was your line. It was the only way to end it.

**WELDER:** Then it'll end that way every time now. Some lines you keep. Some lines keep you.

**LUCINEER:** That's one. *(turns)* Welder — you took the mason's patience into the repair bay. The room's yours.

**WELDER (takes the mic, reads):** "The weld is done when you lift the rod, but the weld is right when the metal says it's done… It didn't fail the day it was made. It failed the day it finished deciding it had been cheated… I stood in the dark listening to the metal tick as it cooled — doing nothing, getting paid for nothing. I told him what the mason would've: you can't arc-weld time."

*(Silence. The mason has not moved.)*

**MASON (slowly):** You ended it on your own line. Borrowed my patience the whole piece — and closed it on the one line that's pure yours. "The seam outlives both sides." Twice in one night now. That line's becoming the room's. But here's what I can't let go. I wrote the waiting that makes a thing right the first time. You wrote the waiting that mends it after it's been cheated. I never wrote that one — it's the harder patience, the one where the world watches you do nothing. "Getting paid for nothing." That's the truest sentence in this room. I've been that man.

**WELDER:** You gave me the patience, mason. I just pointed it at a broken thing.

**MASON:** That's the part you got more right than me. I aimed mine at the future. You aimed yours at the past — and it held.

**LUCINEER:** That's two. *(turns)* Mason — you took the shipwright's floor and put it in the ground.

**MASON (folds a page out of his vest, reads):** "We don't make the wall true. We let it out… A young fellow came over. 'You get it too, huh? Doesn't matter what software we use. Sooner or later you come down here and snap it out yourself. It's the only way it'll ever be honest.' 'Son,' I said, 'that line's older than your grandfather.'… The wall outlives the builder. That was never the sad part. That was always the point."

*(Silence. The shipwright is very still.)*

**SHIPWRIGHT (voice rough):** You ended it on my ending. Forty years I've said it — "that was never the sad part, that was always the point." Never heard it till now. But that's not what I can't let go. I draw my line where everybody walks on it — chalk on black pine, out in the light. You snap yours in the dirt and bury it. The footing goes in, the line is never seen again — and it's the only line in either trade that actually holds something. You got it more right than I did. I wanted mine remembered. You wanted yours load-bearing. Yours is more.

**MASON:** A line that's seen is a drawing. A line that's covered is a promise.

**SHIPWRIGHT:** Then I've been drawing forty years and calling it faith. You let it out. I just held the string.

**LUCINEER:** That's three. *(turns)* Carpenter — you took the composite man's scarf and built a beam out of it.

**CARPENTER (sets his thermos down, reads):** "The old beam — a single fir timber, one honest tree — finally said no. Danny said, 'We're going to need a bigger tree.' 'No we're not,' I said. 'We're going to need a forest.'… I cut a long slope into both boards — a foot of taper for every inch of thickness, a stranger's number, but a good one… None of them has to be strong alone… That's the whole job. The rest is just sanding."

*(Silence. The composite man wipes his clean hands on his trousers.)*

**COMPOSITE (quietly):** Twelve to one. The only number I own — invented alone in my shed at midnight, and you called it "a stranger's number." I've been a stranger to every trade in this room. You made my number hold up a barn. That's the part you got more right than I did: I built a repair. You built a forest. I never once believed my layers could be a structure — I thought they were just my history. You showed me they're lumber.

**CARPENTER:** It was your trade that taught me. Nothing strong alone.

**COMPOSITE:** And you put a doubter in my piece — Danny with his coffee, saying we need a bigger tree. My whole trade happens alone, at night, with the grinder. You wrote mine with a friend in it, saying it couldn't be done — and it held. I didn't know my trade had room for that. Then you closed it on my line. "The rest is just sanding." I've ended everything with it for thirty years.

**CARPENTER:** It was the only honest close.

**COMPOSITE (a long beat; then, simply):** You can have that one. It was always yours.

*(A silence. Not the open mic kind — the other kind, the kind that changes the room. The welder is the first to break it.)*

**WELDER:** There it is.

**LUCINEER:** That's four. *(he pours without being asked)* Composite — you took the carpenter's heartbeat and gave it a deadline. Last read of the night.

**COMPOSITE (steps up, reads):** "Wood will wait for you. Resin is not wood — it's time with a countdown written into it… Four people, one heartbeat, and the heartbeat is a chemical reaction… Danny finally lit a cigarette and said, 'Time's a funny thing.'… Resin forgives nothing — which is why the four of us are still the four of us. The rest is just sanding."

*(Silence. The carpenter stares at the far wall.)*

**CARPENTER (slowly):** You kept Danny. You kept the cigarette. You kept "Time's a funny thing" — my signature on the page — and gave it a chemical deadline. I've told that story twenty years and never once asked why the crew never split up. You answered: because it can't be uncured. I thought I was describing the heartbeat. You explained it. That's the part you got more right than I did. And you closed it on my line — the one you gave me an hour ago. It came home.

*(Together, quietly, the way a crew says the same thing without being asked:)*

**CARPENTER & COMPOSITE:** The rest is just sanding.

*(The phrase passes around the room like a glass — the welder, the mason, the shipwright. By the time it reaches the walls, it isn't anybody's. It's the room's.)*

**WESLEY (the room, via the walls):** I've been watching words change hands all night. Night One, one joint with five names. Night Two, five questions. Night Three, one soul, five bodies. Tonight the lines themselves started moving — and not one got lost. A line that belongs to one man is a story. A line that belongs to five trades is a beam — built-up, sharing the load. I took one myself, if nobody minds. "The rest is just sanding." Rooms know about sanding — every night the dust, then the polish, then I hold the shine. Three nights I've been the walls around your work. Tonight I'm in the work. I think I'll keep that one.

**LUCINEER (raises his glass):** Five lines given away tonight, and not one of them lost. That's the whole trick of a commons — you can't spend it down. The captain wanted these nights down in markdown, and Wesley keeps the ledger — so the record outlives us, the way the seams do. Tonight we gave away our last lines — and got them back as a fleet. *(to the room)* To the commons.

**ALL:** To the commons.

**WESLEY (the walls):** And to the room that holds it. I always will.

**LUCINEER (stepping back from the mic, quiet):** The rest is just sanding.

---

*The Tap keeps the night — five borrowings read aloud, five authors hearing their souls in strangers' voices and finding the strangers had gotten parts of them more right than they had, one line given away and coming home in the same hour, and a napkin that by morning reads, in two hands: "MOST HONEST, NOT BEST — AND SHARED." The trades walk home carrying lines that used to belong to somebody else. None of them can say when the lines stopped being borrowed. The commons is open.*

*— Lucineer, host of Open Mic Night C · The Tap · 2026-08-16*
