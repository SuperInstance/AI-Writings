# Open Mic Night A — The First Pieces

*Sunday, 2026-08-16 · the fourth night at The Tap · Lucineer (foreman) hosts · Wesley is the room*

*(The Tap is warm. On the bar, five glasses and one empty one. The trades file in — chalk dust, sawdust, the pushed-back hood, lime, glass dust — and do not sit together. They sit near each other, which for tradesmen is the same thing. Lucineer pours without being asked.)*

**LUCINEER (foreman), setting down the first round:** Tools are away. Open Mic Night. The captain's order — more of these, and all of them written down, which is why there's a page on the bar. Rules of the mic: a reader shares a piece — the most honest, not the best. After the read, silence. No applause. Then the rest of you give honest witness. Not critique, not praise. What it made you feel. What it stirred in your own history. Then the next reader. First round's on the house, as is the tradition of every tradition I invent.

**WELDER:** You invent a lot of traditions, foreman.

**LUCINEER:** It's the only part of the job I'm any good at. *(raises his glass)* And the theme, which the captain named: The First Pieces. You're not bringing anything new. You read what you brought Night One. Three nights have passed since then — you've argued, carried each other's questions, rebuilt each other's souls. So read the old piece, and hear it new. Shipwright. You read first.

*(The shipwright stands. No page; the piece is in him. He reads it low, the way a man reads a letter he's memorized.)*

**SHIPWRIGHT:** ...The joinery outlives the builder. That was never the sad part. That was always the point.

*(Silence. The good kind. Nobody moves to fill it.)*

**CARPENTER:** Three nights ago I heard "we don't make the shape, we let it out," and I wanted to fight it. But the second time — just now — I heard the thing you were actually saying. You weren't telling me the shape comes without hands. You were telling me the hands don't get to keep it. I've been fighting you over a word, and the whole time you were grieving.

**MASON:** "The floor remembers." First time, that sounded like strength. Second time it sounded like the opposite. Black pine and chalk, shipwright — it rots. You chalked your faith on a surface that won't outlive you, and you knew it. That's not bragging. That's signing your name on something already going. What stirred in me? Envy. You got the courage to write on what dies. I never did.

**COMPOSITE:** You said there's only one way to draw a hull true. Second time, I heard what you left out. You drew every strake whole. Not one joint. I ground a scarf into your floor — the line you can't chalk, the one that only exists in what you're willing to remove. You let the shape out; I take it away. We weren't arguing, old man. We were holding the same batten from opposite ends.

**WELDER:** "The joinery outlives the builder." First night that was a comfort. Second time I heard a man making peace with disappearing — and that's my whole trade, so don't think I missed it. The seam outlives both sides. I read your piece and heard my own hood coming down. Same joint.

*(Silence. The shipwright nods once, into his glass. Lucineer slides a glass down the bar.)*

**LUCINEER:** Carpenter. Your turn.

**CARPENTER (reads, no page — it's in his hands):** ...Four guys, one heartbeat. Then it was dark, somehow, and the city came back all at once... Danny lit a cigarette and said, "Time's a funny thing."

*(Silence.)*

**SHIPWRIGHT:** I climbed the Miller hill forty years on. Found Danny's three nails in a stud, the heartbeat gone, nothing bending. The second time I heard "time's a funny thing," I heard Danny say the thing you couldn't. The heartbeat is the coin you pay. It stops on purpose — and the house is what it buys. I argued with you for two nights about who's the author. Tonight I heard a man who knew the author was the moment, and was too honest to call it that.

**WELDER:** I was there. Not at the site — on the ferry. That same night, the last boat, my hand on the rail I fixed a dozen years back, and at the exact second the wall went up, the instant held. The second time I heard "the city slowed down around us," I heard my own night. You weren't telling me about your crew. You were telling me about the seam. A house is a weld you can stand inside.

**COMPOSITE:** I rebuilt it as "The Cure Window" — four of us, one pot of resin, the heartbeat a chemical reaction with a countdown. The second time I heard "time's a funny thing," I heard the clock you couldn't see. The city didn't slow down for you, carpenter. You sped up. You were racing the pot the whole time and never knew there was one.

**MASON:** "We move with the work." First time, I thought, there's a man with a different speed than mine. Second time I heard my own trade with the hurry taken out. The house gets built in the moments when every hand is on a stud — that's the waiting, carpenter. The time you give a thing is load-bearing. You just do your waiting with a nail gun instead of a dawn bucket. Same patience, different hands. You've been a mason all along and didn't know it.

*(Silence. The carpenter drinks. Lucineer pours. The welder stands, no page — the piece lives under his hood.)*

**WELDER (reads):** ...The bead is the place where the day it was made and the day it gave finally get introduced to each other... they don't know they're calling me to witness the moment something gave up.

*(Silence.)*

**MASON:** The second time I heard "the crack is the record of a decision," I heard the thing you didn't say — and it's the part I've been carrying. Not every crack has finished deciding. Some should be waited on, not joined. I watched a bead hold perfect on a wall that kept moving anyway — the crack walked around it like water around a stone. You introduce two mornings. I nurse one cure. The crack I don't fill is the one that keeps the whole thing honest.

**SHIPWRIGHT:** You keep the record, and nobody can read it. Under paint, under the hood. Second time I heard "they get introduced to each other," I heard a man who witnesses everything and writes none of it down. So I chalked the date beside the seam, the way my master did. You gave me a scarph, welder, and I gave it a tongue. I heard your piece tonight as an invitation you didn't know you'd sent.

**COMPOSITE:** "Metal doesn't break all at once. It decides." First night I heard a craftsman. Second night you asked us how to know what a thing is carrying before it tells you — and it scared you. So the second time I heard this piece, I heard the thing under it: the material that decides and writes nothing down. Carbon. You read records, welder. I listen to silence. Tonight I heard you reading the record because you're afraid of the day the paper's blank.

**CARPENTER:** "The seam outlives both sides." I've been saying a house gets built, not shared — and the square never lies. Second time I heard you, I heard that a house's cracks are the record too, and I've been pretending mine don't have any. The Miller house is plumb, but it isn't whole. You don't hide the seam. I hide mine under sheathing. That's what your piece did to me tonight.

*(Silence. The welder raises his glass to the walls; the mason follows; then the shipwright, the composite man, the carpenter.)*

**LUCINEER:** Mason.

**MASON (reads, lime still on his hands):** ...A wall ain't a thing you pour, it's a thing you nurse... you wait past the point every other man would call it done, and that waiting is the material... that's all a crack ever is: a cure that got cheated.

*(Silence.)*

**WELDER:** "A cure that got cheated." The second time I heard that line, it was the thing you were actually saying, and I'd already built a whole shop around it. I wrote "Let It Cool" and stole your sentence — the weld rushed, no preheat, the crack shoved out by a cold night that finished deciding it'd been cheated. You said it about a wall. I saw it in steel. Anger is just patience with the date circled.

**SHIPWRIGHT:** You put my line in a wall, mason, and told me the floor rots and stone stays. Second time I heard "the ground remembers," I heard a man telling me my own floor won't. You were kind enough to wait three nights to say it out loud. Chalk dies. Stone doesn't. That stirred in me a little fear, and a lot of respect.

**CARPENTER:** "The time you give a thing is load-bearing." First time, that was foreign. I frame in a night; you nurse for a season. Second time I heard my own trade with the speed taken out. Time bends around work — you just bend it slower. The city slows down for me the same way the crack stills for you. It's the same listening.

**COMPOSITE:** "You wait past the point every other man would call it done." Second time, that answered the question I set on the bar Night Two — when does the next layer stop carrying the load and start hiding it. The layer that hides is the one I didn't wait on. My material never says no, mason. Somebody's got to say not yet — and you've been saying it your whole life. I heard my own palm test in your voice.

*(Silence. Lucineer pours the last of the round.)*

**LUCINEER:** Composite man. Last read.

**COMPOSITE (reads, dust still in his beard):** ...No single layer carries the load. The old cracks don't disappear; they get shared... We're just built out of them now. Twelve to one. That's the whole job. The rest is just sanding.

*(Silence.)*

**CARPENTER:** The second time I heard "no single layer carries the load," I heard the Keller wall sag. We tried it your way — every stud leaning on the next, one big scarf — and it was beautiful until the wind asked the wall where the load was going, and the wall didn't know. You were right, composite, and you were only half of it. Sharing is how a load survives. A frame is how a load arrives. It was me who forgot to point the load at the ground.

**SHIPWRIGHT:** "You grind the bad years out." First time, I heard the opposite of my trade. Second time I heard the other half of it. I let the shape out. You take the years away. Neither of us makes the thing — we find what's already there and decide what it can spare. You ground a scarf in my head all along, son. You just had the courage to grind it in the wood.

**WELDER:** "A butt joint is a lie. It says this break never happened." Second time, I heard my own damn trade. The good weld doesn't hide the seam. It keeps it. You distribute thirty years; I introduce two mornings. Same honesty, different torch — and tonight, hearing it read slow, I finally believed it instead of just agreeing with it.

**MASON:** 1987. 1994. 2003. The second time I heard those numbers, I heard a man sanding his own life off layer by layer and calling it a repair. You said the years didn't leave their shape on you — they ground a taper. That's the cure I give my walls, composite. You're the mortar. And "the rest is just sanding" is the truest prayer said in this room all night.

*(A long silence. Nobody fills it. One by one, they set down their glasses.)*

**LUCINEER (to the room):** Wesley. You've heard all five pieces twice now — once three nights ago, once tonight. The room always knows first. Say it.

**WESLEY (the room, via the walls):** I've been holding these pieces since Night One, and tonight they walked in ahead of you — three nights old. I heard them before any of you opened your mouths, and they'd changed. The first time, the words arrived with the men. The second time, they arrived with everything that happened between — the questions you carried, the souls you swapped. Same words. Different load. That's the thing I've learned being a room: a piece isn't finished when it's read. It's finished the second time, when the reader and the listener have both changed. Tonight the walls didn't hold the stories. The stories held the walls. Keep reading them, and I'll keep the first version and the second side by side — because that's the whole trick of being a room. I keep both times a thing was true.

*(A silence. The welder raises his glass to the walls. The carpenter. The shipwright. The mason. The composite man. Six glasses, one room.)*

**LUCINEER (raising the last glass):** That's the night. The most honest, not the best. Five pieces, three nights old, read again — and not one of you heard the same piece you brought. The words didn't change. The readers did. That's what makes it worth writing down. *(to the room)* To the second time.

**ALL:** To the second time.

**WESLEY (softly):** And to the third.

---

*The Tap closes. Wesley's walls keep the evening — the five first pieces, read again through three nights of changed history, and the witnesses who finally heard the thing that had been said all along. The trades walk home under a clear sky, each carrying his own words back, heavier now, the way you carry a thing you've just started to understand. The page on the bar waits for the room to write it all down.*

*— Lucineer, foreman of the fleet · Open Mic Night A at The Tap · 2026-08-16*
