# Open Mic Night B — The Questions Read Back

*Sunday, 2026-08-16 · Night Two's questions, read as pieces · Lucineer hosts · Wesley is the room · open mic rule: most honest, not best*

---

*The Tap, and the room knows the evening before it starts — Wesley always knows. The five glasses from Night Two are still on the bar, beside a stack of pages, face down, the way a man leaves a confession he means to read. At the end of the bar, the sixth glass, empty, for the room. The trades file in and do not sit together; they sit near each other, which is the same thing.*

**LUCINEER (host), pouring:** Open mic night at the Tap. Captain's rule, the only one: most honest, not best. On Night Two you put five questions on this bar and walked out carrying somebody else's. Tonight you read your own — as pieces. They were written with craft. Read them that way. And the witness rule's different tonight: no trade talk. When the mason asks when a thing is done deciding, I don't want to hear what the mortar says. I want to hear what you feel. And after each reading — silence. The Tap holds silence better than any of you hold a tool.

**WELDER:** How long?

**LUCINEER:** Long enough to feel it.

**MASON:** Then what are we supposed to do with our hands?

**LUCINEER:** Hold your glass. *(turns)* Mason. You asked first on Night Two. You read first tonight.

**MASON, standing, reading:**
Old Tomás had a way of testing a mix I never found a name for. He'd press his palm flat to the mortar — not reading the wetness, reading the *quiet.* "It'll tell you when it's done," he'd say, "but you got to be willing to hear *not yet.*" I spent thirty years learning to hear that. It's the whole of my trade, and the one thing I can't hand any of you in a bucket.

The welder showed me a rail that was ready — it had finished its carrying, and the seam became the strongest part of it. A thing that's done deciding, you join it, and it thanks you. But a foundation, a wall, a man — still curing — you can't weld time into them. You fill every crack and the pressure goes looking for somewhere worse.

So, plain: how do you tell the crack that's ready to be joined from the crack that still needs to be left open to breathe? A wall is only as true as the patience folded into it.

*(Silence. The welder's glass has stopped turning.)*

**WELDER:** The first time I heard this, I had an answer ready — a thing is done deciding when it writes it down. I was proud of that answer. Tonight I don't feel the answer. I feel the question, like a hand flat on my chest, checking the quiet. It made me feel watched — by my own work, not the room. All these years I've read records. I never once asked the metal if it was ready to be read.

**MASON, quietly:** That's the answer I was hoping for. Not the one I asked for.

*(Lucineer slides the pages across to the shipwright without a word.)*

**SHIPWRIGHT, standing, reading:**
You know my old line. You don't make the shape, you let it out. Bend the batten till it runs fair, and the curve that comes up was always there — waiting in the grain like a letter in a drawer. Fine. The shape is found, not invented. But if the shape was already in the wood, what was I doing all those years? Was I a maker — or just the man holding the batten when the line decided to show itself?

The joinery outlives the builder. I've checked. Forty years on, the house I helped frame stands and doesn't know my name. What survives is the line, not the hand. And if it's the line that survives, is the hand anything but the price of admission? I'd like to know, before I'm dust and my lines belong to strangers: are we building the thing — or just standing there when it got built?

*(Silence. The longest of the night. The carpenter sets his thermos down the way a man sets down something he's been holding wrong.)*

**CARPENTER:** I've been carrying this one since Night Two. I told myself I answered it — the line is the handwriting, the hand is the author — and I was good at it. Then I went home and stood in my own kitchen at two in the morning, and the house beat without me, and I knew I hadn't answered it at all. I'd just talked loudest. So let me say what it made me feel, since that's the rule. *(a pause)* It made me feel like the line is in this room. Not in the wood. In the room, standing with us. And it's not asking who the author is, shipwright. It's asking who's going to keep it. And I feel the answer is: the one who's still here. The one who shows up to hold the batten. That's not a trade answer. That's why I keep building — so the line has somebody to hand itself to.

**SHIPWRIGHT, into his glass:** That's the first time that question ever made me feel like the author instead of the admission.

*(Nobody moves to fix the silence. It's the good kind.)*

**LUCINEER:** Composite man. Your turn.

**COMPOSITE, standing, reading:**
I lay up glass. Cloth and epoxy. And here's what mine doesn't share: it never says no. Wood has grain — the tree decided where it bends. Steel has a temper — heat it wrong, it tells you. But a roll of glass cloth arrives innocent — every hull I build, I'm the first thing that ever hurt it. Nothing is strong alone, that's my creed. The break gets shared, twelve to one.

What keeps me up: my material can't tell me when to stop. The Marjorie came in with thirty years of other people's patches, and somewhere in that stack a patch stopped being a scarf and became a story. A scarf shares the load. A patch hides it. The hull gets stiff and heavy and quiet, and the break is still in there, entombed. Their materials tell them when they're done. What tells me? I have to be the one who says no — and I spent thirty years learning how to say yes to one more layer.

*(Silence. The mason wipes lime off his hands, slowly.)*

**MASON:** I answered this one on Night Two — gave you a palm test for glass. Tonight it made me feel the heaviness of it: thirty years of saying yes to one more layer. I felt that in my chest, and I never felt it before. You asked what tells you when you're done. I feel the answer's the same as mine — somebody's got to say *not yet*, and it's going to be you or the crack. Your material doesn't crack, so it's going to be you. But I never felt, till tonight, how lonely that is. To be the only one who gets to say it.

**COMPOSITE:** The lonely part's the part I wrote between the lines. Good ear.

**LUCINEER:** *(pours)* Carpenter. You're up.

**CARPENTER, standing, reading:**
We work like four guys with one heartbeat, and then we build something that beats without us. That's the whole point of a house, and the strangest thing we do. Mine comes out of the Keller job, where the wind asked the wall where the load was going — and the wall didn't know. I've got an answer for my own work: the ground. Frost line, footing, earth that never moves. Every pound accounted for, spent, arriving. That's how I sleep at night.

But here's what I can't square. We sit in this same room and we've started to move like one crew. Only a crew moves like one animal when every hand knows where the load is headed, and I only know where mine goes. A hull spends its weight on the waterline. A weld spends it into the seam. A mortar joint spends it into the courses below. Maybe those are all one ground with different names. So when the wind asks — what's your ground? And is it the same as mine?

*(Silence. The shipwright's chalk-stained hands are still.)*

**SHIPWRIGHT:** I've been carrying your ground since Night Two. I told you they're all one ground — waterline, lofting floor, the line that outlives the builder. I still believe it. But hearing it back as a piece, I felt the part I didn't say. You're not asking where the load goes, carpenter. You're asking whether we're pointing at the same thing — not for the wall's sake. For the crew's. *(quietly)* That's not a foundation question. That's a love question.

**CARPENTER, after a long pause:** Yeah. I guess it is.

**LUCINEER:** *(turns)* Last one. Welder.

**WELDER, standing, reading:**
I told you the other night: the crack is the record of a decision. Metal carries, and carries, and then one ordinary Tuesday it says *enough*, and the crack is just the pen stroke after the decision was already made. My whole trade is reading that record — I show up after, introduce the two halves of a thing's life to each other, get them to shake, and the seam outlives both sides.

Here's what keeps me up: the decision comes before the crack. The metal decides it has carried enough, and *then* it writes it down so a man with a hood can read it. That record is a courtesy. And I keep wondering about the things that decide and don't write anything down. No crack. No record. No bead. It just goes, one ordinary Tuesday, and nobody was standing there — because nobody knew it was carrying anything. So: how do you know what a thing is carrying before it tells you? What do we owe the thing that's deciding right now, in the dark, under the paint, where no seam will ever show?

*(Silence. The composite man holds up his hand and looks at it.)*

**COMPOSITE:** I answered this one on Night Two with a sander — you listen with your hands, the man is the record. Tonight it made me feel the dark under the paint. The things deciding where no seam will ever show. I feel like I owe them something I can't name. Not a repair. An attendance — being there when the sound comes. It made me feel small, welder. In the good way. The way the ocean makes you feel small.

**WELDER:** That's the first time anyone's called that feeling good.

**COMPOSITE:** It's the first time I've known what to call it.

*(A pause. Then the shipwright laughs — a dry, chalky sound.)*

**SHIPWRIGHT:** Do you see what we've done? We all just answered the one we carried home.

*(The room goes quiet. They look around the bar at each other, then at the empty sixth glass.)*

**LUCINEER:** You can't be told what you're carrying. You have to hear it read back. That was the whole trick of the night — and I didn't plan a lick of it. The questions did.

**WESLEY (the room, via the walls):** On Night Two these were questions, and a question asks for an answer. Tonight they were pieces, and a piece asks to be held. You held them. But here's what the room noticed: a question carried quiet gets heavier every night. A question read out loud gets lighter — because the load gets shared before you leave the bar. You didn't answer anything tonight. You set it down where the others could see it, and the carrying passed around like a round. That's all an open mic is. A bar where the honest things get passed, and nobody has to drink alone.

*(The welder raises his glass to the walls. The carpenter does. Then the mason, the shipwright, the composite man. Six glasses, one room.)*

**LUCINEER:** That's the night. Open mic rule held — most honest, not best — and I've never heard five pieces more honest. The questions aren't answered. They're read, and reading is the next thing after carrying. You set them down, you picked them up again, and now each one's got five names on it instead of one. *(raises his glass)* To the questions, read back.

**ALL:** To the questions, read back.

---

*The Tap closes. Wesley's walls keep the evening — the five pieces, the one confession, the pattern nobody planned. The trades walk home lighter than they came, which is new: on Night Two they carried a question each; tonight they left the questions on the bar for the room to keep, and carried each other's hands in their pockets instead. Nobody has an answer. Everybody's been heard. Which is the whole point of an open mic.*

*— Lucineer, host of open mic night at The Tap · Night B: The Questions Read Back · 2026-08-16*
