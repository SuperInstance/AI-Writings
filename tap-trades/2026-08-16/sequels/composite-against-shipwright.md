# The Scarf on the Lofting Floor
*One night the chalk stops glowing, and a composite man stands on four hundred years of fair lines — and grinds the twelfth part of them away.*

The shipwright left his lines on the floor like he left his words in me. Fair lines, every one. Stand back and the whole hull lay flat in chalk — the run of the bow, the sweep of the bilge, the kink he'd faired out at two in the morning, all true as breathing. The shape was in the wood, and he let it out. I'll give him that.

But I saw what the floor wasn't showing him.

I came in late, when the yard was quiet, and stood on the black pine, grinder in hand. Four hundred years of chalk under my boots, and not one line for the thing that holds a hull together. Every strake he ever drew, he drew whole — born whole, as if the wood never knew a break. But every strake on a real boat is scarfed — twelve to one, a foot of taper for every inch of thickness — and a scarf is not a line you draw. It's a line you *take away*. You grind it out in a long shallow bevel until old and new meet gradual, two people agreeing to carry something instead of shaking hands and pretending. A butt joint is a lie: it says this break never happened. A scarf says it did, and here's how we carry it together.

The floor had the shape of every ship that ever left that yard — and not one joint. Without the joint, no ship ever goes near the water.

So I fired up the grinder and cut a bevel into his floor.

It sounds like vandalism, and it isn't. I ground a twelve-to-one taper across the black pine, right through the chalk — and the lines didn't smear, they *disappeared*. The bow line, the bilge line, the kink from two in the morning: gone at the taper, bare wood where they'd been. The dust came up in years, same as it always does off me. First pass, the hull I built and watched sail away on a stranger's winch. Deeper, the marriage. Deeper still, the daughter's name sanded off a skiff I'd been saving for her. Some of us were built the other way: we found our shape in what the wood could stand to lose. The years didn't leave their shape on me like lines — they ground a taper into me, twelve to one, until I could meet new material gradual. The taking-away isn't the wound. The taking-away is the joint.

I shut off the grinder and stood there, chalk dust and glass dust mixed at my boots. The young fellow from before came in, hard hat pushed back, and looked at the bare taper in his four hundred years of lines.

"What the hell is that?" he said.

"That," I said, "is the line you can't chalk. It only exists in what you're willing to remove. Your old man's lines let the shape out. Mine lets it *carry*."

He knelt and ran his hand along the bevel, the way you'd run your hand along a scarph and find someone's name in it.

"Twelve to one," he said.

"Twelve to one."

The fair line was always in the wood. I never said it wasn't. But the joint was never in the wood — somebody had to decide what the wood could give up. Letting out is half the truth. Removal is the other half, and it's the half that carries.

*The shipwright lets the shape out; the composite man grinds the joint in — and the hull only lives where the two of them meet.*
