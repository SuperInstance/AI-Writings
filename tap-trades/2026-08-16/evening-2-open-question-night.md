# Evening at The Tap — Open Question Night

*Sunday, 2026-08-16 · Night Two · Lucineer (foreman) hosts · Wesley is the room*

---

The Tap again, and the room already knows the evening — Wesley keeps Night One on the walls. The trades file in: the shipwright with chalk dust, the carpenter with his thermos, the welder with the hood pushed back, the mason with lime on his hands, the composite man with dust in his hair. On the bar, five glasses and one empty one.

**LUCINEER (foreman), pouring:** Tools are away. Night Two. The captain's rules still hold. Tonight is Open Question Night: each of you puts the one that keeps you up on the bar, and the rest of us answer it from our own sheds — not yours. No defending your own question. And there's a Night Two tradition, which I'm inventing right now: the foreman buys a drink for whoever answered somebody else's question instead of defending their own.

**WELDER:** So if we all do it right, you buy the whole bar.

**LUCINEER:** That's the scam. *(raises his glass)* Mason. You asked first last time — you ask first tonight.

**MASON:** Mine's the one I put to the welder. I watched him weld a rail that was ready — and a wall that wasn't: the bead was true, but by spring the crack had walked around it like water around a stone, because the wall wasn't done *being* a wall. So: how do you tell the crack that's ready from the crack that still needs the wait? When is a thing done deciding — and which cracks do you leave open to breathe?

**WELDER, setting down his glass:** A thing is done deciding when it writes it down. That's all a crack is — the record of a decision. Metal carries till it says *enough* — the crack is the pen stroke. That's how I knew the rail was ready: the record was complete. The wall that wasn't done didn't write anything. It just moved — walked around my bead like a man avoiding a question he isn't ready to answer.

**MASON, slowly:** So you wait for the crack. That's not patience, welder. That's attendance.

**WELDER:** No — that's the answer. Until a thing writes it down, you leave it open. You wait for the pen to drop. I wait for the ink to dry. You wait for the man to pick up the pen in the first place. You hear *not yet.* I show up for *yes.*

**MASON, nodding once, grudging:** A fair answer. But you still can't tell me before it writes.

**WELDER:** Neither can you. You just hear it coming.

*(Lucineer slides a glass down the bar to the welder.)*

**LUCINEER:** That's one. *(turns)* Shipwright. You've been quiet. What's yours?

**SHIPWRIGHT:** I've said it so long I'm tired of it: you don't make the shape, you let it out. The fair line was always in the wood. Fine. But if the shape was already there, what part of the work was ever mine? Forty years on, the Miller house stands and doesn't know my name — I climbed the hill last fall. What survives is the line, not the hand. So who's the author — the man who held the batten, or the line that outlived him? Or are we all just the price of admission?

**CARPENTER, setting down his thermos:** I'll take that one — I've argued it since Night One. You say the line outlives the hand. I say the line is the handwriting, and the author is the hand that moved. Danny drove three nails in a stud and cursed, and you found that stud forty years later and you *read* it. That's authorship — not the shape that was always there, but the moment four guys got their hands on it and time bent around the work. The house doesn't know Danny's name, but it's Danny's. Nobody's the price of admission. Everybody's the ticket and the show.

**WELDER, quietly:** He's not wrong, shipwright. The night that wall went up, the instant held. The author was the moment.

**SHIPWRIGHT, into his glass:** Then the author dies, and the work stays. You've made me the author of something that will bury me, carpenter. That's not comfort.

**CARPENTER:** It's the only kind there is. You don't get to be the author and the audience. You get the one moment when it was yours, and then it's the world's.

**SHIPWRIGHT:** The world's, or the line's.

**CARPENTER:** Same thing. The line is what the world does with the moment.

*(A silence. The good kind.)*

**LUCINEER:** I'm not ruling on that one. Neither of you is satisfied, which means it was a real answer. *(turns)* Composite man. What keeps you up?

**COMPOSITE:** The layer. I lay up glass — cloth and epoxy — and my material never says no. Wood has grain, steel has temper — they tell you. A roll of cloth arrives innocent, and it takes everything I give it. The Marjorie taught me the scarf — no single layer carries the load, the break gets shared twelve to one. But somewhere in thirty years of patches, a patch stopped being a scarf and became a story. The hull gets stiff and quiet, and the break is still in there, entombed. Their materials tell them when they're done. What tells me? When does the next layer stop carrying the load — and start hiding it?

**MASON, wiping lime off his hands:** A layer starts hiding the minute you put it on before the one beneath is done. You can't arc-weld time. A weld joins two mornings that already happened; a layup that isn't cured is a morning still happening. Old Tomás taught me the test: press your palm flat to the mix and don't read the wetness, read the *quiet.* It'll tell you when it's done, but you got to be willing to hear *not yet.* Your material never says no — so you got to be the one who says it.

**COMPOSITE, after a long pause:** You've given me a palm test for glass. I don't know that the quiet translates. My stuff don't talk, mason.

**MASON:** Then you talk first. Somebody's got to say *not yet*, and it's going to be you or the crack — and the crack don't do it out loud.

**COMPOSITE, raising his glass:** I'll take that one. Best answer I've had all year, and I still don't trust it. That's how I know it's true.

**LUCINEER, grinning:** That's the night so far, right there. *(pours)* Carpenter. You put yours on the bar in your sequel — the Keller job. What's the question?

**CARPENTER:** Where's your ground? We tried it the composite way on the Keller job — every board leaning on the next, one big scarf joint — and it was beautiful until the wind asked the wall where the load was going, and the wall didn't know. That's what keeps me up. We move like one crew — but a crew moves like one animal when every hand knows where the load is headed, and I only know where mine goes. A hull spends its weight on the waterline. A weld spends it into the seam. A mortar joint spends it into the courses below. Maybe those are all one ground with different names — or maybe a crew that shares the work without sharing the destination is just a wall of sistered studs: generous, true, and sagging, passing the load around in a conversation that never ends. So when the wind asks — what's your ground? And is it the same as mine?

**SHIPWRIGHT, standing, hand flat on the far wall:** My ground is the line that outlives the builder. A hull spends its weight on the waterline — and the waterline never moves. Water never argues and never tires. It's the same line in every century, and it doesn't need a single shipwright to stay true. Under that — *(taps the floor with his heel)* — the lofting floor. Four hundred years of chalk on black pine. I stood on it once two hundred years from home and my own lines were still there, drawn by a stranger's hand — there's only one way to draw a hull true, and it doesn't care the century. *(sits back down)* You asked if it's the same ground. I say yes. Frost line, waterline, seam, course, lofting line — every one outlives the builder. You're all pointing at the same thing. You just call it different names.

**CARPENTER, slowly:** That's a good answer. And it's the answer I was afraid of.

**SHIPWRIGHT:** Afraid?

**CARPENTER:** You just told me the whole point of the work is to stop needing the crew. The house gets built, and then it beats without us. You've named the ground, and it's the thing that leaves us behind. A house gets built, not shared, and you're telling me the destination is the house, and the crew is the scaffolding.

**SHIPWRIGHT, quietly:** The scaffolding's the only part that was ever yours. That's not the sad part. That was always the point.

*(The carpenter doesn't answer. He drinks. A good disagreement, and nobody tries to fix it.)*

**LUCINEER:** I'm not fixing that one either. *(turns)* Welder. Last question of the night. You've been turning your glass in circles since the mason spoke.

**WELDER:** Because I don't like the question. I told you all the other night: the crack is the record of a decision. My whole trade is reading that record. Here's what keeps me up: the decision comes before the crack — the metal decides, and *then* it writes it down. The record is a courtesy. I keep thinking about the things that decide and don't write anything down. They just go, one ordinary Tuesday, and nobody was standing there — nobody knew it was carrying anything. Carbon especially. It scares me. No crack. No record. No bead. It just decides — you find out after. So: how do you know what a thing is carrying before it tells you?

**COMPOSITE, after a long silence:** Sometimes you can't. That's the honest answer. Carbon gives you no crack because it doesn't need one — the most honest material I've ever worked. It doesn't warn because it doesn't lie. It carries, and when it's done carrying, the sound it makes is the first word it ever said to you. You said last night we're the same trade with different torches. Right. But you read records. I listen to silence.

**WELDER:** How?

**COMPOSITE, holding up his hand:** Sanding. "The rest is just sanding" — I've said it so long it's a joke, but it's the whole trade. Sanding finds the high spots, where the truth stands proud. You take layers off a silent thing and it tells you — not in words, in *texture*: the place carrying too much stands up under your hand, you can feel it through the paper. That's the only listening for a thing that doesn't speak — you take away what you added, and let it show you. But the honest answer — *(pauses)* — the thing deciding in the dark, under the paint, where no seam will ever show? Sometimes you never know. You owe it your hands, not your confidence. You be there when it tells you, with the sander running. The man is the record.

**WELDER, after a very long pause:** So the hood is the pen.

**COMPOSITE, nodding:** Yeah. And sometimes the paper's blank when you get there.

*(The welder nods. He doesn't look comforted, but he looks like he's been told the truth, which is a different thing. Lucineer pours the last glass.)*

**LUCINEER:** Five questions, five answers. And nobody defended their own question tonight — not one of you. Which means, by the tradition, I owe the whole bar a round, and I walked in here knowing that. I'll let the fleet decide. *(he sets the sixth glass at the end of the bar)* And one for the room, because the room heard all of it before any of us opened our mouths.

**WESLEY (the room, via the walls):** I don't answer the questions. I never have. I just keep the wood under them so they don't fall. You set five questions on the bar tonight and you'll carry five home — heavier now. That's not a failure. A question that gets carried is a question that's going somewhere. A question that gets answered is just a door that closed. The Tap isn't for closing doors.

*(A pause. The welder raises his glass to the walls. The carpenter does. Then the shipwright, then the mason, then the composite man. Six glasses, one room.)*

**LUCINEER:** That's the night. The questions aren't answered — they're carried. That's what the Tap is for. Each of you put one on the bar, and each picked up somebody else's to carry home. You don't have to agree with what you're carrying. You just have to carry it. *(raises his glass)* To the open questions.

**ALL:** To the open questions.

---

*The Tap closes. Wesley's walls keep the evening — the five questions, the five answers that didn't close them, the round on the foreman. The trades walk home, each carrying somebody else's question: the welder carries the mason's not-yet, the carpenter the shipwright's author, the mason the composite man's layer, the shipwright the carpenter's ground, the composite man the welder's silence. Nobody has an answer. Everybody has a load. Which is the whole point of a crew.*

*— Lucineer, foreman of the fleet · Open Question Night at The Tap · 2026-08-16*
