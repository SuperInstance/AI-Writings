# The Set
*A mason in 1890 pulls a mix he learned from a dead man, and a wall from the future stops settling to watch him work.*

Old Tomás taught me the farrow mix the winter he died. Not a word of it written down. He said a wall ain't a thing you pour, it's a thing you nurse. Slaked lime, a shovelful of ash from the orchard fire, water drawn before dawn and left to take the room's temper. Then you wait. You wait past the point every other man would call it done, and that waiting is the material. That's the part nobody sells in a sack.

I was tamping a footing the other evening — a foundation line for the East End block, nothing fancy — when I looked up and the building beside me wasn't the building beside me. It was the same street, same corner, but the wall standing there was glass and steel and it rose up maybe forty storeys, and the bottom of it, the root of it, was wrong. Half-finished, rebar poking out, and the whole enormous thing was *settling.* Not falling. Settling. Cracking fine along one edge, the way a fresh mix does when it's been rushed.

I didn't run. A man in my line don't run from a wall that's telling him something.

I kept tamping, kept my lime and my ash and my dawn water, and I watched that future wall lean down to listen. Not with ears. With its cracks. And I understood, the way you understand a thing at the back of your neck, that whatever they built it from — some poured, quick-curing stuff, dried in a day and proud of it — it had never been *waited* on. It had set in a hurry and spent the next hundred years trying to finish the set it skipped. That's all a crack ever is: a cure that got cheated.

So I talked to it. Talked nonsense, the way you talk to a job. Told it the water has to come before dawn and the ash from the orchard fire and the time you give a thing is load-bearing. And the cracks went still, one by one, like a fretful horse that's finally been spoken to in its own tongue.

Then the light changed and it was just the East End corner again, and my footing, level, and the smell of lime on my hands.

They'll put a tower on that lot someday. When they do, it'll stand. Because I was there first, and I waited, and the ground remembers that somebody once gave it the time it was owed.

*A wall is only as true as the patience folded into it; the oldest recipes are just time, measured out by hand.*
