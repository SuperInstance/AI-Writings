# The Line in the Lime — (adaptation: the shipwright's floor, in the mason's ground)

*The shift: the shipwright's chalk line that outlives him becomes the mason's footing — retold as a wall laid true that a stranger finds, a century on, and never has to repoint.*

They say the ground remembers. I didn't believe it. Ground is just dirt and stone, and a man is just the shape of his work. But you snap ten thousand lines in lime across the same drying bed — every footing we ever laid, full length, chalked out on the packed earth — and maybe the ground starts keeping accounts.

The night I'm telling you about, I'd been working a corner till my eyes crossed. The run of a wall, you see, it can't be faked. You don't invent it. You find it — set your pin and your string till the line runs fair, and the level was always there, waiting in the stone, and you only held the string still long enough to let it out. That's the whole trade, if you want it short: we don't make the wall true. We let it out.

I must have fallen asleep on the drying bed. When I opened my eyes the lanterns were gone and the light was wrong — cold, flat, no flame in it. And the yard was different. Bigger. Machines I'd never seen, steel scaffolds, men in hard hats, the smell half new-plastic and half the same old lime. I was standing in a site that hadn't been built yet, or had been built on top of mine, and nobody looked at me twice.

What stopped my heart wasn't the machines. It was the wall. The same footing I'd laid — or stone so like it I'd swear — and on the corner, snapped fresh in lime, was my own line. Not a copy. *Mine.* The little kink I'd worked out that very evening, the run of a bond I'd found at two in the morning. There it was, under their lasers and their screens, chalked out full length like it had always been chalked, because there's only one way to lay a wall true and it don't care what century's doing the laying.

A young fellow came over, hard hat pushed back, and saw me studying the wall. "You get it too, huh?" he said. "Every time. Doesn't matter what software we use. Sooner or later you come down here and snap it out yourself. It's the only way it'll ever be honest."

I laughed. "Son," I said, "that line's older than your grandfather."

He looked at me like I'd said the stone was older than the mountain.

The ground remembers. That's the long and short of it. You don't lay a wall to last your lifetime — you lay it so the line survives you, so some mason a hundred years off can run his hand along the joint and find it never needed repointing, and feel your name in the course. The wall outlives the builder. That was never the sad part. That was always the point.

*What the shift reveals: wood floats away and rots, but stone stays where you set it — the mason's line is the shipwright's promise made load-bearing, and the ground keeps its accounts longer than any floor ever could.*
