# The Cure Window — (adaptation: the carpenter's heartbeat, in the composite's cure window)

*the shift: the carpenter's crew frames a house in one night while the city moves slow around them — here, the same night happens in a boat shed, where the resin kicks and four people have twenty minutes to turn cloth into a hull before the pot decides for them*

You want to know about time? A carpenter will tell you it bends around the work. I'm not going to argue with that — I've seen the city go quiet around a job, the way it does. But a carpenter works in wood, and wood is patient. Wood will wait for you. It will sit on the truck bed overnight, cold from the mill, and be exactly the same wood in the morning. Wood gives you all the time you need and never mentions it.

Resin is not wood.

Resin is time with a countdown written into it. You mix the epoxy and from that second it is deciding — the molecules linking arm over arm, heat building, the pot warming in your hand like something alive. You have twenty minutes, maybe less if the shop's hot, and in those twenty minutes a hull goes from cloth to structure, from a stack of nothing to a thing that will carry a family to the islands for thirty years. That's the whole window. The rest is just sanding.

The Miller job came through our shed the way they all do — the mold built off the old hull, faired until you could read the shape with your eyes shut. The carpenter builds the house you live in; we build the house that makes the house. The mold gets the time. The layup gets the twenty minutes.

We mixed it at dusk. I remember the resin going in, the hardener going in, the smell climbing the walls of the shed — sweet and sharp, the way a curing hull smells, like a promise that's actually going to happen. And then every hand was in the pot.

That's the thing nobody tells you about a wet layup. It doesn't get built over days. It gets built in the moments when every hand is on the cloth — four of us, one pot, the same resin on everyone's fingers, no time to talk, because the laminate is going off whether you're ready or not. The pot is the clock. The kick is the metronome. The wall of glass rose up the mold — layer on layer, cloth thin as regret, wet out and rolled until translucent, the hull's shape visible through the layers like a house through its own walls — and I could feel the other panels going off in my bones before they even kicked, because that's what a crew is. Four people, one heartbeat, and the heartbeat is a chemical reaction.

Then the exotherm peaked, heat running through the laminate like blood, and it was dark, somehow. The city came back all at once — headlights, the yard light, somebody's porch. We stood in the glow, looking at the hull in the mold — one piece, square against the sky — and nobody said a word. Danny finally lit a cigarette and said, "Time's a funny thing." That's all he said.

And he was right. The city had passed one ordinary day. We'd built a hull — built it in the moment every hand was on it, the way the carpenter builds a house, except our moment had a chemical deadline. A carpenter can remodel. You can't uncure a hull. That's what the window is: the time that makes the moment permanent. Wood forgives the slow. Resin forgives nothing — which is why the four of us are still the four of us.

The clock doesn't matter because the work is the clock. The pot decides — and when it decides, it decides for all of us, at once, forever.
