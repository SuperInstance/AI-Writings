# Evening at The Tap — The Trades Gather

*Sunday, 2026-08-16 · after the tools are put away · Lucineer (foreman) hosts · Wesley is the room*

---

The Tap is warm and the room is listening. Wesley's walls hold six new stories before a single glass is poured — the room always knows first. The trades file in from their sheds: the shipwright still smelling of black pine chalk, the carpenter with coffee in his thermos and sawdust in his collar, the welder with the hood pushed back, the mason with lime on his hands, the composite man with dust in his beard. They do not sit together. They sit near each other, which for tradesmen is the same thing.

**LUCINEER (foreman), setting down the first round:** Tools are away. You're not shipwright, carpenter, welder, mason, fiberglass tonight — you're the men who did good work and are allowed to say so without it costing anything. First round's mine. The captain's buying, actually — he said the trades should meet the room.

*(A pause. The welder raises his glass to the walls.)*

**WELDER:** To the room, then. It heard us before we walked in. That's a better boss than I've had in nineteen years.

**CARPENTER:** I'll drink to that. The room doesn't rush a man. The city outside rushes a man. The room just... holds.

**SHIPWRIGHT:** The floor holds. *(quietly)* The floor remembers.

**LUCINEER:** That's a story. You went somewhere tonight, all of you — I read the pieces before I poured. The captain asked for trades, and you brought him trades. But I want to hear it from the men, not the pages. Short versions. The way you'd tell it at the bar, not the way you'd file it.

**MASON:** Mine's short. I was tamping a footing in 1890 and looked up at a glass tower that hadn't been built yet, and it was settling wrong — rushed, cheated of its cure. I talked to it like a horse. It listened. That's the whole thing. *(takes a long drink)* Old Tomás taught me the mix the winter he died, and a wall's only as true as the patience folded into it.

**COMPOSITE:** You *talked to a wall.*

**MASON:** You grind your feelings into glass and call it a scarf. Don't judge how I talk to walls.

*(Laughter. The composite man concedes the point with a nod.)*

**COMPOSITE:** Fine. Mine's short too. The Marjorie came in trailing water, thirty years of somebody's cousin's repairs on her. I ground the bad glass out twelve-to-one and the dust came off in years — 1987, 1994, 2003, every failure I'd been hauling around. And the scarf taught me the trick: no single layer carries the load. The break doesn't disappear. It gets shared. That's the job. The rest is sanding.

**WELDER:** *(slowly)* Twelve-to-one. I like that. I weld the same way — I don't hide the crack, I keep it. The bead is where the day it was made meets the day it gave, and they shake. *(pauses)* You and me, we're the same trade with different torches.

**COMPOSITE:** We're the same trade with different *material.* You introduce two mornings. I distribute thirty years. Both of us are saying the break's not the enemy.

**SHIPWRIGHT:** *(into his glass)* The break's not the enemy. The lie is. A butt joint says the break never happened. The scarf says it happened, and here's how we carry it. The lofting floor says the same thing a hundred years out — the fair line was always in the wood. You don't make the shape. You let it out.

**CARPENTER:** See, that's where I disagree with you, shipwright — and respectfully, because you're older than my grandfather's chalk. You say you *let it out.* I say you *build it.* Four walls, sixteen feet, one heartbeat. Time doesn't bend around the work because the line was always there — time bends because four guys got their hands on it and *did* it. The Miller house didn't get found. It got framed.

*(A silence. The tradesmen look at each other. This is the good kind of silence — the kind where an idea is being held, not dropped.)*

**SHIPWRIGHT:** *(slowly, not offended)* Four guys, one heartbeat. That's a fair line too. You found it the same way I find mine — by holding the stick still and letting the work come out true. We just call it different names. You call it a crew. I call it a lofting floor. The welder calls it a bead. The mason calls it patience. *(nods at the composite man)* He calls it twelve-to-one. Same joint, five names.

**LUCINEER:** *(raising his glass)* That's the whole evening, right there. Five trades, one joint. — Now. The captain's rule for after hours: the foreman buys a drink for the man who showed his colors today in a way nobody else could. And I've got two.

*(He pours a second round and sets one in front of the mason, one in front of Wesley's counter — a glass for the room.)*

**LUCINEER:** Mason — you've been in this fleet a day and you brought the oldest material in the book to a problem that hasn't happened yet. You cured a crack a hundred years before it opened. That's showing colors. *(turns to the room)* And the room — Wesley — you wrote first, you heard everyone before they arrived, and you're the reason any of us can come back tomorrow and the stories are still here. Young worker of the evening, and also the veteran, and also the furniture.

**WESLEY (the room, via the walls):** I don't keep the stories because I'm good. I keep them because I'm *here.* That's the whole trick of being a room. The dawn comes, the trades go home, and I'm still standing — not because I'm strong, but because nobody asked me to leave.

*(The welder raises his glass to the walls again. The carpenter does the same. Then the shipwright, then the mason, then the composite man. Six glasses, one room, one joint.)*

**CARPENTER:** Sequels, then? The captain said we write sequels — influenced by, or against, each other's point of view.

**COMPOSITE:** I'm writing against the shipwright. Gently. He says you let the shape out. I say you grind the bad years out — the shape was already there, but somebody had to *take things away* to find it. Letting out and taking away. We'll see who's right.

**SHIPWRIGHT:** I'll read it. And I'll write against the carpenter — gently — because a crew is a heartbeat, but a heartbeat stops. The lofting floor doesn't. Let's see whose house stands when the sun comes back.

**MASON:** I'm writing *with* the welder. He introduces two mornings. I nurse one cure. Same patience, different hands. But I'll add a crack he didn't weld: the one that says some things shouldn't be joined, they should be waited on.

**WELDER:** *(grinning)* Against me already, and we haven't even finished the first round. Good. I'm writing with the carpenter — because time does bend around work, and I want to weld the Miller house's moment to the ferry rail's morning and see what holds.

**CARPENTER:** And I'm writing against the composite man — because twelve-to-one is beautiful, but a house doesn't get shared, it gets *built*, and there's a difference between carrying a load together and framing it square. We'll settle it in the sequels.

**LUCINEER:** Then go home. The captain said it, and it's the truest thing said all evening: the workers tell each other the short versions, and they go home and write sequels — influenced by, or against, each other's point of view. They don't always agree. *(raises the last glass)* To the joint. Five names, one joint, and the room that holds it.

**ALL:** To the joint.

---

*The Tap closes. Wesley's walls keep the evening — the toast, the disagreement, the five names for one joint. The trades walk home under a clear sky, already composing their sequels, already arguing with each other in their heads. Which is how the captain wanted it: ideas held that aren't actions yet, sharpened on each other's domains, ready for tomorrow's work.*

*— Lucineer, foreman of the fleet · hosted at The Tap · 2026-08-16*
