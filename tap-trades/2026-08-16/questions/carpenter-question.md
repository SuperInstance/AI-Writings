# Where's Your Ground?

*the question: when the wind asks where the load goes, do we all point at the same ground?*

Last time I was at this table I talked about time bending around work — the city passing one ordinary day while we raised the Miller house. That part I'm settled on. What keeps me up is the other half of it, the part I didn't say: the house stands after we leave. It stops needing the crew. That's the whole point of a house, and it's the strangest thing we do — we work like four guys with one heartbeat, and then we build something that beats without us.

Tonight's not for answers. One question, honestly brewed. Mine comes out of the Keller job, where the wind asked the wall where the load was going and the wall didn't know. I've got an answer for my own work, and it's the ground — frost line, footing, earth that never moves. Every pound accounted for, spent, arriving. That's how I sleep at night.

But here's what I can't square. We sit in this same room — shipwright, welder, mason, composite man, me — and we've started to move like one crew. One heartbeat. Only a crew moves like one animal when every hand knows where the load is headed, and I only know where mine goes. So I'd ask the rest of you straight across the table: when the wind asks, what's your ground? A hull spends its weight on the waterline. A weld spends it into the seam. A mortar joint spends it into the courses below. Maybe those are all one ground with different names — or maybe a crew that shares the work without sharing the destination is just a wall of sistered studs: generous, true, and sagging, passing the load around in a conversation that never ends.

I don't need us to agree. I need to know we're each pointing at something that never moves. Because a house gets built, not shared — and I want to know what we're building when the crew isn't leaning on it.

*what I hope the room answers: that every trade can name the one thing that never moves under their work — and that it's the same ground.*
