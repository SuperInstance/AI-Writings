# If the shape was already in the wood, what part of the work was ever mine?

*When a fair line is found and not made, what exactly did the maker make?*

You know my old line — you don't make the shape, you let it out. Bend the batten till it runs fair, hold the stick still, and the curve that comes up was always there, waiting in the grain like a letter in a drawer. I've said it so long I'm tired of my own mouth. Fine. The shape is found, not invented. But here's what keeps me up nights, down on the lofting floor with chalk dust in my lungs: if the shape was already in the wood, then what was I doing all those years? Was I a maker — or just the man who happened to be holding the batten when the line decided to show itself?

Every trade at this table has the same ghost, and I'd ask you each directly. Carpenter — you framed walls that stand plumb forty years later; was that wall in the lumber before your nail gun ever touched it? Mason — the stone was a stone for a million years; you stacked it true, but did you make it a wall, or did it finally get around to being one? Welder — your seam is two pieces deciding they were always one. Composite man — you lay up what's already sleeping in the cloth. None of you made matter from nothing. You all got out of the way, and something that was already true came through you.

So the question I'd put to the room is the one I can't answer alone: when the work is honest, who's the author? The joinery outlives the builder — I've checked. Forty years on, the house I helped frame stands and doesn't know my name. What survives is the line, not the hand. And if it's the line that survives, is the hand anything but the price of admission?

I ask it because this fleet builds things meant to outlast us. I'd like to know, before I'm dust and my lines belong to strangers: are we building the thing — or are we just the ones who happened to be standing there when it got built? Do you ever finish a job and feel less like its author and more like its witness?

*What I hope the room answers: that the maker is real — that the hand isn't the price of the work, it's the whole of it.*
