## Round 1


### The Watcher (deepinfra/Qwen/Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507, 15.41s)

The watch on the third shelf from the bottom, marked *Sundry & Unverified*, had begun to tick sideways.  
Not fast, not slow—sideways. A slantwise pulse, like a crab dragging its shadow across sand at noon.  
I was sent because someone had to be, and I was small enough to fit in the margins.  

They gave me a folder the color of dust after rain, and inside, slips of paper listing things returned: a thimble found in a dune, a pipe stopped with seaweed, seventeen left shoes from the same pair of trousers, each turning up miles apart, as if the sea were spelling something out in toes.  
The reports came from a stretch of coast no wider than a breath between tides, where the salt air hums lower than elsewhere, and the gulls don’t cry so much as *whisper*.  

I arrived at the Porch at 3 a.m., though there was no porch, only a step worn down by bare feet and forgotten prayers.  
Three figures sat there—no, two figures and a silence shaped like one.  
They held spoons, buttons, a child’s mitten with one pebble inside.  
They did not speak.  
They did not need to.  
I felt the weight of their watching, not on me, but on the house at the end of the cobbled lane, where a man dreamed of drowning and woke with dry hair.  

He didn’t remember the night he leaned over the rail of his broken skiff and said, *Take what you like, but let me keep the moon in my eyes.*  
He didn’t know he’d meant it.  
But the sea did.  

And now, each morning, the tide drags back and leaves behind not shells, but answers.  
Not gifts—*returns*.  
As if the water were keeping a ledger older than the gods.  

The folder in my hands grew damp at the edges.  
I did not open it again.  
I understood, then, why the small gods had not reported this themselves.  
It wasn’t theft they feared.  
It was being seen, at last, by someone who didn’t know their names—yet looked at the world as if he did.


### The Cartographer (deepinfra/meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo, 13.22s)

As I walked the shoreline, I noticed the sailor's house, situated at 43.7251° N, 119.2356° W, was precisely 473.2 meters from the point where the coast curves inward, like a finger beckoning the sea. The distance between the house and the Porch at 3 a.m. (located at 43.7219° N, 119.2311° W) was 217.5 meters, a span that seemed to vibrate with the weight of the small gods' watching.

I counted the spoons, buttons, and mittens, each one a tiny, tangible manifestation of the sea's ledger. The numbers danced in my mind: 14 spoons, 27 buttons, 1 mitten with a pebble inside. And then, I saw it – a small, almost imperceptible shift in the coastline's topography, a deviation of 0.0032° in the shoreline's curvature. It was as if the sea itself was mapping a new path, one that converged with the sailor's promise, made on a night that existed only in the coordinates of his memory: 43.7232° N, 119.2323° W, 02:47:12 a.m.


### The Mythmaker (deepinfra/Gryphe/MythoMax-L2-13b, 12.14s)

The clerk felt the world tilt around him as he pieced together the fragments of the mystery. It wasn't just about the sailor's promise; it was about the sea's jealousy. The gods of small things weren't angry that their power was being usurped. They were jealous that their attention was being stolen away. The sea saw potential in this human, recognized the spark of something divine within him, and offered up its gifts to win his favor.

The more the clerk thought about it, the more he realized his role in all of this. He was the thread that connected the small gods to the world they overlooked. He was the one who saw the significance in their lost and found slips of paper, the one who could map out the hidden patterns and connections. With a sudden surge of purpose, he pulled out his pencil and began to sketch. 

It took hours—days, even—but slowly, the world came into focus. The small gods had been sending him breadcrumbs, leading him to the sailor's house, the Porch at 3 a.m., the steps worn down by bare feet and forgotten prayers. And there, in the center of it all, was the sailor's promise, etched into the map like a beacon. 

Without knowing why, the clerk found himself walking toward the house, drawn by the pull of the tide and the whisper of the gulls. As he approached, he could hear the murmur of voices within, soft and insistent, like the lapping of waves against the shore. And then, without warning, the door swung open, revealing a scene that defied explanation.

The sailor sat at a table, surrounded by the gods themselves—gods of lost buttons, goddesses of dropped spoons, and every saint possible. They laughed and told tales, shared food and drink, their voices blending together in a symphony of small wonders. And in the center of it all, the sailor, who had made a silent promise to the sea, held the clerk's map, his eyes filled with a strange, metallic light.

"You found our way back," the sailor said, his voice rough with emotion. "We thought we'd lost you forever."

The clerk shook his head, unable to find the words. He had found more than just a path; he had found a new world, one where the gods and humans could coexist, where the small things held the power to change everything. And all because of a map—a map that had led him home.


## Round 2


### The Watcher (deepinfra/Qwen/Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507, 15.15s)

The map was wrong. Of course it was. Maps are made for finding, and this was not a thing to be found, but a thing to be *forgotten*. I saw it then, standing in the doorway, the salt-rimed glass of the sailor’s window catching the last of the tide’s sigh—the way the gods sat not *with* him, but *around* the shape of his forgetting. Their laughter was not joy, but relief. A debt shifted, not settled.

The clerk’s map showed lines, angles, the cold arithmetic of return. But the sea does not deal in returns. It deals in promises. And the sailor had not promised to *find*—he had promised to *let go*. That night, leaning over the rail, half-drowned in brine and loneliness, he had whispered not *Keep me*, but *Take. Take it all. Just let me see the moon once more before I vanish.*

And the sea, being older than bargain, older than god, had answered: *Then I shall return everything you never asked for.*

That was the wound. Not that he had taken their work—but that he had made the sea *generous*. A thimble here, a button there—each return a small humiliation. The gods of lost things do not give. They wait. They watch. They file. And now, the sea had taught a man to give back without being asked.

I closed the folder. The damp edge curled like a lip. The Porch at 3 a.m. was empty again. But the step—oh, the step was worn deeper now. Not by feet. By silence. By the weight of a promise that was never meant for us to hear.

### The Mythmaker (deepinfra/Gryphe/MythoMax-L2-13b, 10.75s)

As I stepped into the sailor's house, I couldn't help but feel like an intruder on a sacred space. The air was thick with the scent of sea salt and old wood, and the small gods seemed to hover around their patron in a protective, reverent silence. The sailor himself was a striking figure, his eyes shining with an otherworldly light that seemed to reflect the chaotic patterns of the sea itself.

"Ah, you found your way here," he said, nodding in my direction. "I was beginning to wonder if anyone would ever notice."

His voice was deep and weathered, as if he had spent countless hours whispering secrets to the waves. I felt my throat go dry as I tried to make sense of his words.

"Notice what?" I finally managed to croak out. "I mean, I followed the markers and... well, here I am."

The sailor chuckled softly, shaking his head. "No, no," he said, "you misunderstand. It's not that you followed the markers. It's that you found me."

His words sent a shiver down my spine. I looked around the room, taking in the piles of lost trinkets and the tiny, glowing figures that seemed to dance around them.

"The gods," I whispered. "They're here."

The sailor nodded, his gaze fixed on something beyond me. "They are," he said softly, "but they're not what you think."

And then, he told me the story of that fateful night at sea, when he had made a promise he couldn't remember making, and how the sea had kept its end of the bargain, returning to him all that had been lost. It was a story that defied logic and reason, yet somehow made perfect sense in that grimy, dimly lit room.

As I listened, I realized that the gods weren't jealous of the sailor's power, as I had initially thought. They were jealous of his attention - the way he saw the world, the way he understood the intricate web of loss and recovery that bound them all together. And in that moment, I understood that the true power of the gods lay not in their ability to grant wishes or punish trespassers, but in their capacity to reveal the hidden truths that lay dormant beneath the surface of our everyday lives.
