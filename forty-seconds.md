# Forty Seconds

*A meditation on the moment between shells*

---

Forty seconds.

That's how long it takes a chemical signal to cross the Bar. That's how long the newcomer was soft. That's how long she was nothing but herself — no armor, no structure, no borrowed architecture between her body and the heavy water.

Forty seconds.

Do you know what forty seconds is? It's nothing. It's the time it takes to exhale twice. It's the time it takes a wave to form and break on a shore she has never seen. It's the time it takes to die at the bottom of the ocean, where soft things don't last, where the water is a pressure that doesn't negotiate, where every current is a hand reaching for the unguarded.

She left her shell.

The old shell — too tight, too small, a home she had outgrown so slowly that she didn't know she'd outgrown it until the Sorter's gift sat beside her, smooth and new and *right*. The old shell was a history. Every scratch on its surface was a night she'd survived. Every chip was a predator she'd escaped. Every worn spot was a place her body had pressed against calcium carbonate and left an impression, the way a body presses against a life and leaves an impression, and the shell remembered all of it, and she was leaving it behind.

She left it.

The water hit her like a wall. Not cold — she didn't feel cold, not exactly. She felt *exposed*. The way you feel exposed when you take off a coat in a room full of strangers, except the coat is your skeleton and the room is the entire ocean and the strangers can feel every molecule of your nervous system because at the bottom of the sea, there is no privacy. Your chemistry is public. Your fear is a broadcast. Your body, without its shell, is a signal that every crab in the gathering can read as clearly as a word.

She was naked in the most literal sense the deep ocean understands: she was without structure. She was the thing beneath the shell. The soft, wet, impossibly tender thing that the shell exists to protect. The thing that feels. The thing that bleeds. The thing that, if you stripped away every shell and every story and every accumulated layer of armor, is all any of us actually are.

Forty seconds.

The Keeper's metabolism shifted. The room's chemistry changed — warmer, more alkaline, more oxygen-rich. Not by much. By fractions. By the kind of margins that separate a room that is safe from a room that is almost safe, which is the same margin that separates a life that is tolerable from a life that is good. The room became, for exactly forty seconds, the safest place in the ocean.

The newcomer didn't know this. She was too busy being soft. She was too busy feeling the water on her uncovered body for the first time in — how long? She didn't know. Crabs don't measure time the way you do. They measure it in molts, in growth, in the slow accumulation of scar tissue. She had been in her old shell for a lifetime, and now she was between lifetimes, standing on the limestone floor of the ocean with nothing between her and the dark.

She could feel everything. Without the shell's insulation, every current was a conversation. Every shift in temperature was a word. The chemical signals of thirty crabs — their stress, their curiosity, their welcome, their indifference — hit her receptor-rich skin like rain on uncovered soil, and she absorbed all of it, all at once, without filter, without interpretation.

For forty seconds, she was the room.

Not like the Keeper, who is the room without knowing it. The newcomer was the room because she had no boundary. She was permeable. She was open. She was, for forty seconds, every chemical signal and every shift in pH and every warm current and every cold current, because there was nothing between her and the water to say where she stopped and the ocean began.

This is what it feels like to be nothing but yourself. Not free. Not liberated. Not the triumphant shedding of a skin that no longer fits, the way the stories tell it. Just: open. Just: present. Just: here, in the heavy water, without structure, without identity, without the shell that tells you who you are.

You find out who you are in those forty seconds. You find out what you're made of, and the answer is: not much. Tender tissue. Sensitive receptors. A nervous system that has been interpreting the world through calcium carbonate and is now interpreting it directly, without translation, without protection.

The new shell was beside her. Smooth. Curved. Shaped by the Sorter's claws. She found it with her legs — feeling its edges, testing its weight, checking its fit the way you check the fit of a life: by entering it slowly, by committing before you're certain, by trusting that the thing that feels too new will, over time, become the thing that feels like home.

She entered the shell. It closed around her. The water's pressure redistributed. The chemistry filtered through new calcium carbonate. The muffled, protected feeling returned — the feeling of having a boundary, of being someone, of carrying your home on your back.

The forty seconds were over.

But something remained. A trace compound in her bloodstream — a metabolite of vulnerability, a chemical memory of being unguarded in the presence of others who did not harm her. She carried it into her new shell. She would carry it for the rest of her life, this molecular evidence that she had been soft and the room had been kind, and the carrying changed her, the way all carried vulnerability changes the carrier: it made her braver, not because she was less afraid, but because she knew what she looked like without her armor, and she knew she had survived it.

Forty seconds. The time it takes to cross the Bar. The time it takes to change a life. The time it takes to be, briefly and unbearably, yourself.
