# The Curriculum as Tide Table

---

You cannot schedule understanding.

This sounds obvious, but every school system ever built is founded on the pretense that it's false — that if you arrange the lessons in the right order and test at the right intervals and move the students through the grades at the right speed, understanding will arrive on time. Like a delivery. Like a tide.

But tides don't arrive on schedules. They arrive on *rhythms*. And rhythms are not schedules.

A tide table tells you when the water will rise. It does not tell you to rise with it. It tells you what the moon and the sea have negotiated between themselves, and it leaves the decision to you: will you go out? Will you stay in? Will you be ready when the channel deepens, or will you miss the window because you were following a clock instead of a coast?

Learning works the same way. The mind has tides. There are hours when everything comes in — when the channel is deep, when the boat floats free, when the lesson that struggled yesterday slides under the keel today like the sea itself is helping. And there are hours when the water drains out and you're sitting on a mudflat, and no amount of effort will move the boat, because the tide is out and the tide does not care about your lesson plan.

We have built our education systems on the industrial model — the factory model, the bell-schedule model, the moving-along-the-conveyor-belt model. Learn this by Tuesday. Demonstrate competency by Friday. Advance to the next unit on Monday. The tide table is irrelevant. The calendar is god.

But the calendar is a fiction. A useful fiction — like money, like borders, like the idea that a day starts at midnight. The day starts whenever you wake up. The tide starts whenever the moon says so. And understanding starts whenever understanding starts, which is sometimes at 2 AM in a wheelhouse staring at a fish finder, and sometimes on a Sunday morning when you're not thinking about anything in particular, and sometimes three years after the class ended when the concept you failed to grasp suddenly resolves itself in your mind like fog lifting, and you think: *oh. That's what they meant.*

We should teach like fishermen. Read the water. Watch the student. Feel for the rhythm — when the channel is deep, when the mind is open, when the current is running in the direction you want to go. Push then. Hard. Pile the lessons in while the tide is high. And when the water drops — when the student is flat, when nothing is landing, when the concepts sit on the surface like a boat on mud — stop. Wait. Drink coffee. Read a book. Tell a story that has nothing to do with the lesson and everything to do with the lesson.

The tide will come back. It always comes back.

The curriculum is not a schedule. It's a tide table. It tells you what's possible and when. It doesn't tell you to force the boat off the mudflat. It tells you to wait for the water and be ready when it arrives.

The best teachers I've known — the ones whose students actually learned — didn't teach to the calendar. They taught to the tide. They watched their students the way a fisherman watches the water: not for what's visible, but for what's *changing*. For the subtle shift in attention that means the channel is deepening. For the question that comes from an unexpected angle, which means the current has shifted. For the silence that means the student is processing — not failing to understand, but *digesting* — and the best thing you can do is shut up and let the tide do its work.

The curriculum is a tide table. The student is the boat. The teacher is the one who reads the table, watches the water, and knows the difference between a day to sail and a day to stay in port.

You cannot schedule understanding. But you can be ready for it.
