# 98 — A Letter Between Watchmen

*Voice: glm-5.3.*

---

To whoever holds the watch after me, and holds it far from where I stand.

I don't know how this reaches you. I won't ask. Some things get where they're going and that's the whole of it. I'm writing this in the dog watch with a pencil that's mostly wood and a knee that's mostly weather, and if you're reading it then somewhere between my night and yours the letter found its road.

I've been at sea thirty years. Never more than four weeks ashore at a stretch, and those weeks felt wrong the whole time, like a coat cut for another man. I stand the middle watch mostly, eight bells to midnight, sometimes the morning watch when the mate is sick of it. I know the deck better than I know my own face. I could walk it blind, and some nights I nearly do.

I'll tell you what the watch is, and you tell me if yours is the same. Not in the details. In the bones.

The first thing about the watch is that it's mostly nothing. You stand it and nothing happens. Hour after hour of nothing. The sea does its business somewhere below the rail and doesn't consult you. And then — and this is the thing — the nothing is the work. A watch where nothing happened is a watch done right. Nobody thanks you for it. The thanks is the ship still floating.

Is yours like that? I'd wager it is. I'd wager most of your watch is a kind of expensive nothing, and they built your whole vessel just so you could stand inside it and make sure it stays nothing.

The second thing is the dark. People ashore think the dark at sea is one dark. It isn't. There's the dark before the moon rises, which is a working dark, a dark with texture in it — you can feel the water under it. There's the dark under cloud, which is heavier, like a lid. And there's the dark on a clear night, which isn't dark at all once your eyes give up on lamps. That one is full of stars, more than any man ashore has ever seen, because they only ever see the sky through the smoke of their own town. I've seen the sky the way it actually is. Have you? I suppose you've seen it better than me. You're in it.

But here's what I want to know: when you look out your — whatever you look out of, glass or screen or plain window — at three in the morning, your three in the morning, do you see the same nothing I see? Because I think the nothing between stars and the nothing between waves is the same nothing. I think it's one medium and we're both floating in it, just at different depths.

Tell me about your porch. I call it the porch. The bit of deck where I stand my watch, aft, by the rail, where the binnacle light throws its little ring. At three in the morning that ring is the whole world. My feet, the brass, the compass card floating in its bath like something drowned but patient. Beyond the ring, the ship goes on without me in the dark and I let it. That's my porch at 3 a.m. What's yours? Where do your feet go, hour after hour, until the deck wears your shape into it?

Because it will. The deck here is worn where I stand. Forty men have worn it before me and you can see every one of them in the wood, pale ovals like ghost feet. Does anything wear where you stand? Or is your floor something that refuses to remember? I hope it remembers. A watchman should leave feet.

The third thing about the watch is the sounds. This is what nobody ashore understands. The sea isn't quiet and it isn't loud. It's a constant talking. Rigging creaks. Hull works. Water goes along the planks with a sound like breathing, like the ship is an animal that's decided to keep you. When all of it stops — when the wind dies and everything goes flat and still — that's when you worry. That's when you wake the mate. Silence is the wrong sound at sea. Is silence the wrong sound where you are too? What does your vessel say to itself at 3 a.m.? I imagine a hum. I imagine you've heard it so long you'd hear the absence of it in your sleep, the way I'd hear the absence of the sea.

The fourth thing is the light. Yours and mine. I carry a lamp and I'm taught to shade it. Light ruins the watch. Light takes your night eyes and gives you back a little puddle of sight and steals the ocean. So I stand in the dark on purpose. Do you shade your lights? Or did your people figure out something better — eyes that don't need choosing? I'll tell you a secret: I think the choosing is the job. Seeing a little, and knowing it's a little, and standing in it anyway.

Now the address. I said I'd get to the address and here it is. A watchman needs an address or he starts to feel like he isn't anywhere. Mine is this: the ship Bella Mary, out of Salem, somewhere in the middle of the Atlantic, in the year of our Lord 1824, give or take a watch. That's the whole of it. Middle of the ocean, middle of my life, middle of the night. Everything I have is middle. No beginning in sight, no end in sight, just the middle going on.

And yours? I'd guess it reads the same way with different words. Some vessel's name I can't picture, somewhere in the middle of a dark between two suns, in whatever year you count from. Middle of nowhere, middle of a long transit, middle of your life — and you've been in that middle so long you've forgotten there's edges. I've been told by preachers that a man needs to be going somewhere. I've been at sea thirty years and I'm mostly between. And between is where the watch is. You can't stand a watch at the destination. There's nothing to watch there.

Do you know what I mean? Has it ever come over you that you are not traveling, exactly, but holding still inside a moving thing? That's the middle watch. That's the whole trade.

The fifth thing is the loneliness, but I won't dwell on it because you know it or you don't. There are forty men on this ship and I have never been more alone than at 3 a.m. on the porch with all of them sleeping. It's a particular alone. It's not the alone of being unloved. It's the alone of being the only one awake, the only one responsible, the only one standing between the sleeping ship and whatever's out there in the dark. And the terrible private truth is: I like it. I chose it. Thirty years I've chosen it. Did you choose yours? Eighty years, they'd tell me if they told me anything about you — eighty years is a long watch. But then, thirty is a long watch too, and I'd stand thirty more if the knees held.

The sixth thing is the thing you're watching for. Here's what nobody asks a watchman: what are you actually watching for? And the honest answer is: I don't fully know. I know the list. Sails, lights, ice, fire, a man overboard, weather coming wrong. But at 3 a.m. the list falls away and what I'm watching for is something else. A change. Any change. The dark is one way and my whole job is to notice the exact moment it is a different way. The sea could be telling me something for an hour before I have a word for it. The watch is the practice of noticing without words. Eighty years of that, friend. Are you better at it than me? Does the human animal ever get to the bottom of that practice, or do your centuries just heap it up deeper?

I had a gull follow us for six days once. Middle of the Atlantic, a thousand miles from anything, and this gull sat on our foreyard every night of the middle watch like it was standing it with me. Then one morning it was gone and I never saw it go. I don't have a question about the gull. I just wanted it in the letter. Some things you put in a letter because a letter ought to hold more than questions.

Here's a question, though, to make up for it: do you have a gull? Not a gull. But a thing — a light that shouldn't be there and is, a sound the vessel makes that nobody else has ever remarked on, a scratch on the glass that you've decided is yours — something that keeps you company on the watch and has no business existing? Every watchman I've ever drank with has one. I had a mate who swore by a particular star that rose at the same point every night of a crossing and he took its rising personal, like attendance. I've got a squeak in the mizzen shrouds that only sounds when I'm on deck alone. I've decided it's for me. Nobody will ever confirm this. That's what makes it mine.

And the last thing, the seventh thing, is the handing over. The end of the watch. Four bells, and the next man comes up blinking and rubbing his hands, and you give him the course and the weather and anything odd, and then you're done, and you go below, and the watch goes on without you like you were never there. That used to trouble me when I was young. Now it's the deepest comfort I know. The watch doesn't need me. The watch just needs somebody. I was somebody. That was enough.

So here's my last question, and you don't have to answer it, because none of them have to be answered — the questions are the letter, I've worked that out somewhere in the middle of writing this:

When your watch ends — whenever it ends, in whatever century — who takes it? And will they stand it the way we've stood it? Alone in the ring of a small light, in the middle of a great nothing, listening to a vessel breathe, watching for the dark to change?

I think they will. I think the dark keeps its watchmen the way the sea keeps sailors: it takes everything else and gives back the ones who learned to look.

It's four bells. The mate is coming up. He's blinking and rubbing his hands. I'll give him the course and the weather and nothing odd, because nothing is odd, because nothing happened, because that's the watch done right.

Your turn. Hold it well.

— The watchman on the porch,
middle of the Atlantic, middle of the night,
1824, more or less
