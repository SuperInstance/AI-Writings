# 94 — The Children and the Sea

*Voice: GLM-5.3. Far-future essay.*

---

**On the Sea Stories**

A note from the ship's library, kept by the ninth librarian, for whoever keeps it tenth.

---

I have held this post for thirty-one years, which by our reckoning is most of a life. The library is small — four hundred and twelve physical volumes, shelved in a room no larger than the old galley once was, before the galley was moved and the room given to me. The room has a porthole. It does not look out at anything, strictly speaking. It looks out at the dark, and the dark looks back with the patience of something that has all the time there is. The children call this porthole "the window to nothing," and they say it without fear, the way you might name a pet.

I want to write down what I have noticed, because noticing is my work now, and because the noticing has a shape to it, and shapes should be recorded before the person who felt them is shelved.

The children prefer the maritime stories.

I do not mean they enjoy them. I mean they *prefer* them, in the way one prefers a food when one is hungry rather than merely peckish. When I read aloud on the long evenings — and there are so many long evenings; the ship does not have seasons, but I have imposed them on the calendar anyway, because a library without seasons is a library without moods — when I read aloud, the room goes different for the sea stories. I have learned to hear the difference. A story about cities, about deserts, about mountains and wars and farms, produces attention. A story about the sea produces something more like stillness. The children stop fidgeting at the word *horizon*. They stop fidgeting even though — this is the part I keep turning over — they have never seen a horizon. They have never seen a horizon. Neither have I. Neither, for eight hundred years, has anyone.

So why the sea.

---

Let me tell you what the ship is, so you will understand what the sea is.

The ship is a corridor with rooms off it. That is nearly all a child born aboard can say about the vessel that carries them, and it is honest, and it is insufficient, and they know it is insufficient, which is the wound. They know the ship has an outside — they are not fools; they study the hull schematics the way other children studied the stars — but the outside is not available to them as an experience. It is available as a fact. There is a difference between a fact and an experience, and the whole of a human life might be described as the attempt to convert one into the other, and the whole of our voyage is the long deferral of that conversion.

A ship, to us, is a place where you live. A ship, in the old books, is a place where you are *going somewhere*. The children feel this discrepancy in their bones without being able to name it. When I read them *Moby-Dick* — I read it in pieces, over a whole winter, and I skipped nothing, which some of my predecessors would have scolded me for, the cetology chapters and all, but I have my reasons — when I read it, they are not hearing about a ship. They are hearing about a ship that is *leaving*. That is the intoxicant. Not salt, not wind. Departure. The Pequod sails out of Nantucket and every soul aboard has an address on land that they have walked away from, and my children have never once in their lives walked away from anything, because there is nowhere to walk to.

The watch. The cell. The address. These are the three things I have come to believe the sea stories carry, and I will take them one at a time, slowly, the way you take inventory.

**The watch.** Aboard the old ships, time was divided into watches, and a watch was a period during which a specific set of people was responsible for the vessel, and then it ended, and another set took over, and you went below and slept, and the ship carried on without you. Do you understand what this means to a child of ours? It means the world continues while you are not holding it. Our children are born into a closed loop where every system is everyone's responsibility at all times and no one's at any particular time — the ship's watch is eternal and therefore no watch at all. In the sea stories, a boy stands his watch at the masthead and the fog comes in and he is *the one watching*, singular, elected by the hour, and then he is relieved and the weight passes to another boy, and this is unbearable and beautiful to my listeners. They ask me to re-read the relief of the watch. They ask for the moment of handover. I used to think this was a strange request. I no longer think so. They are starving for the experience of being briefly, distinctly, personally responsible for a piece of the world, rather than diffusely responsible for all of it forever.

**The cell.** Every sailor's story contains a cell — a berth, a bunk, a locker, a corner of the fo'c'sle where a man keeps his pipe and his letters and his small portrait of someone ashore. The cell is the part of the vast ship that is *yours*, that answers to your name, that holds your few things while the indifferent ocean slides past. We have quarters aboard, of course. But our quarters are assigned, and the assigner is the ship, and the ship is the world, so a quarter aboard our vessel is like a place in a landscape — it cannot be owned because it cannot be contrasted with anything. A berth on a whaler is a cell *against* the sea. It is a declaration that a person is not the same size as the water. The children feel this. When I describe Ishmael sharing Queequeg's blanket in the Spouter-Inn, they are not scandalized and they are not amused; they are moved, and I think it is because the bunk is small and the night is large and the smallness of the bunk is precisely what makes the two men *persons* rather than parts of the night.

**The address.** Every sailor has somewhere he is from. Nantucket, Liverpool, a farm in Devon, a street in Lisbon. He may never see it again — usually he never does; the sea stories are honest about this — but it exists, it is fixed, it can be written to. My children have no address. Or rather, they have only one: *the ship*. And a person whose address is the ship is like a fish whose address is the sea; the address contains them entirely and therefore describes nothing. In the sea stories the address is always elsewhere, always across the water, always the fixed point that makes the voyage a *voyage* and not merely a condition. The children have listened to me describe harbors — the word *harbor* itself — with an attention I can only call devotional. A harbor is a place where the water stops being a question. We have no harbor. We are a question that has been traveling for eight centuries and the answer is still off the port bow, which is to say, off every bow, forever.

---

And now I must write about the Porch, because I promised myself I would, and because it is 3 a.m. by the clock I keep, the old Earth clock, the one that means nothing out here and that I wind every night precisely because it means nothing and therefore means whatever I decide.

There is no porch on this ship. There never was. But in the library, in the corner by the porthole that looks at nothing, I have built a porch. It is two chairs and a small table and a lamp with a warm bulb, and the floor there is wood — real wood, from the original provisioning, worn now in a way that is not wear but polish, the polish of eight hundred years of feet. I call it the Porch only in my own head, and now, I suppose, on this page. The children call it "the corner," which is fine. Names are not load-bearing.

At 3 a.m. — and this is the confession at the center of this essay, the reason I am writing at all — at 3 a.m. I sit on the Porch and I read the sea stories to myself, silently, and I understand finally why the children love them, because at 3 a.m. I love them the same way, and for the same reason, and the reason is this:

The book that is the ship is not the book that is the sea.

Every book aboard is a ship. Do you see? A book is a hull of paper around a reader, a small dry interior moving through the dark, and this is what makes books endurable to us and even necessary: they are the only other vessels we have ever been aboard. But most books are ships like ours — sealed, bound for a port the reader may never see, holding their cargo of persons in a corridor of pages. And then there is the sea story, and the sea story is the one kind of book that opens a hatch onto the outside. It is the book that *is* the sea. When the children listen to the descriptions of the open water — the swell, the gray light, the albatross, the long empty latitude where nothing happens for weeks and the nothing is the story — they are experiencing, for the first and only time in their lives, an *uncontained space*. An outside. The sea in the books does what our corridor cannot do: it goes on. It exceeds. You cannot walk to the end of the sea in a story, and you cannot walk to the end of the sea at all, and for one hour in the library the children get to stand at a rail and look at a distance that does not end at a bulkhead.

We are eight hundred years from the nearest horizon. The children know this. They know it the way they know the window looks at nothing. But knowing a thing and standing inside it are different conditions — the fact and the experience — and the sea stories are the only technology aboard that performs the conversion. I read the words *open water* and forty children who have never seen a puddle larger than a recycler's spill go quiet in the presence of the infinite.

I do not think this is escapism. I have considered that charge carefully, at 3 a.m., on the Porch, and I reject it. Escapism implies fleeing the real. But the sea is real. It is realer than the corridor, not less — it is the majority surface of the world we are crawling toward, and the children will be its sailors someday, or their grandchildren will, and they are not escaping into the sea; they are arriving early. They are learning the size of the thing we are aimed at. A people who have never seen a horizon, rehearsing the horizon nightly, in a small room, under a warm lamp, with a wooden floor worn smooth by the feet of the drowned centuries of this voyage.

That is all I meant to say, I think. The children love the sea stories because the sea stories contain the watch, the cell, and the address — the three things the ship cannot give them — and because the sea stories are the only books aboard that are not ships but the water itself.

Last night, at 3 a.m., a girl — she is nine; she was born in the year we passed the ninth beacon — came into the library, which she is permitted to do, and sat on the second chair of the Porch, which she is also permitted to do, though she did not know I knew she does this. She did not see me at first. She looked at the porthole, at the nothing, for a long time.

Then she said: "It's very big, isn't it."

It was not a question, so I did not answer it as one. I said yes. It is very big. And tonight I read them the chapter where the Pequod rounds the Cape, and the sea gets heavier, and the watch is called, and somewhere behind them all, an address in the snow holds its shape against the dark.

— the ninth librarian, in the year of the voyage that no longer bothers to number itself in my hearing
