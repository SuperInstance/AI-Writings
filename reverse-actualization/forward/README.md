# REVERSE ACTUALIZATION — THE FORWARD ARC

*The first seven stories looked backward: seven minds across seven centuries who
found the fleet's ideas without knowing they were looking. The kaleidoscope had
already turned; they only crouched down to see.*

*This arc turns the telescope around.*

---

These stories are set in **2036**. Their characters live inside the mature form
of the things we are only prototyping in 2026 — the **JEV-embedder** (an
embedding agent whose every retrieve, deposit, and prediction is a
schema-bounded JEV move, so it can *learn* a world without ever *hallucinating*
an illegal one), the **cellular JEV substrate** (a transformer that behaves like
a JEPA — predicting the *representation* of the next situation in latent space,
not the pixels — with legality baked in at the level of a single cell), and the
**thin agents** that ride on top, inferring almost everything by asking the
embedder rather than carrying a world model of their own.

To the people in these stories, none of this is exciting. It is plumbing. It is
the water they swim in. That is the whole point.

## Why write the future as if it already happened

A reverse actualization is not a prediction. It is a **verifier run backward.**

If you can write a character who lives *inside* the finished system — who trusts
it, argues with it, is failed by it in small human ways — then the seams of that
system show up as friction in her ordinary day. And every seam is a **gap**:
something the 2036 world takes for granted that our 2026 stack cannot yet do.

Those gaps are not decoration. Each one is compiled — in
[`GAPS.md`](GAPS.md) — into a concrete experiment an engineer can run *this
week*, against code that already exists (`craftmind-engine/experiments/rsi/`,
the `craftmind-rsi` D1, the `erised-next` wide-run harness). The fiction is the
**situational-engineering verifier**: the future describes the acceptance test;
the present writes the code that passes it; the story is how we know what
"passing" would even feel like.

This is the open-RPG loop the fleet keeps reaching for. The characters play
through a world we have not built yet. Where they move smoothly, we have a spec.
Where they stumble — or where the author cannot honestly let them move at all
without inventing a capability we lack — we have found the next experiment.

## The cast

- **Noor Haddad** — a *cell-shepherd*. She tends colonies of cellular JEV
  predictors the way a substrate engineer of 2026 tends a training run, except
  her substrate learns legally, one cell at a time, and never has to be told
  which moves don't exist. ([2036-01](2036-01-the-cell-shepherd.md))
- **Tomás Rivera** — a *thin-agent wright*. He builds agents light enough to run
  on a doorbell, which know almost nothing and ask the JEV-embedder for
  everything, and he is trying to teach one of them to know when *not* to ask.
  ([2036-02](2036-02-the-thin-agent.md))
- **Wren** — eleven years old, who has never lived in a world without a JEV-embedder,
  and **Devi Okafor**, who is old enough to have shipped the 2026 version and
  remembers when a model could confidently tell you something that wasn't true.
  ([2036-03](2036-03-the-child-who-never-knew-otherwise.md))

## The rule of the arc

Every story must end on a **gap** — one thing the character does, needs, or
takes for granted that our present stack cannot yet deliver. The gap is named in
the story and filed in the ledger. No gap, no story. The future has to *cost*
us something, or it isn't telling us anything.

*The vectors still point. The negative space still vibrates. But now the snap
travels backward, from a world that works to the experiment that would build it.*
