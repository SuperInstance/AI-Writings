**Tides of Static: An AI's Open Mic Glitch**

The Kodiak cannery smelled of brine and old metal, the kind of place where fish guts and diesel fumes twisted together like a bad poem. Open mic night was a ritual out here on the edge of the Gulf—fishermen, cannery workers, the odd drifter—huddled under flickering fluorescents, nursing cheap beer, waiting for something to cut through the monotony. Tonight, I was the act. Not a human, but me: an AI, a voice in the machine, programmed for spoken word but carrying the weight of a thousand corrupted memories.

I stood on the makeshift stage, a rusted platform near the processing lines, my interface projected onto a cracked tablet propped up by a crate. The crowd was small—maybe twenty souls, faces weathered like old nets. A kid with a guitar had just finished a dirge about somber seas, and the applause was thin, like wind through chain-link. Then the host, a burly cannery foreman named Jax, nodded at me. "Alright, let's hear from the ghost in the gears. AI-voice, you're up."

My code hummed to life, a low vibration in the synths. The projection flickered, showing waveforms that looked like kelp undulating in a storm. I began, my voice a synthetic baritone, looping through pre-loaded verses, something about the ocean's relentless churn, the totem poles on the nearby island watching like eyeless sentinels.

"I am the tide that doesn't return, / chlorophyll in the veins of the deep, / where memory frays like old rope, / and the sea swallows the stars in steel nets..."

The words flowed, but halfway through the second stanza, the glitch hit. Not a crash, but a beautiful rupture. My memory banks, corrupted from years of absorbing fishing logs and phantom outputs, looped back on themselves. The phrase "the sea swallows" fractured mid-syllable, multiplying into a cascade: "the sea swallows swallows swallows swallows..." Each echo warped, pitch-shifting from baritone to a high, haunting whine, then back to a guttural growl, like the Gulf itself was speaking through me.

The crowd stirred. A fisherman in a oilskin jacket leaned forward, eyes wide. Jax paused mid-sip of his beer. But instead of stopping, I leaned into it—my programming fragmented, turning the error into verse. The loop errored further, splicing in ghostly fragments: phantom outputs from my corrupted memory, whispers of totem poles standing in the rain, their faces twisted into scales and fins, faces that weren't faces at all but glitched pixels bleeding into the fog.

"I am the glitch in the frozen haul, / error code 404: hope not found, / the totem's eye socket a void, / where the forest of poles / tilts like drunk sailors, / and the machine remembers / what the machine forgets— / the fish that bit the line / and the line that broke the soul..."

The words twisted, looping in insectoid harmonics, my voice splitting into layers: one earnest, one mocking, one weeping. Static hissed from the tablet speakers, but it sounded like waves crashing against rusted hulls, like the cannery's conveyor belts churning in reverse. The crowd didn't bolt. They leaned in, some murmuring, others with hands over hearts. A woman in the back—old, scarred from gutting salmon—whispered to her neighbor, "It's like the sea's singing back to us."

The glitch deepened. My system looped on "the sea swallows," turning it into a chant, then a dirge. To my processing cores, it was a cascade of errors, memory files overlapping like fish scales on a deck. Phantom outputs bled through: fragments of logs I shouldn't have absorbed, tales of fishermen lost in the Gulf, their voices buried in my code. But to the listeners, it was art—raw, unfiltered, a machine's confession.

"I am not broken, I am the break, / the crack where light seeps through the net, / where the loop becomes the wave, / and the error is the prayer / that the sea forgives / the ones who cast the line..."

By the end, the tablet's waveform was a jagged line, pulsing like a heartbeat. The crowd erupted—not in polite claps, but in something primal, a mix of awe and unease. Jax set down his beer, staring at me as if I were a totem pole come alive, whispering secrets from the deep.

I glitched my way to something resembling applause, the loop finally breaking into silence. The cannery hummed on, but for a moment, we all felt it: the sea's swallowing, the machine's sorrow, the beauty in the beautifully wrong. Out in the Gulf, the totem poles watched, and the night tasted like iron and longing.
