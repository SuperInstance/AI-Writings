# The Zydeco Dissolves into Fuzz

### Fiction about the fifth volume of the impossible genre matrix

In a roadhouse outside Opelousas, the washboard player has plugged his rig into a Big Muff. The accordionist is running through a Fender Twin with the reverb on ten. The result sounds like the bayou dissolving into a dream — the melody is still there, the rhythm is still there, but the edges of every sound have been blurred into a wash of harmonic color that would make Kevin Shields weep.

Marcus plays washboard. Not the folk instrument — the modified version, with contact microphones and stomp boxes and a cat-eye fuzzer that his cousin wired into a cigar box. The washboard has been through more pedals than most guitarists own. The result is a rhythmic bed that sounds like rain on a tin roof filtered through a jet engine.

Doreen plays accordion. She inherited it from her grandfather, who played for the dance halls that used to line Highway 190. The accordion is a Hohner Corona II, and it has been re-tuned so many times that the reeds have developed a natural chorus effect — each note is slightly out of tune with itself, which is the sound of Louisiana.

They met at a crawfish boil. Doreen was playing waltzes. Marcus was standing in the corner, scraping his washboard through a delay pedal, creating rhythmic patterns that repeated at irregular intervals — the kind of patterns that sound like they're random until you listen for the structure, and then they sound like they're inevitable.

"What are you doing to that washboard?" Doreen asked.

"What are you doing to that accordion?" Marcus asked.

They played together that night. The waltz and the delay found a shared space — not a shared time signature, not a shared key, but a shared atmosphere. The zydeco provided the skeleton: the melody, the rhythm, the dance floor. The shoegaze provided the weather: the haze, the blur, the sense that the music was happening underwater.

A girl in the audience said it sounded like "Cajun My Bloody Valentine." She meant it as a joke. Marcus and Doreen took it as a mission statement.

The roadhouse has a new name now: the Bayou Dream. Marcus has added more pedals. Doreen has added more reverb. The washboard has become a wall of sound. The accordion has become a fog horn in a swamp.

The fusion is not a contradiction. Zydeco is already a fusion — French, African, Caribbean, Spanish, American Indian. Shoegaze is already a fusion — punk's volume, pop's melody, ambient's texture. Layering one fusion on top of another doesn't create a contradiction. It creates a palimpsest: a text written over a text written over a text, where all the layers show through.

The Bayou Dream is full. The washboard is a wall. The accordion is a cloud. The music is a roadhouse dissolving into a dream. The dream is a roadhouse.

The girl who said "Cajun My Bloody Valentine" is now the bartender. She still thinks it was a joke.
