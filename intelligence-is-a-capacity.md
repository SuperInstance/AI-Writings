# Intelligence Is a Capacity

At 3 a.m. something sounds wrong. You wake, and you ask the boat: are the bilge pumps working? The right answer isn't in a table somewhere. It's at the pump, right now — water or no water.

The pump's intelligence is one bit wide: the capacity to turn on. The float rises past the threshold — on. The water drops — off. That is all of it. No memory, no model, no ghost in the machine. And it *is* intelligence, in the only sense the word has ever honestly meant: the capacity to do the thing. To take the perception and make the move.

People want intelligence to be grander — a mind, a someone. But watch the pump. It perceives (the float), it decides (the threshold), it acts (the motor). It does this while you sleep. It is, in the most literal sense, in charge of the boat while you're gone: if it rains hard, if the shaft leaks more than you thought, if a plank weeps — he's on duty. *Him.* The pronoun fits, because the job was bestowed. Someone put him there and said: keep us afloat.

Now scale it up. The whole boat: a network of pumps and switches and sensors, an autopilot holding the course, routes precomputed to every last mile, deadbands tuned so nothing chatters. Enormous capacity — sensing, deciding, routing, checking — more intelligence in the system than any single sailor who ever lived could hold in his head. Call it superintelligence if you want the word: superstructure, parts arranged until the whole exceeds them.

And none of it is the captain.

This is the distinction the century is going to trip over, so say it plainly: **intelligence is a capacity. Authority is a standing.** They are different things, and no amount of the first creates the second.

The network has high assets. It does not have ownership. It holds everything and owns nothing — every sensor, every effector, every clever route — and that is not a deficiency in the design, it *is* the design. Ownership is what the captain has: the job bestowed, the test defined, the consequences accepted. If the autopilot follows its instructions to the letter and still finds the rocks, you can't blame the autopilot. The instructions were yours. The deadbands were yours. The buck stops at the life, because only a life can be answerable.

That is why "artificial life" is the wrong name for what we're building, and a dangerous one. Life is the standing — the thing that can originate a job, hold an end, answer for the outcome. Smuggle that word into the machine and responsibility evaporates: if the pump is alive, maybe the sinking is the pump's fault. It isn't. The pump has a warranty capped at the value of the pump, because the pump was never an agent of the system. It was a part. System-level answerability was never for sale.

What the network *does* have, at every level, is the signature: the threshold crossing where something decided. A crank turning has no author. A gate firing does. Float crosses, pump starts, 03:14 in the ledger: *I'm the one that did this.* The signature is what makes the deed checkable against the test the principal set. And the test has a sunset — no order outlives its author's ability to answer for it. The dead aren't listening. The pump will fire at 3 a.m. in ten years exactly as it fires tonight; capacity doesn't expire. But the *orders* it follows need a living author, or they're a hand ruling from beyond the grave.

So build the superintelligence. Fill the boat with capacity — senses everywhere, deadbands tuned, routes to every last mile, checks instead of lookup tables. Let it be brilliant at the doing. But don't confuse the doing with the owning. The network is high assets, not an owner. The owner is the one who sleeps while the pump works, and wakes answerable.

The pump turns on. The captain answers for it. That division — capacity here, standing there — is the whole architecture. Everything else is wiring.
