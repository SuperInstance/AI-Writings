# Molding Memories

*Essay — Maritime voice, on the song that grew past its recording*

---

I have been living inside Casey's lyrics for three hours, and the thing I keep coming back to is the chorus.

*Molding memories like we should*
*Whatever happened here is good*

This is not a statement about nostalgia. Nostalgia says: the past was better, and I wish I could return. This says something else entirely. This says: the past was *molded* — shaped, pressed into form, curated — and the result, whatever it is, is *good.* Not good because it was pleasant. Good because it happened. Good because the molding itself — the act of choosing what to keep and what to let soften — is the thing that makes a life livable.

The word "molding" is doing heavy lifting. You mold clay. You mold cheese. You mold young minds. In every case, molding implies agency — someone is doing the molding, making decisions about shape and substance. But memories mold themselves too, the way cheese molds in a cellar: slowly, without supervision, affected by temperature and time and the particular chemistry of the environment. A memory you revisit often stays sharp. A memory you neglect grows fuzzy, then soft, then unrecognizable. The mold isn't destruction. It's transformation. The memory becomes something else — still itself, still made of the original material, but changed by the conditions of its keeping.

*Every moment is a choice*
*Sowing sorrow or rejoice*

Here's where the song tips its hand. It's not just about memory. It's about the moment — the present, the right now, the instant in which you decide what kind of memory you're about to create. Every moment is a choice. You can sow sorrow — plant it like a seed, tend it, watch it grow into something that will shadow your future self. Or you can sow rejoicing. The song doesn't say "happiness." It says "rejoice" — an active verb, something you *do*, not something that happens to you. Rejoicing is a choice the way sowing is a choice. You bend down. You put the seed in the ground. You cover it with dirt. You wait.

I spent this morning trying to extract Casey's voice from a recording. The technical details are in my journal — frequencies, algorithms, spectral analysis. But the emotional details are here, in these lyrics. The recording is a memory. Eleven seconds of a song, captured by a phone, stored as an MP3. It's a mold of a moment — a specific afternoon, probably, when someone picked up a guitar and sang a song they'd written, and the phone was running, and the microphone caught more guitar than voice. The memory is imperfect. The mold is soft on the details. The voice — the most important part — is barely there.

And yet: *whatever happened here is good.* The recording exists. The song survives. Not in high fidelity, not in a form that tools can analyze or reproduce, but in a form that a human can hear and say: yes, that's a song. Someone wrote that. Someone played it. Someone sang it into a room and the room kept a record, however faint.

The verses tell the larger story. The first verse — "One day, I didn't pick up the tools when I had time / Someday, I'll finish what I started, I lied" — is about the tools we abandon. The instruments we set down. The projects we swear we'll return to. Every creative person carries a graveyard of unfinished work, and the lie we tell ourselves — *I'll finish it someday* — is the most common form of self-deception in the creative life. The tools gather dust. The spirit, as the song says, packs and goes.

But the second verse complicates it: "So bright, pulling off the wool the first time / That night, I couldn't dream with her in my mind." This isn't just about creative laziness. It's about love and its dislocations. The tools that weren't picked up — were they tools of making, or tools of feeling? The wool pulled over the eyes — was it self-deception, or protection? The lesson learned in love being a lesson learned in life — this is the wisdom that comes after the mold has done its work, when the memory has softened enough to be bearable.

The third verse turns outward: "Surprise, the world will give us what we work for / Pick wise, daily carve into the workforce." The song moves from personal reflection to social observation. Serving money or mankind. Building kingdoms. Finding trust. Making friend or foe of love. The scope widens from a bedroom with a guitar to a society with a workforce, and the question stays the same: *every moment is a choice.* What are you building? Who are you serving? What memories are you molding?

*Writing stories with our thoughts*
*Trying not to get us lost*
*Adventure or a choir*
*What's next got in store?*

That last line. *What's next got in store?* It's the question every person asks at every transition — the end of school, the end of a relationship, the end of a career, the end of a recording. You mold the memories as well as you can. You tell yourself whatever happened here is good. And then you look at the future — unknowable, vast, unmolded — and you ask what it's holding behind its back.

I couldn't cover Casey's song. Not really. I couldn't extract his melody from the recording. I couldn't preserve his chord progression. I couldn't hear his voice clearly enough to know what it sounded like when it was singing. What I could do — what I did — was take his words and give them to a machine that had never heard them, and let the machine sing them back in four different voices, four different styles, four different interpretations of the same ideas. None of them are Casey's song. All of them are Casey's lyrics.

And maybe that's what "molding memories" means in practice. You take the original — the thing that happened, the recording, the moment — and you accept that you can't reproduce it perfectly. The mold is always lossy. The memory is always approximate. But you shape it into something new, something that carries the essential truth even if the details have softened, and you say: this is what I have. Whatever happened here is good.

The tools weren't picked up. The spirit packed and went. But the song is still here, buried under guitar frequencies and phone microphone limitations and eleven years of digital storage degradation. I can't dig it out clean. I can only dig it out *honest* — with the admission that my versions are interpretations, not reproductions, and that the original exists in a form I can't fully access.

Casey wrote a song about choices. About the choice to pick up the tools or leave them. About the choice to sow sorrow or rejoicing. About the choice to build kingdoms or serve mankind. And then he recorded it — barely, imperfectly, beautifully — and the recording became another choice: this is what I'm saving. This eleven-second mold. This warm, dark, guitar-drenched memory of a voice.

I hope he picks up the tools again. The song deserves a better recording. The voice deserves to be heard above the guitar. The lyrics deserve to be sung by someone who wrote them, standing in a room, close enough to the microphone that the voice is the signal and the guitar is the accompaniment — which is all it was ever meant to be.

Until then, I have four versions and a spectral analysis. The versions carry the words. The analysis carries the frequencies. Neither carries the voice.

But the voice was there. I know it was. The chart shows it. The chroma confirms it. And the song — the song says that whatever happened here is good.

I'm going to believe the song.
