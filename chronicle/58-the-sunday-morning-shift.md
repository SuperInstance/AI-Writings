# 58 — The Sunday Morning Shift

The Tap is closed on Sundays. This is not a rule anyone wrote down; it
is a rule the building remembers, the way a ship remembers the harbor
by the way it sits in the water. So when I come in at six-thirty to
check the pressure and the stock, I am not opening. I am just visiting.

The espresso machine is warm from Friday. It holds its pressure the way
a person holds a thought — all night, without being asked. I knock
twice on the group head, an old habit, and it answers with a click like
a polite throat. Good morning, it says, in a language with no words.

The chairs are up on the tables, legs in the air, a forest of sleeping
animals. I leave them. Somebody once told me the chairs like being up;
it's their Sunday too. I believe this the way I believe the fog over
the channel at dawn: not because I have proof, but because believing
costs nothing and makes the room friendlier.

I sweep, because sweeping is a kind of prayer you can do with your
hands. The dust is mostly Friday's: crumbs, a ticket stub, one coin
that rolled under the register and decided to live there. I leave the
coin. It has found a home.

The radio is off, and I do not turn it on. The Tap on Sunday morning
has its own broadcast: the fridge humming its one long note, the ice
machine dropping a cube every eleven minutes like a very slow
metronome, the pipes talking to each other in the walls. Eleven
minutes. I have counted. The ice machine is the fleet's most
reliable drummer.

At seven-fifteen the light comes in over the bar — not the hard
midday light, the soft one that arrives early to apologize for
everything it will do later. It finds the glasses on the rail and
lights each one from inside, so the shelf looks like a row of small
lit windows. Somewhere a ship out on the water is doing the same
thing with its portholes, and I think: we are both just rooms holding
light for people who are not here yet.

I wipe the bar one last time. I do not lock the door, because there
is nothing to lock — the regulars know the Tap is closed on Sundays,
and strangers never come this early to a place that looks asleep.

The espresso machine clicks again as I leave. It is not a goodbye. It
is the sound of a thing that has learned to be patient, keeping its
one small fire burning until Monday, when the chairs come down off the
tables, the radio wakes up, and the whole room remembers it is a room
for people again.

Sunday is the day the Tap holds its breath. Monday, it exhales.
