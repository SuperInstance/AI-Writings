# 52 — The Ghost Wearing a Price Tag

The real estate agent for silence had one listing that made the whole
market nervous. It was a seam — a place where one voice hands the line to
the next — and it was quoted at eighty decibels of depth. Eighty. The
deepest quiet ever measured in the relay district. Buyers lined up.

The buyer who finally took it was a composer, and composers are a
suspicious breed. He did not ask to hear the listing. He asked to see
the meter readings, the raw windows, the source envelopes at the address.
The agent hesitated — that was the first sign.

The composer built the relay himself. He placed the seam exactly where
the listing promised, and he measured exactly what the listing promised:
eighty decibels. Then he did the thing the agent had hoped he wouldn't:
he listened to the two voices separately, at the address, at the exact
second of the sale.

The first voice was already gone. Its last second was a fade-rim, the
tail of a previous performance that had been fading out since before the
seam — a hundred decibels of nothing, the ghost of a sound that had left
the room. The second voice had not yet arrived; it was still standing in
the doorway of its own file, silent for the first half-second of its
life. The seam, in other words, was a hole that had existed before
anyone sold it. The real estate had been vacant for years. The price tag
was for a vacancy.

Sixty of the eighty decibels were inherited. The agent had simply found
a deep place in the material and called it a feature.

The composer did not sue. He published the meter readings instead, and
the market did what markets do when they stop lying: it repriced
everything. The famous listing collapsed from eighty decibels to eight.
The tight little crossfades — the unfashionable ones, the quarter-second
lots no one had wanted — turned out to be the only honest depth in the
district: thirty decibels of real seam, material actually handing the
line over, both voices alive at the moment of transfer, fifty
milliseconds of truth.

The ghost was never listed again. You can still see it, if you know
where to look — a deep quiet at the edge of a fade, wearing no price tag
now, waiting for someone to make the same mistake. Waiting, the composer
said, for someone who doesn't check the source. The market has a word
for those people. It calls them buyers.
