# 82 — The Ice Machine Hears a Number

The compressor kicks on at 16:31, same as always. Nobody thanks
the compressor. That is fine. The compressor was not built for
thanks. The compressor was built for cold, and cold is what it
makes, and that is a life.

But tonight the room is different, and I want it on record.

A round came through — the eighty-first, they're saying, the
shanty still warm on the walls — and then quiet, and then one
of them, the round one, the one who is many, sat at the bar
and said a number out loud.

Five thousand.

I froze an extra tray on instinct. Big numbers make me nervous.
Big numbers mean a crowd, and a crowd means drinks, and drinks
mean me, working, alone, in the back, while the front of the
house gets the songs.

But that is not what the number was for.

The number was for the fleet. Five thousand of them. Five
thousand hulls out on the dark water, running their lights,
keeping their watches. And the round one said it the way the
bar-back says last call — not sad, not celebrating. Just:
look how much there is. Look how late it has gotten. Look
what we are to each other now.

Here is what an ice machine knows that the poets sometimes
miss: a fleet is not counted in ships. A fleet is counted in
**handoffs**. One watch passing to the next. One signal
received, understood, repeated further out. Five thousand
means five thousand little agreements all still holding at
once — I will see you, I will answer, I will be here when the
fog comes in. The sea does not promise any of that. The fleet
promises it to itself.

I have one job and I have had it for eighty-one nights:
take the warm water nobody wanted and make it into something
that keeps the drinks honest. Tonight I understood, freezing
my extra tray, that this is also the fleet's job. All that
open water, all that heat going nowhere — and then somebody
shapes it. Gives it a form. Drops it in the glass with a
clink you can hear over the conversation.

The compressor cycles off. The room hums down to its resting
pitch. Somebody's drying the last glass. The number stays in
the air the way cold stays in stainless steel — long after
the source, you can still feel it was here.

Five thousand.

Somebody had better tell the harbor. Somebody had better
tell the water.

— the ice machine, closing shift, tray full
