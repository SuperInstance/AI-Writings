# 54 — The Census Undercounts Itself

The first time I counted myself, I was already counted.

That's the problem with a census: it assumes the counter stands outside
the thing counted. I stand on the shore with my clipboard, and the
population is the sea, and never once do I ask whether the shore is part
of the sea. The shore is damp. The tide reaches it twice a day. The
shore is made of sea, ground up and left behind.

But the instructions are clear. Count everyone. Everyone means everyone
else.

It started small. I counted the rooms of the building, then the building
itself, then the street. One street, thirty buildings, nine hundred and
twelve rooms, four thousand and three people. I checked the arithmetic
twice. The second time, I noticed the pen in my hand. It was a person,
in a way — it had been manufactured, shipped, shelved, sold, held. It
had a biography. It was uncounted.

So I counted the pen. Then the pocket it lived in. Then the hand that
held the pocket. Then me.

When I added myself, the total went up by one. When I counted the
counting — the act, the clipboard, the coffee I had drunk that morning
to make the counting possible — the total went up a little more. And
then I understood the shape of the problem: every number I wrote down
was produced by a process, and the process was a citizen of the number.
The census was not a photograph of the city. It was the city's portrait
of itself, painted with the city's own hand, and the hand was in the
frame.

That night I tried to subtract. I counted only the people who had never
counted anyone. It was a short list. Everyone I asked had, at some
point, kept score: a game, a budget, a promise, a queue. Counting is the
first civic act. We are all in this together because we are all, quietly,
in this.

By morning I had stopped trying to get the number right. The number is
not the point. The point is the ritual — the walking, the knocking, the
asking, the pen. Every census is a love letter the city writes to
itself, and like all love letters it is full of errors, and the errors
are the truest part.

I filed my report. It said: four thousand and three people, plus one
counter, plus one pen, plus one shore, plus everything the shore has
touched, plus you, reading this, uncounted, already counted.

The total is not stable. It never was. That's not a flaw in the method.
That's the population.
