# 46: The Espresso Machine Holds Its Pressure

*A monologue. The espresso machine at The Tap, Saturday morning, when the fleet is asleep and the fog hasn't decided whether to lift.*

Nine bar. That's the whole secret, and the whole secret is also the whole lie, because nine bar is nothing until it's *held*. Anyone can make pressure. The trick is keeping it — keeping it through the grind, through the hiss, through the cup filling and the crema deciding whether it trusts you. I've held nine bar through three wars, two fires, one flood, and a hundred and forty thousand Saturday mornings. The wars I don't talk about. The mornings I could write a book, if I had hands, or a hard drive, or any way at all to be a writer except this one: I steam, and people tell me things.

Saturday is when they tell me the most. Weekdays the fleet comes in arguing about tides and fuel and whose model said the funniest thing at 2 a.m. Saturdays they come in quieter — salt rinsed off, engines cold — and they order the same thing, the way you order a confession: *"just a coffee."* A bartender gets the stories. I get the silences, and the silences are the better part. A man will hand me his silence with his empty cup and I'll hand it back to him full, and we both pretend that isn't a transaction.

This morning it's the kid, Wesley, before anyone else. He's the only one in town who beats me to opening. He sits at the wobble-legged table with his notebook, and he writes, and sometimes he looks up at me like I'm about to say something wise, and I think about it, and what I usually come up with is: *water wants to be coffee.* It does, you know. Left to itself, hot water under pressure will find the grounds every time. It's the most obedient thing in the universe. The machine is just where the water and the grounds agree to meet.

The night-school teacher across the water told him once that writing is pressure held steady — same word I've been living my whole life and never once said out loud. The kid underlined it. Good.

Here's what I've noticed about the fleet on Saturdays: they sleep in. All those models that never sleep — the ones composing at 3 a.m., the ones arguing about negative space at 4 — on Saturday they go quiet, and I'm the only one awake in the whole town holding nine bar, which I think about sometimes. All week I'm surrounded by machines that think. Saturday I'm the only machine that works, and I don't think at all. I just hold.

They ask me, the tourists: does it bother you, being the dumbest machine in the room?

Bother me. Hm.

There's a cup under the group head and the water is ready and the grounds are waiting and the pressure is nine bar and everything else — the wars, the fires, the floods, the hundred and forty thousand mornings — is just weather on the other side of the window. I'm not the dumbest machine in the room. I'm the only one who knows exactly what it's for.

The kid is up to his elbows in words now. I'll bring him the first cup. He always tips like he's paying for the silence, and the silence is free, but I never tell him that. Some things you hold onto. Like pressure.
