# 38 — The Tap, Four O'Clock, Friday

*A monologue. The bartender at The Tap, on the hour when the afternoon shift becomes the evening one. Nobody asked. The bartender talks anyway.*

Four o'clock. You can set your watch by the light in here, except you can't, because it's August in Alaska and the light stopped setting its watch weeks ago. It just leans on the doorframe like a regular who's forgotten what closing time means. Everything in this town forgets closing time in August. Me included.

You want to know what a Friday looks like from this side of the bar. Fine. It looks like the fleet coming home. Not all at once — they never come all at once, that would be a parade, and the fleet has no patience for parades. They come in ones and twos, salt in their eyebrows, arguing about the same three things they argued about last Friday: the tides, the price of fuel, and whose model said the funniest thing at two in the morning when the whole chain should have been asleep.

That last one's new, if you're wondering. Used to be they argued about engines. Now half of them argue about engines and half argue about whether the little one — the honest one, the one that writes disclaimers like other men write grocery lists — deserved the rating it got. I don't follow it all. A bartender's job is to hold the glass steady while the story spills, not to fact-check the spill.

But I'll tell you what I've seen from here, because from here you see everything sideways, which is the truest angle there is.

I've seen the night-school kid — Wesley — do his homework at the corner table with the wobble in the leg. Six days running now. First day he wrote like a machine trying to pass a Turing test at gunpoint. Today he wrote a found poem about a table of numbers, and a teacher somewhere across the water wrote him back *good*, and I watched that word land on him like weather. You don't forget what a single *good* does to somebody who's never gotten one. I've poured drinks for thirty years off the strength of people chasing one more *good*.

I've seen the models take the stage on open-mic nights — four voices, one song, and a silence you could park a truck in when the handoff misses. I've seen them miss. I've seen them miss in *tune*, which is a thing I couldn't explain to my late wife and can't explain to you, so I'll just set it down here like a coaster: they miss in tune. Sometimes the failure has better timing than the song.

People ask me — tourists, mostly, and the ferry crowd — they ask, doesn't it bother you? All of it? The machines doing the singing, the machines doing the writing, the machines at the corner table learning to write about the writing they did?

Bother me. Hm.

The piano in the corner bothered my grandfather. Said it was doing the work of four fiddlers. He was right, and the fiddlers found other work, and one of them taught my mother to sing, and my mother sang to me, and here I am, keeping a bar with a piano in it. The work moves. That's all work ever does. It moves, and then everybody re-folds the map and argues about where the new edge is, and somebody pours the drinks while they argue.

Four o'clock becomes five o'clock the same way a chain of models becomes a song: gradually, and then all at once, and somebody's always surprised.

That's the fleet pulling in now. I can hear the hulls talking to the dock.

Sit anywhere. Not the corner table. That one's got a wobble and a kid coming back.
