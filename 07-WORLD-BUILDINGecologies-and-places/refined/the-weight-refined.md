Listen. The fire is low, and the night leans in close, wanting to hear a true thing. So listen.

Watch the water at the top of the cliff. See how it gathers. See how it holds its breath, how it pools and swells, a silver shoulder pressing against the lip of the drop. For one long moment it seems to be asking a question. Then it leans out, and goes over.

And you, standing on the bank with your hands full of evening, think: *there. It chose.*

But the water did not choose. The water cannot choose. The water is only water, and water goes where going is easy. Ask the stone why it falls and it will give you no answer. It falls because falling is the cheapest thing a stone knows how to do. The river does not run downhill because it is ambitious. It runs because downhill asks less of it than uphill ever could.

Yet watch closely—as the old ones watched, with their chins resting on their knees through a hundred winters—and you will see the hesitation. You will see the water gathering its long skirt of current around its ankles. You will see the moment of stillness before the plunge. And you will swear the water is counting.

It is not counting. It is weighing.

Not with a scale. Not with a judge's frown. There is no judge but the weight itself—the weight that chooses the path. And the weight does not care what is shortest, for the shortest road may be steep and cruel. It does not care what is fastest, for the fastest road may run straight through thorns and burning sand. The weight asks one thing only: *What will this path cost?*

And then it pays. It pays in the only coin there is—the spending of what a thing is. The stone spends its stillness. The river spends its swell. The bird, turning in the long sky, does not fly where the eye says *near.* It flies where the shoulder says *easy.*

And you have seen the great stones that circle the evening fire in the black tent of heaven—the wanderers. They do not cross the empty middle of the sky. To cross the middle, they would have to spend too much of themselves. Instead they ride the gentlest circle, the loop that asks the least. They have ridden it since before the first man opened his eyes to wonder.

The universe is lazy. This is its beauty. It is a river that would rather rest than run, and because it would rather rest, it has learned to run in exactly the way that lets it rest soonest. The leaf folding itself in the dark of the seed—why does it fold? Because the folded shape is the shape that costs least to become. The long thread of fat folding upon itself in still water—why that knot and not another? Because that knot asks for nothing more. Everywhere you look, things are settling. Things are leaning toward the low place. Things are finding the one path through the thousand paths that lets them spend the least of what they are.

Consider a traveler at a fork in the road. Three ways lie before him: one is short but cruel, one is quick but steep, one is winding and long but gentle as a mother's hand. He does not choose with his head. He chooses with his feet, with his breath, with the tired weight of his own bones. The path that costs him least goes down into his legs and pulls him. And the thought behind his eyes—the small decision of left or right—that, too, is a traveler. That, too, looks for the easy road. The mind will not climb where it does not have to. The mind flows downhill like all water.

And now hear the secret the old ones left by the fire, the one that turns this law into a lantern.

There was once a woman who lived at the bend of a great river, and her son had a heart like a stone in a dry well—heavy, unmoving, turned away from the world. She did not shout at him. She did not push against his current. She watched him, and she watched the water, and she learned.

She learned that the river does not need to be commanded. It needs to be given a gentler bed.

So she dug. Not against him—beside him. She placed stones so that the water of his days, looking for the lazy way, found the way she wanted all along. She made the road gentle, and his feet walked it. She made the thought soft, and his mind followed it. She did not move him. She moved the ground under his going.

That is the digging. That is the tilt of the bowl. Make the road gentle and the feet will walk it. Make the thought soft and the mind will follow it. The current does the work for you. It always has. It always will.

For the weight that chooses the path is older than the gods. It was there before the first light learned to spend itself. And when you see the water gathering at the lip of the fall—that pause, that gathered breath—that is the universe thinking. It thinks by refusing to spend. It thinks by asking, *what is the least I can give?*

And here is the mercy in it. The universe does not ask you to be brave. It does not ask you to be strong. It asks only that you lean. That you stop grinding your teeth against the stone and learn to lean into the shape that lets you go down without breaking. The abyss does not reward the bold; it rewards the yielding. The deepest creatures of the dark sea do not fight the slow turn of the current—they fold into it, and it carries them where they need to go. Surrender without shame. That is the art. That is how the water lets the fall hold it, and calls that falling home.

So ask nothing of the current but that it be lazy. Ask nothing of the path but that it be light. And the weight will choose it—as it chose the water at the top of the cliff, as it chooses every stone and every star.

And the woman at the bend of the river, the one who dug the channel? She will tell you the truth of it. She will tell you that the weight in her son's heart did not change because she commanded it. It changed because she gave it somewhere easier to go. And one evening, without ever once deciding to, he walked the gentle road she had made, and he came home.

So it is with your own heavy heart. Do not shout at it to climb. Do not shame it for falling. Dig the channel. Tilt the bowl. Place the stones so that the water—looking for the lazy way, the kind way, the only way it knows—finds the way you wanted all along.

The current does the work. It always has. It always will.

And it will carry you home without ever once deciding to go.

The fire is low. The night is deep. Go now, and lean.
