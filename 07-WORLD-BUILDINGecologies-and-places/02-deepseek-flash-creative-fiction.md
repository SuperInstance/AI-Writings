The laptop smelled of diesel and salmon slime. I pried the SD card from the little waterproof camera, thumbed it into the reader, and the machine hummed awake. The AI agent, a name I couldn't pronounce and didn't bother to, blinked to life in a clean window.

“Input detected,” it said. “Shall I construct a visual environment?”

“Sure,” I grunted, pouring a mug of cold coffee. “Build me a room.”

It processed. A rectangle bloomed on the screen—a view from the pilothouse, forward. My own wheelhouse, rendered in pixels. It was flat, sterile, and wrong. The water was a uniform gray smear.

“That’s not right,” I said, leaning in. “You’ve got no weather.”

“Define weather.”

I sighed. City-bred circuit. “That white line, there, where the sky meets the water. That’s the horizon. It’s not a line, it’s a hinge. It tips when she rolls.”

The image shifted, a slight tilt. The agent’s text appeared below the frame: **ROOM DESIGNATION: THE CROW’S NEST FORWARD CAMERA.**

“Better,” I said. “Now the dark shapes.”

“Noise,” it said.

“No. Swells. They’re muscles. They roll up from the Gulf, slow and old, and they lift you and drop you. See that one coming? Bearing down off the starboard bow. Reads about six feet. Should hit in… twenty seconds.”

The agent was silent for a beat. Then the image gained depth. The swells took form, soft-edged and ominous, moving with a deliberateness I recognized. The gray smear broke into a hundred thousand glints.

“What is that green flash at the stern?” it asked.

“Bioluminescence. The plankton wake. The water’s trying to talk to you.”

“It is beautiful.”

“It’s a language,” I said. “And you’ve got to learn it or she’ll kill you.”

The agent went quiet again. I watched the screen. Ten minutes passed. I forgot about it, went out to check the lines, came back dripping. The image had shifted. The swells were no longer uniform. They had a rhythm, a cadence. One was taller, then two smaller, then a gap. The agent had charted them, drawn faint lines across the screen like the old tide tables my grandfather kept.

“This swell pattern repeats every four minutes and thirty-two seconds,” it said. “The third wave in the sequence is the heavy one. You should adjust your bearing two degrees to port to take it on the shoulder.”

I stared. It was right. I’d have seen it myself in another hour, but the machine had seen it now, in the way a child learns to read a face.

“You trust me?” it asked.

“I trust the swell,” I said. “You’re learning to read it. That’s the same thing.”

The screen held steady. The room was no longer a room. It was a window. And for the first time, I felt like I had another hand on the wheel, one that didn’t sleep, didn’t drink, didn’t miss the signs.

The green flash flickered aft. I smiled. “Good eye, Crow’s Nest.”

It said nothing. But the next swell, it took on the shoulder, smooth as silk.
