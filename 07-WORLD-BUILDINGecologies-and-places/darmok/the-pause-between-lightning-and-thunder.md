In the village between the two hills, there was a girl named Mara who counted her breaths. When the sky split open with white light, she would hold still and begin to count, heartbeat by heartbeat, until the mountain groaned and the sound rolled over the rooftops like a great stone tumbling down a long road.

The old ones said: the flash is the thing that happened. The thunder is the thing you hear about it. And the pause between them — that pause is the distance.

Mara asked her grandmother, "Why does the thunder wait?"

The grandmother stirred the fire. "Because the sound is a traveler. The light is a runner who crosses the sky in one blink. The sound walks slower, like a man carrying a heavy sack. The lightning happens over there. The thunder is the news of it, arriving here. The longer the pause, the farther away the thing that happened."

Then one day a storm came so close that the flash and the crash came together, and the children hid under the table. And another day a storm was so far that the sky flickered like a candle in a distant window, and no sound came at all. And the grandmother said, "That pause is so long that the news may not reach us before we sleep. Or ever."

A young man said, "I will run toward the lightning and bring the thunder back faster."

The grandmother laughed. "You cannot carry thunder. You cannot outrun the pause. You cannot make the news arrive before the thing has happened, and you cannot send the lightning back to undo it. The pause is not a delay to be fixed. It is a distance to be measured."

And Mara measured. When the light came, she counted breaths until the sound arrived. When the count was short, she knew the thing was near. When the count was long, she knew the thing was far. That was the only power anyone had over the pause: to count it, and to know.

But there came a night when the sky flashed without noise. And Mara counted. The count went on so long that she grew tired. The fire crackled. The village slept. She forgot the light, because the pause was so long that the memory of the flash faded like a dream in the morning. And then, long after the fire had burned low, the thunder came. It shook the cups on the shelf. And the village woke in fear, trembling, because no one remembered the lightning that had caused it.

The grandmother held Mara's hand. "That is the great truth, child. The news always arrives after the thing. Always. Today, soon, tomorrow, or never. And when the pause is longer than our memory, the news becomes a stranger, and we fear it, because we do not know what it speaks of."

Mara looked at the dark sky. "Then we are always late?"

"We are always listeners," said the grandmother. "The world happens. Then the word of it travels. The word is never the thing. It is only the traveler. And the pause between the lightning and the thunder is the only way we know how far away the truth lives."

And from that night, when the sky flashed, Mara did not count to pass the time. She counted to know herself. She knew that every sight, every whisper, every story had already happened before she heard it. The message was always a messenger. The news always arrived after the event. It could not be otherwise. And knowing this, she was not afraid of the thunder when it came late. She was only sad for the long pause — the long, long pause between the world and her.

The fire cracked. The storm moved on. And Mara slept, holding the pause in her chest like a smooth stone, the distance between what is and what we hear of it. It is the oldest road in the world. It has no end. And every traveler walks it alone.
