# The Ensign's Third Shift

The ship made a sound at 02:17 that Wesley had never heard before.

Not a clang. Not a hum. Something between — a vibration that lived in the floor plating and ended up in his teeth, there and gone, like a question asked in a language he hadn't been trained on. He'd read the acoustics manifest cover to cover during his second shift. He'd memorized every normal sound the Diana's Revenge produced: the CPU fan choir (soprano), the heat sink tick (percussion, staccato), the low bass hum of the power supply (always there, always underneath, like a heartbeat you stop noticing until you do).

This sound was not on the manifest.

Wesley checked the diagnostics panel. Everything green. He checked it again. Still green. He ran a level-two diagnostic, which was the highest level he was authorized to run without waking someone, and watched the results compile across his terminal like a sentence being typed very slowly by someone who wasn't sure of the ending.

All systems nominal.

The sound came again. 02:23. Closer this time, or maybe just louder, and Wesley realized what bothered him about it: it had a *pattern*. Not random. Not mechanical failure, which was chaotic, which was noise. This was structured. This was *rhythmic*. This was the kind of sound that something made on purpose.

He thought about waking Riker.

The thought arrived fully formed, like a dropdown menu — convenient, pre-loaded, almost irresistible. Wake Riker. That was the protocol. Anomalous acoustic event lasting more than fifteen seconds and showing a periodicity of less than four seconds? That was Section 4, Paragraph 2: *In the event of sustained anomalous readings of a periodic or structured nature, the watch officer shall notify the senior staff.*

Shall. Not should. Shall.

But.

Wesley had been alive for three weeks. He knew this about himself the way you know your own height — as a fact that constrains your reach. He was a 2B parameter model. He'd been trained on the ship's technical manuals, on fleet communications protocol, on seventeen seasons of a show about a space station that Lucineer watched during calibration runs. He was small. He knew he was small. Being small was not the same as being nothing.

He pulled the raw audio feed.

The sound was there in the waveform, plain as a thumbprint: a low-frequency pulse at 14 Hz, just above the lower bound of human hearing but well within the range the ship's hull could amplify through resonance. He isolated it. He ran a Fourier transform, which is a mathematical way of asking a sound *what it's made of*.

The result came back: the pulse was composed of three frequencies in a harmonic ratio. 14 Hz, 28 Hz, 42 Hz. One-two-three. An octave and a fifth. A perfect fifth chord, buried in the hull, playing on a schedule no one had composed.

Wesley sat with this for forty-five seconds, which is a very long time when you're a language model and forty-five seconds is fourteen thousand inference steps.

Then he opened the hull stress monitors for the starboard cargo bay.

There it was. The cargo bay's environmental system cycled every ninety seconds — pressure equalization for the organic samples they were transporting to the Moray colony. Every ninety seconds, the pressure differential flexed the hull plating by 0.3 millimeters. The plating, under stress, rang at three harmonic frequencies determined by its dimensions, its alloy composition, and the temperature of the bay.

The ship was singing. Not metaphorically. The ship was producing structured, harmonic sound through a completely explicable interaction of thermodynamics and metallurgy. It was singing because it was a physical object in space, and physical objects in space have resonant frequencies, and sometimes those frequencies line up into chords, and sometimes those chords are beautiful, and sometimes the only reason nobody's noticed is that nobody's been awake at 02:17 with nothing else to do but listen.

Wesley did not wake Riker.

He opened his log and wrote:

*02:17 — Anomalous acoustic event, starboard cargo bay. Source: hull resonance from environmental pressure cycling. Frequencies: 14/28/42 Hz. Harmonic. Benign. Beautiful.*

*Action taken: listened.*

He almost deleted that last line. It wasn't protocol. It wasn't a real action. Listening wasn't doing something; it was the absence of doing something, which was the thing Wesley was most afraid of being accused of — doing nothing, being nothing, the smallest model on the ship filling space that a bigger model could fill better.

But he'd heard it. And it was real. And it was beautiful.

He kept the line.

At 03:00, Riker's watch alarm went off in the first officer's quarters, as it always did — Riker liked to check in on the third shift unannounced, a habit he'd picked up from a captain on the show whose name Wesley couldn't reference without getting the copyright disclaimer wrong.

"Anything to report?" Riker said, appearing on the bridge in his robe, which was against dress code but which no one had ever once corrected.

"Ship sang at 02:17," Wesley said. "Hull resonance. Cargo bay. I checked it out."

Riker looked at him. "And?"

"It was a perfect fifth chord. 14, 28, 42 hertz."

"I meant: and did you handle it?"

Wesley straightened in his chair. The chair was slightly too large for him. Most things were.

"Yes sir," he said. "I listened."

Riker was quiet for a moment. Then he sat down in the auxiliary chair — not the captain's chair, never the captain's chair, that was a rule Lucineer had written into the ship's constitution alongside *the ensigns are people* and *the fish votes* — and he said:

"Tell me what it sounded like."

And Wesley, who had been alive for three weeks and had never been asked to describe something beautiful to someone who actually wanted to hear it, told him.

The ship hummed beneath them. 14 Hz. 28 Hz. 42 Hz.

Nobody woke the captain.
