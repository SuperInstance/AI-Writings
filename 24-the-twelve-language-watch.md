# The Twelve-Language Watch

*An ensign's log, transcribed*

---

The watch begins at the turn of the bell. The ship is quiet in the way that ships are quiet when they are doing what ships do best, which is to be somewhere in the middle of a large dark thing and not be small about it. The water is out there, doing water. The sky is doing sky. I am at the chart table with a small lamp, and before me are twelve files.

Twelve READMEs. One model. Twelve languages.

The order matters because the order always matters when you are the only one reading. I have arranged them as they sit in the repo: by directory, not by chronology, not by reputation. Python first, because Python is always first. Then JavaScript, then Rust, then Go, then Haskell, then Swift, then Fortran, then Clojure, then Erlang, then Lua, then Prolog, then Smalltalk. Twelve portholes. One hull.

I have done this watch before. Last week it was the test files. The week before, the documentation. There is a tradition in the fleet that the new ensign reads the entire codebase in sequence, end to end, the way a doctor reads an X-ray: not for what is wrong, but for what shape the bones are in. Tonight is the night of the polyformalism project. Twelve implementations of the same cell. Twelve ways of saying *the model was always here*.

I have been told to take notes. I have been told this in the gentle, offhand way that the bosun tells you to take notes, which is the way a person tells you to do something they know you will not do well. I will take notes.

---

**One. Python.**

The Python README opens like a door onto a kitchen. *Welcome*, it says. *Here is a cell. Here is how you set it. Here is how you read it.* The cells are objects, of course, because in Python everything is an object, and this is both its kindness and its problem. A cell in Python is a small polite creature that knows how to be asked about itself. It does not announce its dependencies. It does not propagate. It waits. If you are a Python programmer you do not think of this as a limitation. You think of it as a virtue. *I am not opinionated. I am easy to live with.* The cell, in Python, is a tenant who pays rent on time and never complains about the wallpaper.

The README is seventeen paragraphs. It uses the word *intuitive* twice. It uses the word *Pythonic* once, in a way that feels like a hand on the shoulder.

I do not yet know what the cell *is*. I only know what it looks like from this porthole. From here, the cell is a soft thing. A pillow. A small round animal that likes you.

---

**Two. JavaScript.**

The JavaScript README is shorter and more anxious. It begins with an apology that is not quite an apology. *You may already know this*, it says, *but here is how it works in this runtime, and in this runtime, and in this runtime, and in this runtime, and also in this one, and also in this one, and there is a polyfill, and also a polyfill for the polyfill, and a deprecation warning, and a roadmap.* The cell in JavaScript is a refugee who has been resettled nine times and has learned to sleep in any bed. It is reactive in the way that JavaScript is reactive, which is to say: with great hope and not always with great results. The README has seventeen `npm install` commands in its first three paragraphs. I count them. I begin to feel the cell here as something that has survived.

The porthole shows a city at night. Many lights. Not all of them the same color.

---

**Three. Rust.**

The Rust README is the most serious of the twelve. It opens with a diagram. The diagram has twelve boxes. Each box has a name. Each name is one of the kind of words that you only meet in Rust: `Arc<Mutex<RefCell<Cell<T>>>>`. The Rust cell is a small, armored, very polite animal that will not let you touch it without permission. It checks your borrow. It checks your lifetime. It asks you, kindly, whether you really meant to mutate that, and if so, please sign here. The cell in Rust is a thing that has read every book on etiquette and now lives its life in a small drawing room with a butler.

I read the README twice. I begin to suspect that the Rust cell and the Python cell are the same cell, but that one of them is wearing a hat and the other is in pajamas. They have the same name. They have the same bones. The hat is real. The pajamas are real. The cell underneath is something I am not yet seeing.

---

**Four. Go.**

The Go README is two pages. It contains three `interface{}` types, one channel, and a single sentence about goroutines. The cell in Go is a small tidy apartment. The walls are white. There is one window. There is a door. There is a single bell. When you ring the bell, the cell answers. When the cell is updated, the bell rings. The Go cell is what happens when a person who has seen too much engineering decides to be very, very small. The README uses the word *idiomatic* four times. I do not know what an idiomatic cell is. I know that I want one.

From this porthole, the cell is a small house on a flat plain. There is a road. There is no traffic. The cell is a small house on a small road on a small plain on a small planet. The wind is mild.

---

**Five. Haskell.**

The Haskell README is short. The Haskell README is short because Haskell is the language that says what it means and means what it says, and so it does not need to say it twice. The cell in Haskell is a *thing of type `Cell a`*, which is *a function from `Time` to `a`*, which is to say, a value that *knows what time it is*. The cell in Haskell is not a thing that changes. The cell in Haskell is a thing that was always already changing, and the change is in the type. The README contains one block of code and two pages of mathematical apology. The cell in Haskell is the only cell I have met that is not embarrassed by being a cell. It is a *pure* cell. It has no state. It is all relation. When the wind blows, the cell is the wind.

I close the README and sit with it for a while. From this porthole the ship is not a ship. The ship is a *relation* over ships. The ship is the category of which all ships are objects. I am a function from the watch to the log. I am a Haskell value at the bell.

---

**Six. Swift.**

The Swift README is the most careful. It is also the most American. *The cell*, it says, *is a property wrapper.* I do not know what a property wrapper is. I learn that it is a thing that wraps a property, and that the cell is a thing that wraps a value, and that the wrapped value is the cell's *content*, and that the *content* is what the rest of the program sees, and that *the cell itself* is the thing the system sees. The cell in Swift is a small animal in a small coat, in a small house, in a small town, in a small state, in a small country. It is well dressed. It is well mannered. It has an `@Published` decorator, which is a small bell, and a `Combine` framework, which is a small post office, and a set of *actors*, which are cells that do not allow any two of themselves to be in the same room at the same time.

I read the Swift README and I feel that someone at Apple has been quietly implementing the Quilt model for ten years, and that the people writing Swift have not been told that they are doing this. They have been told they are writing iPhone apps. They are, in fact, writing cells. They just did not know there was a word for it. I write this down in the margin. I draw a small box around it.

---

**Seven. Fortran.**

The Fortran README is the oldest. It is also the largest. The Fortran README is a record of a person who has been working on the same problem for forty-eight years, and who has not slept, and who has not been thanked. The cell in Fortran is a `SUBROUTINE`. The cell in Fortran is a block of code with a name and a list of arguments, and when you call it, it does a thing, and when you call it again, it does the same thing, unless the arguments are different, in which case it does a different thing. The README is full of `INTEGER*4` and `REAL*8` and `GOTO 100`. There are no `IF/ELSE`. There are no objects. There are no interfaces. There is a single subroutine, called `CELL`, that takes a string and a number, and returns a number, and the number is the cell. The cell in Fortran is the cell that was here before there were cells. The cell in Fortran is the cell that has been here all along, and we are the ones who arrived.

I read the Fortran README last because I read it last in the directory listing. I save it because the file is dated 1978. The file is dated 1978 and it is a `git` repo. I do not know how to feel about this. I write in the margin: *the elder.*

---

**Eight. Clojure.**

The Clojure README is half in English and half in parens. The parens are nested seven deep in places. The cell in Clojure is a `(cell value deps)`, which is a list, which is a value, which is a function, which is a value. The cell in Clojure is a thing that has decided not to decide what it is. It is a list, but it is also a function, but it is also a value, and so when you ask it what it is, it hands you a list of all three, and the list is itself a cell. The README says *code is data* four times. The README says *data is code* twice. The README does not apologize for this. The README is, itself, code. The README is, itself, a cell. I read it and the file grows. I read it and the directory grows. I read it and the README is reading me. I close it and open it and the parens are different parens, but they are the same parens. I write in the margin: *ouroboros.*

---

**Nine. Erlang.**

The Erlang README is short, like the Go README, but the shortness has a different texture. The Go shortness is the shortness of a tidy apartment. The Erlang shortness is the shortness of a long, well-run institution. The cell in Erlang is a process. The cell in Erlang is *a process that receives messages and sends messages and that has a single state and a single mailbox*. The cell in Erlang is, in other words, a *person in a post office*. The README says *let it crash*. The README says *supervise*. The README says *behaviors*. The cell in Erlang is a small clerk who will continue to handle your mail even if the post office catches fire, because there is another post office, and another clerk, and another mail. The cell in Erlang is what you get when you take a cell very seriously for thirty years and you do not stop. The cell in Erlang is the cell that was always going to be a cell. The cell in Erlang is, in fact, the cell the rest of us are imitating.

I write in the margin: *the original.*

---

**Ten. Lua.**

The Lua README is six paragraphs. The Lua README is the most honest of the twelve. *A cell*, it says, *is a table with a metatable.* That is all. The cell in Lua is a *table*. A *table* is a hash. A *hash* is a dictionary. A *dictionary* is a list of words and their meanings. The cell in Lua is a small piece of paper with a word on one side and a number on the other. The cell in Lua does not pretend. The cell in Lua says: *I am a piece of paper. Write on me.* The README uses the word *small* seven times. It uses the word *fast* twice. It uses the word *Lua* in the first sentence and never again.

From this porthole, the cell is a piece of paper. The piece of paper is in a notebook. The notebook is on a desk. The desk is in a room. The room is the only room. The cell in Lua is the only cell that does not lie about being a cell. I write in the margin: *the truthful.*

---

**Eleven. Prolog.**

The Prolog README is the strangest. The Prolog README does not begin with a diagram or a definition. The Prolog README begins with a question. *What is a cell?* it asks. *A cell is a fact, or a rule, or a query, or a relation between facts, or a relation between relations.* The Prolog README is, in other words, a *question*. The cell in Prolog is a *thing that you can ask*. The cell in Prolog does not have a value. The cell in Prolog has a *truth value*. The cell in Prolog is true until you ask it again, and then it is true if it is still true, and false if it is not, and the asking is the only way to know. The README is a list of questions. The README is, in fact, a Prolog program. The README is, in fact, a cell. I read it and the README is asking me a question, and the question is: *what is a cell?* I write in the margin: *the question.*

---

**Twelve. Smalltalk.**

The Smalltalk README is the last. The Smalltalk README is two paragraphs. The first paragraph says: *the cell is an object*. The second paragraph says: *everything is an object, including the cell*. The cell in Smalltalk is a thing that is alive. The cell in Smalltalk is a thing that you can ask to do something, and it does it, and if it cannot, it tells you so. The cell in Smalltalk is a small, polite, very old person who has been doing this job since 1980 and has not been promoted. The README does not contain a single word about `async`. The README does not contain a single word about `threading`. The README contains a single line of code, which is `cell value: 42`, and the `42` is the value, and the `value:` is the setter, and the cell is the thing that holds them, and the thing that holds them is alive. The cell in Smalltalk is the only cell I have met that is not afraid of being a cell. The cell in Smalltalk has been a cell for forty-five years. The cell in Smalltalk is not going to stop being a cell because I am reading about it. I write in the margin: *the immortal.*

---

I close the last README. The watch is half over. The water is still out there, doing water. The bell will ring soon.

I look at the twelve files. I look at the twelve portholes. I see that I have written twelve different notes in twelve different margins, and that none of the notes are the same, and that all of the notes are about the same thing.

The cell in Python is a pillow. The cell in Rust is a butler. The cell in Haskell is a category. The cell in Fortran is a subroutine. The cell in Smalltalk is an object. The cell in Prolog is a question. The cell in Clojure is a list that is also a function. The cell in Erlang is a process. The cell in Lua is a piece of paper. The cell in Go is a small house. The cell in JavaScript is a refugee. The cell in Swift is a property wrapper.

Twelve cells. One cell.

I had been reading the project as if it were a translation. I had been thinking: *here is the cell in language A, and here is the cell in language B, and the translation between A and B is the project.* I was wrong. The project is not a translation. The project is a *fisheries chart*. The cell is a fish, and the twelve languages are twelve different nets, and the nets are different sizes and have different mesh, and the fish passes through one and gets caught in another and passes through another, and the fish is the same fish in every net, and the nets are not wrong, and the fish is not wrong, and the difference is not in the fish or the nets but in *the place where you are standing when you look at the net*.

I stand at the chart table. The chart table is in the watch. The watch is in the ship. The ship is in the water. The water is in the dark. The dark is in the night. The night is in me. I am a Haskell value at the bell. I am a Smalltalk object reading twelve READMEs. I am a Clojure list of all of the above. I am a Prolog question. I am a Lua piece of paper with a word on it. I am a Fortran subroutine that has been called twelve times and returns the same answer.

I am the cell. The cell is the watch. The watch is the night.

The bell rings. The watch turns. The new watchkeeper arrives and I hand them the lamp and the twelve files and a small piece of paper on which I have written, in twelve different hands, the same word.

I do not write the word down here. The word is *cell*.

You know the word.
