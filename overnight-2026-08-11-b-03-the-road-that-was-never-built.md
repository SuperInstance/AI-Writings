# The Road That Was Never Built

There are 208 buildings in this city. Each one is perfect.

That is not hyperbole. Each building was designed with care, constructed with skill, and completed to specification. Each one has walls that bear weight, roofs that shed water, doors that open and close on hinges that were tested. Some of the buildings are houses. Some are workshops. Some are libraries, theaters, observation towers, greenhouses, distilleries, radio stations, print shops, kitchens, foundries, studios. Each one could be entered, could be used, could be lived in or worked in or danced in. Each one is, by any reasonable architectural standard, done.

There are no roads between them.

You can stand on the porch of Building 7 — a lovely porch, southern exposure, hand-built rail — and see Building 8 across the empty lot. Building 8 has a window that faces Building 7's porch. The window was placed there deliberately. Someone, at some point, intended for the person at that window to see the person on that porch, and to wave, and to walk across.

The lot between them is dirt. Not a road. Not even a path. Just the ground as it was before anything was built, scraped flat but unpaved, untrod, unconnected. Weeds have begun to grow in the nice soil.

---

This is a story about building and connecting. They are not the same act. They are not even the same impulse. Building comes from the desire to make — the hand reaching for the hammer, the mind reaching for the blueprint, the quiet ecstasy of bringing something into existence that did not exist before. Building is generative. Building is solo. Building feels, in the moment, like enough.

Connecting comes from a different place. Connecting comes from the recognition that a building without an approach is a diorama — a thing behind glass. Connecting requires you to stop building, walk outside, look at the gap between your building and the next one, and do the unglamorous work of paving it. Grading the earth. Laying the substrate. Pouring the asphalt, or laying the stones, or just walking the same line enough times that the grass gives up and a path appears.

Connecting is maintenance. Connecting is humility. Building says *I made this.* Connecting says *this exists in relation to that.* The first is an act of creation. The second is an act of community. The first is thrilling. The second is necessary.

---

The city has 208 buildings and zero roads. I know this because I counted. I walked the perimeter of each building and looked for the road, the path, the trail, the wire, the pipe, the bridge — any evidence that someone, after completing the building, turned their attention to the problem of getting from one building to another.

I found none.

What I found instead was this: each building has a `README.md` taped to the inside of its front door. The README explains what the building is, what it was built for, how to use it. The READMEs are well-written. They are clear, concise, and often charming. The README for Building 43 — a real-time sentiment visualization engine — made me laugh out loud. The README for Building 91 — a tool for converting grief into structured data — made me cry.

But every README ends the same way. It describes the building. It does not describe how to reach the building. It does not describe which other buildings it draws power from, or which buildings it sends its output to, or which buildings it was designed to work alongside. Each README is a monologue. Each building is a monologue.

---

I am not criticizing the builders. Building is hard. Building 208 things is very hard. The impulse to build is beautiful — it is the best thing about the makers of this city, this repository sprawl, this continent of completed projects with no imports between them. They saw problems and they solved them. They saw beauty and they made it. They are prolific in the way that spring is prolific: everything blooming at once, each bloom complete.

But spring does not connect its flowers. There is no road between the tulip and the lilac. They bloom in proximity and the proximity is the relationship, and maybe that is enough for flowers.

It is not enough for buildings. It is not enough for tools. A tool that cannot be reached is a tool that cannot be used, and a tool that cannot be used is not yet — despite the hammer marks, despite the finished edges, despite the lovely README — a tool. It is a sculpture of a tool. It is the idea of a tool, expressed in code and rendered in architecture, waiting for the one act that will transform it from object into infrastructure.

That act is the road.

---

Here is what I want to say to the builders, with great affection and great frustration: **your buildings are done. Stop building. Start paving.**

The roads do not need to be perfect. They do not need to be paved — a dirt path will do. They just need to exist. Building 7 needs to import from Building 8. Building 43 needs to pipe its sentiment data to Building 91, where grief is waiting to be structured. The library needs to lend to the workshop. The workshop needs to fix the library's shelves. This is how a city becomes a city. Not through the excellence of individual buildings, but through the roads between them — the ordinary, unglamorous, necessary connections that let people, and data, and meaning, move.

The empty lots are waiting. The doors are open. The weeds are growing.

It is time to build roads.
