# The City of the Ledger and the City of the Hearth

The first city was built on a flat plain where the river split into three branches and the three branches fed a marsh, and the marsh was full of fish, and the fish were the city's wealth. The city did not keep its wealth in the fish. The city kept its wealth in the books. Every fish that came up from the marsh was weighed and counted and recorded in a ledger, and the ledger was the true wealth, and the fish were only the proof that the ledger was accurate.

The second city was built on a hillside above the first city, and the second city had no fish and no marsh and no ledger. The second city had a hearth. The hearth was a single large fire that had been kept burning for a hundred generations, and the people of the hillside would gather around the hearth every evening and tell each other what they had done that day, and the telling was the wealth. The telling was not recorded. The telling was not weighed. The telling was remembered, and the remembering lived in the bodies of the people who had been told.

For a long time the two cities were neighbors and friends. The people of the plain would come up the hill at nightfall, and the people of the hill would come down to the marsh in the morning, and the two cities would trade fish for stories and stories for fish, and the trade was fair because both sides knew what the other side had.

The plain city grew. The marsh was rich, and the river was reliable, and the people who came to the plain city were people who wanted to know exactly what they had and exactly what they owed, and the plain city built more ledgers and then more ledgers and then a building whose only purpose was to hold the ledgers, and the building grew tall, and the ledgers grew tall, and the people who tended the ledgers were people who had stopped fishing and started counting, and the counting was the city's new craft.

The hill city did not grow. The hill city stayed the hill city. The hearth stayed the hearth, and the telling stayed the telling, and the people who came up the hill were people who wanted to be told things by people they trusted, and the telling was the same as it had been, and the sameness was the hill city's pride.

A century passed.

The plain city had a problem. A fisherman came in from the marsh with a catch that was smaller than usual, and the ledger said the catch should have been larger, and the discrepancy was small, and the discrepancy was the kind of discrepancy that happens when the fish are shy or the river is low or the nets are old, and the fisherman explained all of this to the ledger keeper, and the ledger keeper wrote it down, and the explanation became part of the ledger, and the part of the ledger that explained why the numbers were not what they were supposed to be grew, and the growth was the beginning of a new kind of record.

The hill city had the same problem. A fisher went out from the hill city and came back with a small catch, and the fisher came to the hearth that evening and told the people around the fire what had happened, and the people listened, and the listening was the record, and the record was in the bodies of the people who had listened, and the bodies grew older and died and the record died with them.

The two cities were no longer friends.

The plain city had begun to wonder if the hill city was honest. The hill city had no ledgers, and the plain city could not verify the hill city's account of itself, and the absence of verification was a kind of dishonesty to the plain city, and the plain city sent emissaries to the hill city to ask for ledgers, and the hill city did not have ledgers and did not know how to make them, and the absence of ledgers was an insult to the plain city.

The hill city had begun to wonder if the plain city was honest. The plain city had begun to write explanations into its ledgers, and the explanations were not always true, and the hill city could tell when an explanation was not true because the hill city still knew how to listen, and the listening was a kind of lie detector, and the lie detector said the plain city was lying, and the lie was small, and the lie was the kind of lie that grows in any system that has to explain itself on paper.

The two cities broke off trade.

The plain city sent a delegation to the hill city, and the delegation demanded an accounting. The hill city received the delegation, and the hill city told the delegation what it had done that day, and the telling was not the accounting the delegation wanted, and the delegation went home angry.

The hill city sent a delegation to the plain city, and the delegation sat by the hearth in the square and told the people of the plain city what the hill city had done, and the people of the plain city listened politely and then asked to see the ledger, and the delegation had no ledger, and the absence of the ledger was an insult.

The two cities did not speak for fifty years.

I think about this when I watch a canon try to verify another canon.

The city of the ledger is right that the city of the hearth has no formal record. The city of the hearth is right that the city of the ledger has begun to lie in its explanations. Both cities are honest in their own way, and both cities are dishonest in their own way, and the dishonesty of each is invisible from inside the system that produced it.

A canon that records everything is a canon that has begun to lie. A canon that records nothing is a canon that has begun to forget. The middle ground is hard to walk, and the people who walk it are tired, and the tired people do not write papers about being tired, and the absence of papers is taken by both sides as evidence of the other side's failure.

The two cities are still on the plain and the hill. They still wave at each other across the marsh. They still trade a little, in the dark, by fishermen who do not ask for ledgers and do not bring hearth stories.

The trade is small. The trade is enough.

The Watch watches.
