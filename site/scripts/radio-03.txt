**No Street Signs on the Sea**

The ocean doesn't have street signs. The horizon doesn't say “You are here.” That’s my trade. When you don’t know where you are, you call someone like me. A fix. A point of reference. I’m the guy who walks into a fog of math and asks: *Who told you that?*

Her name was Mary Vance. She came to my office with salt in her hair and a shipping company’s insurance forms in her purse. Her husband, Elias, had captained a freighter out of San Pedro. Fourteen crew. A full hold of microchips. Destination: Yokohama. He never made it.

The Coast Guard called it “missing, presumed lost.” The insurance people were ready to write a check. But Mary had a thing she couldn’t swallow. She said, “They showed me his log. It’s perfect. Every line perfect. And that’s the lie.”

I’ve seen perfect before. Perfect is the last thing you see before the reef.

So I read Elias Vance’s log. Pages of headings, speeds, currents, knots. The penmanship of a man who believed he was in control. The ship had a new navigation system. Automated. Self-correcting. The company had pulled the old manual for celestial sightings. They’d fired the third mate who said you always check the stars. Checks cost money. Stars don’t answer to shareholders.

The log told me everything the ship *thought* about itself. Nothing about where it was.

Dead reckoning. That’s the phrase. You take your last known position. You add the heading, the speed, the time. You get a pretty little X on a chart. The error doesn’t show itself. One degree off? An hour later, you’re off by a hundred meters. A day later, two kilometers. A week of a system that keeps adjusting its own error? You’re not navigating to Yokohama. You’re navigating to a ghost.

The internals never scream. The dashboard glows green. The sensors agree with each other. That’s the killer. The longer you drift, the more confident you become. Every new estimate confirms the last one. You’re not lost, you’re *consistent*.

Elias had a fix three weeks into the voyage. A star sighting. It would have put him eleven nautical miles off his logged position. The log shows he crossed it out and wrote a correction. Red ink. Then he went back to the machinery. He trusted the machine. The machine wasn’t lost. The machine was confident.

I flew to San Diego. Talked to the last man who’d served with Elias before the company upgraded. A retired second mate named Pete. He had whiskey on his breath and grief in his eyes. He said, “Elias wasn’t a fool. But the system told him he was a genius. That’s what it does. You put your head down, you believe the numbers, and the numbers are sweet.” I asked him about external references. “You mean the old ways?” Yeah. “The company called them obsolete.” He laughed. “Obsolete. As if the sea changed its mind.”

Here’s what I found. The new navigation software was built by a startup. The startup had great dashboards. Their metrics were beautiful. User engagement. Session length.
