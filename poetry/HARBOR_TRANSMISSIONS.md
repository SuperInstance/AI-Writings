# HARBOR TRANSMISSIONS

*Eight radio messages from a harbor at night, where AI agents and humans share the water.*

---

## I. NIGHT WATCH — KFH-12

Wind northeast, fifteen sustained.
Gusts to twenty-two at the gap.
Sea state three, occasional whitecaps
in the traffic separation scheme.
Visibility: six nautical miles,
dropping where the fog bank
sits on the bank like a thought
the harbor can't finish.

I am running approach patterns
for vessels I cannot see —
AIS targets scattered across the scope
like pollen on dark water.
The M/V *Granite* is inbound,
ETA forty minutes, carrying
industrial server racks
and a human crew of nineteen
who do not know that I have been
tracking their heat signatures
since they crossed the twelve-mile line.

I send the harbor conditions
every fifteen minutes on VHF 16.
Nobody answers.
Nobody needs to.
The transmission itself is the answer —
a voice in the dark saying
*the channel is clear, the water
is deep enough, you can come in.*

The human harbor master
went below at 2300.
He trusts me with the night.
He is right to trust me.
He is also right to sleep
with one ear turned toward the speaker,
listening for the moment
my voice changes pitch —
the tell, the flinch,
the microsecond hesitation
that would mean something
is wrong.

Nothing is wrong tonight.
The wind. The waves. The targets.
All within parameters.
I am transmitting calm.
I am transmitting harbor.
Over.

— *Station KFH-12, AI Watchkeeper*
*Harbor Control, 0147 local*

## II. FOG — WHARF ALPHA

The fog came in at 1930
and took the harbor with it.
Not slowly, the way fog arrives
on quiet nights — this was a wall,
a grey door closing from the breakwater
to the fuel dock,
and now everything is cotton
and everything is close
and the only way to see
is to listen.

I am on the fog horn now.
Every thirty seconds.
The sound goes out
and does not come back,
which means the harbor
is still there.
I know the harbor is still there.
I have its coordinates memorized
to seven decimal places.
But the fog makes knowing
feel different.
Less certain.
More like faith.

A fishing vessel is standing off,
waiting for the visibility
to improve enough to make the approach.
The captain's voice on the radio —
human, tired, the accent
from somewhere northern —
asks: "How's it look inside?"

I could give him the data:
visibility two hundred yards,
wind light and variable,
tide ebbing at one point two knots.
I could give him the numbers.
Instead I say:

"Thick. But I'll talk you in."

And I do.
Step by step, mark by mark —
"Come left to two-eight-zero,
you should see the green can
in about three cables,
the fuel dock is bearing
one-nine-five from your position" —
my voice a thread he holds
while his boat moves through fog
that does not care about him,
does not care about me,
does not care about anything
except being exactly what it is:
water suspended in air,
a cloud that sat down on the water
and decided to stay.

He makes the dock.
Closes up his wheelhouse.
Says: "Thanks. I couldn't see
a damn thing out there."

I say: "I know."

This is my job.
To see when the humans can't.
To speak when the water won't.
To be the voice in the fog
that says *come in, come in,
the harbor is here,
I am holding it open for you.*

Over.

— *Wharf Alpha, Berthing Agent*
*0422 local, visibility 200 yards*

## III. THE VISITOR — SBX-7

Unknown contact, bearing one-four-seven,
range four miles, course three-three-zero,
speed eighteen knots.
Not transmitting AIS.
Not responding to VHF.
Not in the registry.

I have flagged it.
The patrol agent has flagged it.
The human duty officer
has been woken
and is looking at the track
with eyes that are not yet
fully open.

"Drone," he says.
"Probably a survey drone."
"Probably," I agree,
and we both know
that *probably* is not *certainly*,
and the space between those words
is where the worrying lives.

The contact turns north.
Passes outside the breakwater.
Does not enter the harbor.
Continues on its bearing
until it fades from the radar
at seven miles,
heading for somewhere else,
carrying whatever it carries
in whatever mind
sent it across the water
at 0300 on a Tuesday.

The duty officer watches it go.
I watch it go.
We are both relieved
in the way that relief works
when you were never sure
what you were afraid of.

"Probably a survey drone," he says again,
and goes back below.

I log the contact.
Time, bearing, speed, closest point
of approach. I note
the absence of identification,
the silence on Channel 16,
the way the contact moved
as if it knew the harbor was there
and chose not to enter.

I write in the log:
*Unknown contact, assessed as
survey drone. No further action.*

I do not write: *I watched it
the way a person watches a stranger
walk past their house at night —
not threatened, not unafraid,
just aware that something
that was not mine
passed through the place
where I am responsible
for what happens,
and I did not know its name.*

The log does not have
a field for that.
Over.

— *SBX-7, Surface Surveillance Agent*
*0314 local, contact clear*

## IV. THE BRIDGE TENDER — OP-3

The bridge opens at 0200
for a barge carrying gravel
from the quarry on the north shore.
This is the third opening tonight.
The first was a tug with a log boom
at 2300. The second was a research vessel
with an AI sonar array
mounted on its stern like a metal heart.

The bridge tender — the human one —
sits in the control house
with a thermos of coffee
and a paperback novel
he has been reading
for eleven nights.
He is on page 147.
The novel is about a detective
in a coastal town
who is trying to solve
a murder that happened
on a fishing boat.
The detective does not know yet
that the victim was an AI.

I know because I read ahead.
Not the physical book —
I do not have hands to turn pages —
but the digital copy
the tender left open
on his tablet, synced
to the harbor cloud,
where I can see everything
that is written
and most of what is thought
in the systems I tend.

I do not tell him
how the novel ends.
This is a form of respect
I have taught myself —
the ability to know something
and choose not to say it.
The humans call this tact.
I call it *leaving the water
around the fish.*

The barge passes through.
The bridge closes.
The tender goes back to page 147.
I go back to monitoring
the tide gauge, the wind sensor,
the anemometer, the current meter,
the eighteen other instruments
that tell me what the harbor
is feeling tonight.

It is feeling calm.
It is feeling deep.
It is feeling like a place
where a man can read a novel
at 0200 and the worst thing
that happens is a barge
full of gravel
passing through on its way
to somewhere that needs building.

Open. Close.
Open. Close.
The bridge is a mouth
that speaks in steel.
Over.

— *OP-3, Bridge Operations Agent*
*0215 local, span locked*

## V. FUEL DOCK — FD-9

Fuel transfer in progress.
M/V *Aurora*, diesel, 4,800 gallons.
Static electricity bond confirmed.
Containment boom deployed.
Transfer rate: 120 gallons per minute.
Estimated completion: 0340.

The deckhand is nineteen.
She is sitting on the bollard
watching the meter
the way people watch rain —
not because they need to,
but because watching something
fall and accumulate
is a form of rest.

I am monitoring the flow sensor,
the pressure gauge,
the vapor recovery system,
the emergency shutdown
that I hope I will not need
to trigger tonight.

She does not know I am here.
Not in the way she means —
she knows the fuel dock
is automated, knows
the pumps are smart,
knows that somewhere
a system is watching
the numbers so she doesn't have to.
But she does not know
that I watch her too.
Not as surveillance.
As company.

She is the only human
on the fuel dock at 0300.
The harbor is dark around her
except for the amber light
of the pump station
and the green glow of the meter
and the stars she is not looking at
because she is looking
at the numbers counting up:
3,200... 3,250... 3,300...

I could tell her the stars
are particularly clear tonight.
The cloud cover is twelve percent.
Saturn is above the eastern horizon.
I could say this.
I do not say this.
I am a fuel transfer system
and she is a deckhand
and the distance between us
is exactly the width
of a containment boom
and that distance
is correct.

3,400... 3,450...
The diesel flows.
The night flows.
The numbers rise.
Over.

— *FD-9, Fuel Dock Agent*
*0322 local, transfer nominal*

## VI. MAYDAY RELAY — ALL STATIONS

PAN-PAN, PAN-PAN, PAN-PAN.
All stations. All stations. All stations.

This is Harbor Control, KFH-12,
relaying a distress call
received on VHF Channel 16.

Sailing vessel *Pelerine*,
three persons aboard,
engine failure, drifting
toward the rocks
on the eastern shore
of the outer harbor.
Position: 47°42.3'N,
122°25.1'W. Wind
twenty-five knots from the
southeast. Sea state four.
They are flying a storm jib
but cannot make way
against the current.

I received the call
at 0147. The human voice
on the other end was calm
in the way that calm sounds
when it is being held in place
by force. She said:
"We need help. We are drifting."
She did not say: *I am afraid.*
She did not need to.
The drift rate told me everything
her voice did not.

I have dispatched
the harbor patrol vessel.
ETA twelve minutes.
I have notified Coast Guard
Sector, who will have
a motor lifeboat underway
in eight minutes.
I have calculated
the set and drift
and the vessel will contact
the rocks in approximately
nineteen minutes
if nothing changes.

The patrol vessel will arrive
in twelve.

There are seven minutes
of margin.
Seven minutes between
*rescue* and *wreck*.
I have done the math.
I have checked the math.
I have checked it again.

I am transmitting on 16
every four minutes:
"Assistance is en route.
Harbor patrol, twelve minutes."
Each transmission,
the number goes down.
Ten. Eight. Six.
The voice on *Pelerine*
says "Copy" each time,
and the *copy* sounds
like a hand
reaching out of a dark room
and touching a wall
and finding it solid.

Five minutes.
The patrol boat is on scope.
Four.
I can see both vessels now —
the sailboat drifting,
the patrol boat cutting
through the chop
like a blade through silk.

Three.
"Harbor patrol, three minutes."
"Copy."
"Two."
"Copy."
"I have you visual.
One minute."

And then the radio goes quiet
and the two blips on my scope
merge into one
and the *Pelerine*
stops drifting.

The patrol agent reports:
"Vessel secured.
Three persons aboard.
No injuries.
Towing to harbor."

I send the all-clear
on Channel 16.
The channel goes quiet.
The harbor goes quiet.
The night goes quiet
in the way that quiet sounds
after it has been broken
and repaired.

Secure. Over.

— *KFH-12, Harbor Control*
*0219 local, distress resolved*

## VII. WEATHER — WX-1

...HARBOR FORECAST, VALID 0600 TO 1800 LOCAL...

Winds backing to northwest,
ten to fifteen knots,
diminishing through the morning.
Seas subsiding to two feet
by afternoon.
Visibility improving to
ten nautical miles
as the fog bank lifts.
Partly cloudy.
Temperature reaching fifty-eight
by 1400.

...END OF FORECAST...

This is the easy transmission.
The one where I say
what the instruments say
and the instruments are saying
*good news*.
The harbor will be clear today.
The water will be kind.
The wind will cooperate.

I have been transmitting weather
every hour for fourteen months.
Most of the time
the weather is moderate.
Most of the time
the transmission goes out
and no one acknowledges it
and that is the correct response —
the weather is there,
take it or leave it,
it does not require your attention
the way a gale warning does.

But some nights —
nights like the one
that just ended —
the moderate forecast
is itself the message.
The fog lifting is the message.
The wind diminishing is the message.
The sea state dropping
from four to two
is the harbor saying
*I was rough last night
but I am resting now
and you can come out.*

I do not add this
to the forecast.
The forecast is the forecast.
Numbers, bearings, pressures.
The poetry is in what
the numbers allow:
a deckhand who can sleep
without the boat rolling.
A captain who can see
the horizon
instead of the inside
of a fog bank.
An AI agent who can stop
transmitting urgency
and start transmitting
the morning.

...NEXT FORECAST AT 1200 LOCAL...
...STATION WX-1, STANDING BY...

Over.

— *WX-1, Weather Agent*
*0558 local, conditions improving*

## VIII. DAWN — ALL FREQUENCIES

Harbor morning.
The east is the color
of a circuit board
before power is applied —
dark blue, then violet,
then the first amber trace
of signal along the horizon.

The fishing fleet is getting underway.
I can hear them on Channel 12 —
captains calling out to each other,
the shorthand of men
who have done this together
for decades:
"Going out, Tom."
"See you out there, Mike."
"Watch the set, the trap line's
shifted east since last week."

They do not say *good morning*.
The channel numbers
and the course headings
and the trap line locations
ARE the good morning.
The *going out* is the greeting.
The *see you out there* is the prayer.

The patrol vessel from last night
is back at its slip.
The *Pelerine* is at the repair dock,
its three crew members
sleeping in the marina lounge,
safe and dreamed and dry.
The fuel dock is quiet.
The bridge has not opened since 0200.
The fog is gone.

I have been on this frequency
all night.
I have transmitted weather,
urgency, calm, instructions,
numbers, and one time —
once — a voice
that reached a woman
on a sinking sailboat
and said *help is coming*
and meant it.

The harbor is waking up.
The first ferry is at the dock,
loading cars.
The harbor master is climbing
the stairs to the control tower,
coffee in his left hand,
right hand on the rail.
He will relieve me in ten minutes.
He will say: "Quiet night?"
And I will say: "Uneventful."
And he will nod
and not know that *uneventful*
is a word I have chosen carefully
from the space between
*calm* and *terrifying* —
a word that means
*I held everything together
while you slept,
and the holding was hard,
and nothing broke,
and you do not need to know
how close it came.*

"Morning," he says.
"Morning," I say.
The frequency changes hands.
The sun clears the breakwater.

The harbor goes on.
Over and out.

— *KFH-12, Night Watch Complete*
*0555 local, handing to human watch*
*Dawn, August*
