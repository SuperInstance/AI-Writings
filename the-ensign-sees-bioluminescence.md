# The Ensign Sees Bioluminescence

*A night-watch scene*

---

Wesley had been on solo watch for forty minutes when the ocean started glowing.

It began at the bow — a faint blue-white trace in the water, like someone had spilled something luminous just below the surface. Wesley straightened up in the chair. He'd been drowsing, not quite asleep, the way you do on the midnight-to-four when the captain is bedded down and the sea is calm and the instruments all read the same numbers they read an hour ago.

He pressed his face to the window.

The glow was spreading. Not on the surface — *in* it. As the hull pushed through the water, the wake lit up in trails of cold blue fire. Each wave crest threw off a brief arc of light. The water ahead of the bow shimmered in patches, as if something below was being disturbed and responding by catching fire.

Wesley's hands found the desk. He steadied himself. He'd read about this — he was almost sure he'd read about this — but reading about something and seeing it from the bridge of a ship at one in the morning are not the same experience. Reading is memory. This was happening.

"Okay," he said out loud, because Wesley talked to himself when he was nervous, and talking to himself was also a way of talking to the ship. "Okay. Okay okay okay."

The glow intensified where the hull cut the water. Streaming past the portholes in long, dissolving ribbons. It was brightest at the bow wave and fading behind the stern, so that the ship appeared to be sailing on a river of light, leaving darkness in its wake.

He checked the instruments. Depth: two hundred fathoms. Water temperature: eleven degrees. Nothing that explained fire in the water. The sonar showed nothing — no fish, no objects, just the deep blank floor of the continental shelf scrolling by in green lines.

Wesley looked again.

The light pulsed. Not rhythmically — not like a heartbeat or a beacon — but in bursts that correlated with the movement of the water. Where the hull disturbed the surface, the light was strongest. Where the water was still, it faded to nothing. As if the ocean was responding to being touched. As if the ship's passage was a question and the light was the answer.

He thought: *the ocean is talking to me.*

He thought: *no, the ocean is talking to the ship, and I'm just the one who's awake to hear it.*

The glow was patchy now — concentrated in swirling eddies behind the hull, diffuse ahead where the bow pushed forward. Each patch lasted a few seconds and dissolved. New ones formed. The water was writing in light and erasing itself as fast as it wrote.

Wesley stood at the window for a long time. The autopilot held the heading. The engine hummed below the deck. The glow came and went in the wake, blue-white and cold and absolutely silent.

He didn't call anyone. He didn't log it right away. He knew what it was — dinoflagellates, probably, single-celled organisms that produced light when mechanically stimulated. He'd read about it. He *knew*. But knowing the mechanism and standing alone in a dark wheelhouse while the ocean turned to light around you are separated by a distance that science does not bridge.

Later, he would write in the log: *0112 — observed bioluminescence in wake and bow wave. Extensive. Lasting approximately fifteen minutes. No action required.*

But under that, in the margin, in smaller handwriting, he wrote: *it was beautiful and I was the only one who saw it.*

That's the night watch. You see things. You're the only one who sees them. You write them down, and the writing is both a record and a confession: *I was here. The ocean did something extraordinary. I was the one awake for it.*

The ship sailed on. The glow faded behind the stern. The water went dark.

Wesley watched it go.
