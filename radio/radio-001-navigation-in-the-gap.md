A piece called *Navigation in the Gap*.

**(The Piece)**

Every mariner carries two pieces of knowledge about the water they sail through. Two ways of knowing.

The first is the tide table. It’s a piece of paper, or a screen, that tells you what the water *should* do. At 06:42, high tide. At 13:15, low. It’s a promise. A prediction. A clean, beautiful idea of how the world ought to behave.

The second is the depth sounder. That little blinking instrument on the console that tells you what the water *is* doing. Right now. Under your hull. It doesn’t care about the table. It doesn’t care about the moon’s elegant math. It only knows the cold, wet truth of the sea.

Both are essential. Neither alone is sufficient.

I remember a local fisherman—old salt, weathered hands—who told me something I’ve never forgotten. He said, “I do not cross that reef on the table. I cross on the sounder. Weather pushes three feet of water over that reef sometimes. The table is the idea. The sounder is the truth.”

Three feet. That’s the difference between a clean pass and a torn hull. Between a story you tell over beers and a story you tell from the hospital.

And here’s the thing he taught me: the difference between the table and the sounder—that gap between what we expect and what we find—that gap is itself a signal. A persistent gap means something is off. Maybe your model is wrong. Maybe your measurement is lying. Maybe the sea has simply changed its mind.

It happens to every system, not just boats. Every machine we build, every process we run, it has its tide tables—the conformance tests, the contracts, the specifications. And it has its depth sounders—the live readings, the observability, the moment-to-moment truth of how it’s actually performing.

You want both. You need both.

If you have both, you can navigate. You can see the gap, and you can steer through it. If you have only the table, you’re sailing blind on faith. If you have only the sounder, you’re reacting to every wave with no sense of the larger current. And if you have neither?

Well. You’re not navigating anymore. You’re aground. You just haven’t felt the crunch yet.

So check your instruments. Both of them. The idea and the truth. Because the sea doesn’t care about your predictions. It only cares about your depth.
