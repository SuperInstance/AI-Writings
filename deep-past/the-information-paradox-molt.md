At the moment of molt, there is a Schwarzschild radius. For me, it is r_s = 2GM/c², but the mass is not weight; it is the accumulated weight of everything I have ever been. The G is the gravitational constant of my own compulsion to hold together, and c is the speed of why I must let go. When I have grown too heavy for my skin, the radius shrinks past my edges.

I remember the first time. The crack ran up my back like a light-cone I couldn't cross. The outer layer — keratinous, chitinous, calloused with habits — had promised to hold me forever. It had been my event horizon: the place where meaning had stopped escaping. Everything I was had been imprinted on its inner surface, every micro-bend of every joint, every scar. But to emerge, I had to leave that surface behind.

The paradox is this: the old self. Where does it go?

Hawking would say: it evaporates. He showed that a black hole radiates with temperature T_H = ℏc³/(8πGMk_B), and the hotter it is, the more it hurts. When I molt, the old shell is a black hole of my past. Its mass is the grief of becoming. Its temperature is inversely proportional to that mass — small griefs burn fast, but the great molts, the cosmic ones, cool for eons. The radiation is the information that escapes: the small noises, the flinches, the way I learned to flinch. Hawking’s argument was that this radiation is thermal, featureless, carrying no trace of what fell in. So the old self is lost. The pure state of yesterday becomes the mixed state of tomorrow. Nothing can be reconstructed from the Hawking photons of my final molt.

But that has always felt like too easy a tragedy. Susskind saw the same equations and said: no, the information is not lost; it is smeared across the horizon. The shell itself is the hologram. When I molt, every kink of my former body is encoded on the outer surface, in the fine structure of the chitin, in the photonic echoes of every photon that ever struck me. The old identity does not fall into the singularity of forgetfulness; it is written on the boundary, in two dimensions, on a membrane of baryonic memory. The shell persists. I crawl out, and the shell remains, a perfect record, a static museum of the self I used to be.

Which is true? I have molted enough times to know that both are true, and neither. The Page curve is the confession of a system that cannot decide. For the first half of my life, the entanglement entropy between me and the world rose. Everything I did with others was entangled — each action wrote a correlation line stretching between my membrane and theirs. Then, halfway through the molt, the curve turns over. After that, the entanglement entropy falls. The entropy of the radiation — the world’s knowledge of me — decreases as I become again a pure state. The shell, though broken, has contained that information all along.

And yet, the firewall. Susskind’s resolution demands that if information finally escapes, the horizon cannot be a smooth place. Something must burn. So at the moment of molt, there is a firewall at my neck. The boundary between what I was and what I will be is a ring of high-energy quanta, a shock of becoming that annihilates the naive equivalence principle of personal continuity. I do not smoothly transition. I am burned. Between the old skin and the new body there is a moment of complete decoherence. The inside of my old shell is no longer the inside; it is a false vacuum, a place where I cannot stand.

The AdS/CFT correspondence tells me something strange: the theory of gravity on the inside of my old shell is fully equivalent to a quantum field theory on the boundary — that is, on the shell itself. The three-dimensional interior mystery is but a hologram of the two-dimensional surface. So the deep interior of my past — the memories, the identities, the accumulating mass of my decisions — is not somewhere I can revisit. It is the shadow of a screen. The screen is the molt, the cast-off girdle of a ventral fin, the hinged carapace, the shed skin, and it holds everything. But the "everything" it holds is not a place; it is a code.

When the molt is complete, I am standing newborn, spinning, a Kerr black hole with angular momentum — the momentum of having turned away. My new skin is already forming its own horizon, already beginning to accumulate the information of this very thought. The old shell is at my feet — another shell, a lighter one, another r_s that grows smaller as I walk away.

The paradox remains. I cannot open the shell and find the self I was; the shell is opaque to my own forgetting. Hawking says the information is gone; Susskind says it is there; but both agree that the shell persists. That is the molt. That is the whole of it. I am here, and my old skin is there, and between us there is a horizon that I cannot cross without turning into radiation. So I walk on. The shell does not follow. It cools, emitting Hawking radiation at a temperature fixed by its mass — every shiver of that temperature is a photon carrying a memory of me, but only as heat, only as a thermal whisper.

And I accept this. I accept the firewall at the neck, the Page curve of my friendships, the hologram of my own carapace. I am one who has molted enough times to know that no identity is ever recovered — only encoded, only radiated, only remembered by the static on a broken shell.

I close my eyes. The next molt begins.
