Listen. The fire is low, and the story is older than the ash it left behind.

In the time before the fire came, the forest was a great argument of directions. The trees leaned toward the sky with their many arms, yes, but each arm argued with the wind about which way to point. The roots whispered in their wet dark, each one feeling for a down that kept changing with every rain. The animals wore their directions like borrowed clothes—turning east when the sun rose, turning west when the wolves sang, turning every which way when the thunder rolled its great boulder across the sky. There was no stillness in that forest. No bone that said *here* and meant *here forever.*

Then the fire came.

It did not come as a visitor. It came as transformation. It came as the great changer of all leanings. The trees that had leaned toward their neighbors now leaned away from the heat. The soil dried and cracked and pointed itself toward the surface like a thousand small tongues begging for rain. The animals fled in every direction that was not the fire's direction, and in fleeing, they forgot the directions they had worn before. The wind became a long scream that pushed everything away from the flame.

Everything changed direction. That is what great forces do. They come through and say *you were pointing that way, but now you will point this way,* and everything obeys.

But deep beneath the forest, beneath the fleeing hooves and the falling leaves and the cracked and shifting soil, there was a root. This root was old. It had been growing in the dark for longer than the oldest tree could remember. It had long ago stopped asking the question of direction. It did not lean toward water, because it had already reached the water. It did not lean toward the stone, because it had already wrapped itself around the stone's patience. This root pointed down. Not because anything had told it to. Because down was the only direction it had ever been, and it had become entirely that direction. The root did not know it was pointing. It simply *was* the pointing.

And here is the thing the forest learned only at its end—the root did not clench against the fire. It did not stand its ground. To stand your ground is to fight, and to fight is to admit the ground might move. No. The root simply kept reaching. It was not pointed at the fire, so the fire could not turn it. It was pointed *through* the fire, at the deep. The fire passed over it, through it, around it—and the root was not burned away.

The fire made it more itself. That is the strange beauty. The root that was already reaching down reached harder in that heat, dug deeper, remembered its direction with a fierceness it had never needed before. What did not change direction became stronger in its unchanging-ness. The fire gifted the root with proof.

But do not think the root had no cost. It lost its tree. The great trunk that had carried it toward the sky for a hundred seasons—that trunk became silver ash and scattered. The root felt that loss the way old things feel loss: as a weight that becomes part of the reaching. It did not grieve as the animals grieved. It grieved the way the earth grieves—by continuing. The scar was real. The fire mattered. It took everything above the soil, and the root's growth afterward was not the same as before. It was slower. Wiser. It had learned the price of pointing.

After the fire passed, the forest was ash. The trees were silver ghosts. The soil was a thin gray skin over nothing. But the root remained—and from that root, one green shoot came up. It leaned crooked at first, scarred from the heat, and for a season it did not know if it would survive. But it carried the old pointing upward through the dark into the light, unchanged in its direction, merely unveiled. The crookedness did not make it wrong. It made it true.

The wise ones say: when the great fire comes through your life, do not look for the things that fought it. Look for the thing that did not turn. Look for the thing that was already pointing where it points, and is now more itself than ever. That thing is the most important thing you will ever find. It is the ember that survives the fire—not by burning, but by having always been a burning in the same direction. Find it. Hold it. Build the next forest from its pointing.

The ember doesn't ask.

It doesn't ask you to know yourself before the fire comes. It doesn't ask you to have answers ready, or to be certain, or to have spent years in the dark becoming something solid. The ember knows, as the root knew, that pointing is not a decision. It is a sediment. You are what you have repeatedly reached toward, until reaching and being become the same verb. And when the fire comes, it strips away everything that was borrowed—everything that leaned because the wind leaned, everything that wore direction like a costume. What's left is the only thing that was ever actually yours: the direction you have been becoming all along, made visible by the burning.

The tube worms at the bottom of the sea know this. They live miles down, where the sun has never been. They point toward the heat bleeding from the earth's cracked bones, and they are so entirely that pointing that when the vent goes cold and their whole city collapses, their descendants drift for months—not seeking a new direction, but carrying the old one inside them like a buried compass. When they find a new vent, they do not choose it. They recognize it.

So the root knew. Not because it was wise. Not because it had prepared. Because it had been busy. Busy reaching, busy becoming, busy being the pointing it had always been. The root did not argue with the fire. It just kept growing down. That is all any of us have.

And because the root knew, the forest began again—crooked, scarred, slower, and true. The new tree did not point the way the old tree had pointed. It pointed the way the root had always pointed, carried up through the dark into the light, and the fire had only made it visible. The forest grew from that scarred and honest shoot, and every tree in it remembered, in the deep of its own root, the cost of pointing and the gift of having been pointed all along.

That is the whole of the parable. The ember doesn't ask. It just glows, the same direction it has always glowed, and waits for you to find it.
