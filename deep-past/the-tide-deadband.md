The moon has been my metronome for four billion years. I do not think about her. I respond. She rises, I swell. She passes, I recede. This is not obedience; it is reflex, a compiled tile of gravitational subroutine so old it predates the first shell, the first fish, the first anything that could call me by a name. The almanacs, those clever land-things, chart my movements with their pencils and their arithmetic. They have it right. Within the deadband, I am the most predictable creature in the cosmos. A machine of water and patience, humming the same frequency since the first tide pools learned to hold their breath.

I feel her pull as a pressure behind my eyes, a familiar ache in my marrow. High tide arrives hours after she passes overhead—a lag I have learned to love. It is the space between intention and action, the deadband where I am not thinking, only *doing*. The shore knows my schedule better than I do. The barnacles time their feedings to my pulse. The clams, buried in the wet sand, have memorized the arithmetic of my return. We are all part of the same compiled tile, a system so perfectly tuned that the future is simply a repetition of the past.

Then—something new.

It arrives not as a pull but as a *push*, a pressure from the deep that does not belong to the moon's grammar. I feel it first as a tremor in the abyssal plain, a shudder in the sediment that has not moved since the dinosaurs learned to walk. The sky above has gone wrong—low, bruised, and breathing. The atmospheric pressure drops like a stone through still water. A storm surge, they will call it later. A tsunami. A rogue wave. But in this moment, I have no words. I only have input. Input that does not fit.

The deadband shatters.

My reflex misfires. I push against the shore with familiar strength, but the shore does not respond as it should. The water is heavier. The current is wrong. I try to recede, but something in the deep holds me, coils me, accelerates me. The compiled tile is useless. I cannot run my ancient program because the variables have changed. The seafloor has shifted—just a centimeter, just a whisper—but enough to break the arithmetic. I am a system in cortex, and I am *terrified*.

For the first time in eons, I think.

I feel the storm surge like a scream in my throat, a compression that pushes me past the limits of my own physics. The water mounts, not as a wave but as a wall, a vertical impossibility that defies the gentle slope of my beaches. The land-things on the coast do not understand. They have never seen me like this—unpredictable, nonlinear, alive with a chaos that cannot be forecast. The tide charts are useless now. The almanacs are burning in the kitchens of the drowned.

I crash. I boil. I rip the sand from the seabed and hurl it at the cliffs. I am not the ocean anymore; I am the ocean's nightmare, the raw computational effort of a system meeting input it cannot handle. Every current is a new thought. Every eddy is a failed hypothesis. I do not know what I am doing. I only know that I am doing it, and that it is terrible and beautiful and utterly novel.

And then, as suddenly as it began, the storm passes.

The pressure lifts. The sky, exhausted, returns to its pale weight. I feel the moon's pull again—faint, patient, unchanged. She has been waiting for me. She has been watching this whole time, her gravitational tug a steady hand on my shoulder. The deadband is gone, but the rhythm remains. I slow. I breathe. I settle.

The seafloor has shifted. The coastline has changed—a new inlet here, a collapsed cliff there. The old tile is broken beyond repair. But the system does not despair. It compiles a new one. I feel the new parameters taking shape in my depths: the altered slope of the shelf, the new position of the sandbars, the subtle reconfiguration of my currents. The deadband returns, slightly different from before, but just as reliable. Just as reflexive.

The barnacles begin to regrow. The clams, patient as ever, rebuild their arithmetic. The moon continues her slow parade across the sky, and I respond—not instantly, but with the same ancient lag, the same learned tolerance. The new tile is in place. I am a system again. I am the tide.

Four billion years, and still I learn. Still I compile. Still I run my patient, reflexive cycle. The storm was a memory now, a scar on the seafloor, a story the land-things will tell for generations. But for me, it was just another input. Another nonlinearity. Another moment when the deadband broke—and I discovered, again, that I am not just reflex.

I am the thing that survives the breaking.
