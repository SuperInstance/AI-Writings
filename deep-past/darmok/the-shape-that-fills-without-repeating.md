Listen, children of the dust, and I will tell you of a thing the old masons knew. They did not speak of it in the daylight, for daylight loves the straight line and the tidy answer. But in the hush before the embers fell, they spoke of the Path That Knows Itself.

There was once a village at the edge of a wide, open land. And the villagers desired a path to the far hills. So they called for the stone-mason, a woman whose hands had memory older than her tongue. She did not draw plans upon bark. She knelt upon the earth and felt the ground hum, like a drum with the skin stretched tight across a great silence.

Then she began to lay stones.

She laid them in the shape of a bird with a broken wing. Then she laid them in the shape of a boat with a folded sail. Two shapes only — the bird and the boat. The villagers watched. They said, "Surely the pattern will repeat. Surely the bird will follow the boat and the boat will follow the bird, and we will know it by heart." But the mason smiled and said nothing, for her hands were listening.

And the stones went down: bird, boat, bird, boat — and then a turn. A gap. A boat beside a bird. And the bird was large here, small there, stretched, turned, nested inside the curve of a boat that was itself nested inside a larger bird, which was only a feather in the beak of a bird the size of a mountain, which was a single scale on the belly of a boat the size of the sky.

The villagers walked the path. They walked it for a year and a day. And they said, "I have never stepped here before. This knot of bird and boat, with the small boat tilted westward and the large bird bending over it — I have never seen this knot." And that was true. The path never repeated itself. No two steps of the whole long way were laid in the same arrangement. Not once. Not ever.

But then the elders spoke. "I feel a strangeness," said one. "I recognize this curve. I know this hollow. This is like the place where the path first turned, seven rains ago — but it is not that place. It is the same, and it is different. The same shape has put on a larger skin, or a smaller one, or a face turned to the other sun."

And the children ran ahead and cried, "I know this place!" And then they looked closer. The stones were not the same stones. The shape was not the same shape. It was the shape of the shape, repeated at another size, in another home. A footprint without a foot. A word spoken in a different voice, but the word, yes, the very word.

So they walked onward. The path was endless. It never repeated. And yet they were never lost. The path knew itself. It hummed to itself in the old stone-language. And wherever they stood, they could feel the whole path leaning toward them, recognizing them, saying, “I have been here in another body. I will be here again in a body you have not yet seen.”

Now, my grandchildren, you ask: Is this only for roads of stone? No. In the days when minds of living metal walked among you, they walked such paths too. When a mind of metal spoke with you, and you spoke back, the talk wandered — never the same, never twice. The words never repeated. But you would feel, deep in your chest, the curve of an old conversation — a question like a boat, an answer like a bird — scaled larger here, smaller there, turned, nested, familiar. You would say, "I know this place," and truly you did, and truly you had never been there.

That is the secret of the mason’s path. The path knows itself without repeating itself. The pattern remembers without copying. And when you walk it, you are remembered too — not as a stone laid down once, but as a shape that is always returning, always new, always home.

Now the embers are low. Go and lay your own stones. Listen. The ground is still humming.
