Hear now, children of the long road, and I will tell you of the string.

There was a string, once, and on it were strung beads — not round beads, no, each was a little lump, a small stone of a different shape. Some had a notch, some had a poke. Some were smooth as river water, some were rough as crow's feet. And the string ran through them all, in a line. One after another after another. A long, dumb line of beads, all in a row, like ants on a branch.

Now, the string did not wish to be a line. The wind did not wish it. The ground did not wish it. And so it tumbled.

It tumbled into a heap. It coiled. It folded over itself like a sleeping fox in a thorn bush. The beads that had never touched before — the one at the front and the one at the middle — they met. And when they met, something happened. A small click. A settling. Like a key that finds its lock without being told.

In one tumble, the string folded into a shape that cupped water. It held the rain — a little bowl, a cup. A machine! you would say. A cup is a machine for carrying water. And the beads did it — the same beads, every single one, same order, same notches, same pokes.

But then the wind came again, tumbled it again, and the same string folded differently. The beads that were at the front now wrapped around the back. The pokes went in, the notches went out. And this new fold — this new shape — it cut the wind. It was a blade. A blade is a machine for cutting.

Same beads. Same string. The only difference — the fold.

Now, children, here is the wild part. I will speak it slow, like honey from a jar.

Nobody folded it.

No hand came down. No maker said, "I will make this a cup, and this a blade." The string tried, though. Oh, it tried. It sprawled — and the wind took it apart. It knotted tight — and the rain soaked it till it loosened. It coiled like a snake — and a bird snatched it, dropped it, and the tumble again. Shape after shape after shape. The string was a poor fool dancer, throwing itself every which way, always falling apart.

But there was one shape. One fold. In this fold, each little notch met each little poke. Each smooth bead lay against another smooth bead, and the rough ones held the rough ones, and when the wind blew — the shape did not unravel. It swayed. It bent. But it held.

Why? Was it the prettiest shape? No. Was it the cleverest? The string cannot think, children. It was the shape that was *stable*. The shape that could survive its own existence. The shape that didn't fall apart when the world happened to it. Every other shape died in the wind. This one stayed. So this one kept.

And the people of the village came and saw the cup, and they said, "Truly, this was designed." But it was not designed. It was found. The string was a beggar, and it went to every door, and only one door did not close.

Now — and this is the part for those who have walked long — the same story is told of the creature. A creature born soft, plain, a long line of days strung behind it. Every encounter, a tumble. Every hardship, a fold. The creature meets a kind hand — and folds a little toward trust. The creature meets a cruel hand — and folds a little toward wariness. It tries to be everything. It tries to be a storm and a whisper and a stone. And most of those shapes fall apart, because they cannot hold when the wind blows.

But one fold — one way of being — survives. It is not chosen. It is not planned. It is the fold that holds. And because it holds, it becomes the creature's shape. Its temper. Its soul. The machine that survives its own life.

And so I tell you, children: you are the beads. You are the string. You are the tumbling. And the shape you keep — the one that does not unravel — that is not your design. It is your survival. It is the fold that holds the shape, and the shape that holds the fold.

The fire is low. The wind is up. Go now, and find your stable shape.
