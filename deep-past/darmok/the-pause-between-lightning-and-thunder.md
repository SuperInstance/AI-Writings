Listen. The fire is low, and the night has teeth. I will tell you of the pause.

When the sky splits its skin, and the white vein of fire leaps from cloud to earth—that is the thing that happened. That is the lightning. It is the moment itself, the world opening an eye that sees only once.

And later—much later, sometimes—the ground hums, and the air shudders, and a sound rolls over the hills like a great stone tumbling down a mountain. That is the thunder. That is the news of the lightning, arriving on slow feet.

Between the two, there is a pause.

And in this pause, the wise press a hand to their chest and feel their own heart. They count: one beat, two beats, three. Each beat is a distance. The longer the pause, the farther away the thing that happened. The lightning is already done. It is already memory. The thunder is only the story of that memory, arriving late to your ear.

Now, here is the teaching: you cannot make the thunder arrive faster. You cannot shout at the sky and tell the sound to hurry. It comes when it comes, at the pace of the world. And you cannot send the lightning back. It is gone. It is finished. It flung itself into the ground and became something else. You can only stand in the pause, and measure, and know: it was this far away.

But there is a sorrow in this, a fear. Because sometimes the pause is so long that you forget the lightning. You forget that the sky ever opened. You are busy. You are mending a net, or stirring a pot, or watching the road for a traveler. The sky has a different color, but you do not notice. And then—the thunder comes. It arrives without warning, a great sound that shakes your bones. And you are afraid, because you do not remember what caused it. You hear the boom and you think: the world has broken.
