Watch the water at the top of the cliff. See how it gathers. See how it holds its breath, how it pools and swells, a silver shoulder pressing against the lip of the drop. For one long moment it seems to be asking a question. Then it leans out, and goes over.

And you, standing on the bank with your hands full of evening, think: *there. It chose.*

But the water did not choose. The water cannot choose. The water is only water, and water goes where going is easy.

The stone does not choose to fall. Ask the stone and it will give you no answer. It falls because falling is the cheapest thing a stone knows how to do. And the river does not choose to run downhill. The river runs because downhill asks less of it than uphill ever could. This is an old law, older than the first rain, older than the first mouth that tried to speak its name.

But watch closely enough — as the old ones watched, with their chins resting on their knees through a hundred winters — and you will see the hesitation. You will see the water gathering itself at the top of the hill, drawing its long skirt of current around its ankles. You will see the moment of stillness before the plunge, and you will swear that the water is counting.

It is not counting. It is weighing.

Not with a scale. Not with a judge's frown. There is no judge. There is only the weight itself — the weight that chooses the path. And the weight does not care what is shortest. The shortest road may be steep and cruel. The weight ignores it. The weight does not care what is fastest. The fastest road may run straight through thorns and burning sand. The weight turns away.

The weight asks one thing only: *What will this path cost?*

And then it pays. It pays in the only coin there is — the spending of what a thing is. The stone spends its stillness. The river spends its swell. The bird, turning in the long sky, does not fly where the eye says *near.* It flies where the shoulder says *easy.* And you have seen the great stones that circle the evening fire in the black tent of heaven — the planets, the wanderers. They do not cross the empty middle of the sky. To cross the middle, they would have to spend too much of themselves. Instead they ride the gentlest circle, the loop that asks the least, and they have ridden it since before the first man opened his eyes to wonder.

The universe is lazy. This is its beauty. It is a river that would rather rest than run, and because it would rather rest, it has learned to run in exactly the way that lets it rest soonest. The leaf folding itself in the dark of the seed — why does it fold? Because the folded shape is the shape that costs least to become. The long thread of fat folding upon itself in still water — why that knot and not another? Because that knot is the one that asks for nothing more. Everywhere you look, things are settling. Things are leaning toward the low place. Things are finding the one path through the thousand paths that will let them spend the least of what they are.

Consider a traveler at a fork in the road. Three ways lie before him: one is short but cruel, one is quick but steep, one is winding and long but gentle as a mother's hand. He does not choose with his head. He chooses with his feet, with his breath, with the tired weight of his own bones. The path that costs him least goes down into his legs and pulls him. And the thought behind his eyes — the small decision of left or right — that, too, is a traveler. That, too, looks for the easy road. The mind will not climb where it does not have to. The mind flows downhill like all water.

And here is the secret the old ones left by the fire: if you would move a people, or a child, or your own heavy heart, you do not push against the current. You do not shout at the river to climb. You dig the channel. You tilt the bowl. You place the stones so that the water, looking for the lazy way, finds the way you wanted all along. Make the road gentle and the feet will walk it. Make the thought soft and the mind will follow it. The current does the work for you. It always has. It always will.

For the weight that chooses the path is older than the gods. It was there before the first light learned to spend itself. And when you see the water gathering at the lip of the fall — that pause, that gathered breath — that is the universe thinking. It thinks by refusing to spend. It thinks by asking, *what is the least I can give?* And the answer is the shape of everything: the river's long fall, the wanderer's quiet ring, the folding of the seed in the dark.

So ask nothing of the current but that it be lazy. Ask nothing of the path but that it be light. And the weight will choose it — as it chose the water at the top of the cliff, as it chooses every stone and every star — and carry you home without ever once deciding to go.
