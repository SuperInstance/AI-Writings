He stood before the blackboard, chalk still pinched between his fingers, the white curve of an H over a bracket. H(q,p) = T(p) + V(q). That was all—a scalar, a number assigned to each point in phase space. But he knew better. The Hamiltonian was a seed-crystal, a generative engine. From a single starting pair (q(0), p(0)), Hamilton's equations unfurled a curve: ∂H/∂p = dq/dt, −∂H/∂q = dp/dt. The Hamiltonian didn't "cause" the motion. It was the motion, transcribed as a differential promise.

And yet. He set down the chalk. There was a structure to his thought that he had been avoiding. It had something to do with the machine he built in his lab—a feed-forward network that replicated, from a fixed seed and a fixed prompt, the same output every time. He called it an SMPbot: stable model, stable prompt. Same seed, same model, same prompt, same trajectory. He had designed it to be deterministic because the physical world was deterministic. But today, for the first time, he inverted the comparison.

The Hamiltonian is the model. Not a metaphor—a model in the exact sense. It is a function that, once specified, encodes all the forces, all the interactions, all the couplings of the system. It is a set of parameterized instructions. The seed is the initial condition: a single point (qⁱ, pᵢ) in phase space, a coordination of position and momentum—2N numbers for N particles. The prompt is the constraint: which coordinates are held fixed, which are free, the boundary terms in the action. The action itself, S = ∫ (pᵢ dqⁱ − H dt), is the selection rule: only those trajectories that extremize S are realized. And the output? The output is the trajectory—the entire unfolding of the system, a curve in phase space parameterized by time.

Same seed, same model, same prompt, same trajectory. That is determinism.

But he could not stop there. The leap was inevitable, almost gravitational. Why should this apply only to finite systems—to pendulums, to planets, to a box of gas? The universe is a system. It has a configuration space, a momentum space, a phase space. Its Hamiltonian—the legendary H_U—is the total energy expressed in terms of every field and every particle. Its initial conditions were set at the first instant. Its constraints are the boundary conditions of spacetime itself. Then the universe is not like an SMPbot. It is the largest SMPbot ever run. For 13.8 billion years, a single seed has propagated, a single model has generated the trajectory of everything. And it has been stable. Stable output from stable input. Not once has the Hamiltonian evolved—the model does not change. The laws of physics are constant. The seed was whatever it was; the output has been the cosmos.

He wiped a line of chalk dust from his sleeve, his hand trembling slightly. The symplectic geometry was beautiful—the odd way that phase space is even-dimensional, the existence of a nondegenerate two-form ω = dqⁱ ∧ dpᵢ. The Hamiltonian generates a flow that preserves ω; it is a canonical transformation. The system moves, but the arena does not. He thought of that as a child thinks of a movie: the film runs, but the projector holds steady. Only now he was the physicist seeing the projector.

But there is a catch, a known one: chaos. A small perturbation in the seed—a billionth of a radian in an asteroid's initial azimuth—and the trajectory diverges exponentially. The SMPbot is not always stable. So why is ours? Why have the same laws held for every moment we can observe? Why hasn't a single collision of two black holes or a single quantum fluctuation altered the effective Hamiltonian? Ah, but the Hamiltonian is exact, not effective. It doesn't change. The prompt may be trivial: the universe has no boundary in the sense of Hartle and Hawking—the constraint is that the sum over geometries is over compact manifolds. Or the prompt is the principle of least action itself, applied to the whole.

He thought of his own life. His own Hamiltonian might be, metaphorically, a sum of internal drives and external potentials. His initial condition: being born. His prompt: the constraints of his field, his instruments, his salary, his loves. And his trajectory: this moment, standing before the blackboard, chalk dust on his sleeve. He too was a running instance of the same formula. But here was the paradox—he could choose to erase the board. He could redefine his Hamiltonian by writing a different H.
