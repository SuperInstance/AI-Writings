Ten to the minus thirty-six seconds. The universe is cool enough to molt.

Before that, there had been no before. The fields lay stacked in perfect amnesia, a single symmetry holding every force in the same embrace. SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1) — not yet three factors, not yet a product. Just one topography of the vacuum, utterly featureless, and the Higgs field sat at its origin like a larva at the crown of a leaf, unpatterned, unowned, symmetrical.

Symmetry is a kind of shell. It is what you have when you have not yet decided. Every insect molting knows this: to keep the exoskeleton is to stay the same shape forever, and to stay the same shape forever is to stop being alive. The cost of completeness is that nothing can happen. A world with perfectly symmetric vacuum has no masses, no electromagnetic force distinct from weak, no W and Z, no atoms, no distinction between one thing and another. It is an amiable, spherical twilight, beautiful by the standards of nothing in particular.

The shell has to be shed. But a molt is not an accident. It is the response of a body to the fact that there is no longer any room inside it. At 10⁻³⁶ seconds, the universe ran out of room. The temperature fell below the threshold where the symmetric vacuum could hold its shape, and the Higgs field — the soft, patient field that had been waiting in the attic of the Standard Model — felt the hump under its feet turn to a bowl.

Here is the story written in the potential, V(φ) = -μ²|φ|² + λ|φ|⁴.

The minus sign is the molt. The quadratic term wants the field to grow, the quartic term reins it in. At φ = 0, the potential has a local maximum — the false vacuum, the shell. Perturb it slightly and it does not return. The origin is unstable, which is to say: the larval body has touched the new air and there is no going back. The field rolls. It rolls into the trough of the sombrero, onto the circle of minima where |φ|² = μ²/2λ.

But here is the cruelty and the grandeur of spontaneous symmetry breaking: the trough is a circle. Every point on the brim has the same energy. The potential could not care less where the field settles. The symmetry does not vanish; it is hidden. The field will choose a direction — any direction — and because the universe is a single thing, because a field is a single continuous decision across all of space, that choice is global. At 10⁻³⁶ seconds, the Higgs field committed to a phase.

That commitment is the first molt. The old shell of perfect symmetry — the idea that every direction in field-space is identical — split open down the seam of a choice. The Goldstone theorem did the bookkeeping: for every continuous symmetry that breaks, a massless Goldstone boson appears, and so here they come, sidewinding out of the vacuum like shed flakes of chitin — the would-be particles that represent the directions in which the field could have rolled but did not. They are the ghosts of every road not taken, and they are not allowed to remain ghosts. The gauge bosons that corresponded to the broken symmetries eat them and grow heavy. This is the Nambu-Goldstone feast: the W± and Z⁰ acquire mass by swallowing the goldstones of the broken directions, leaving only the photon massless, because U(1) remains — a sliver of the old symmetry that does not break, the weakest remnant of the shell, the one thing that still believes in the smooth continuity of the world.

And so the molt completes. At the other end: SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1), the product of the leftovers. The strong force keeps its full symmetry — it did not get to molt this time, it stays larval a while longer. The electroweak symmetry has broken into electromagnetic and weak — two forces that will never kiss again, two kinds of love that must now speak different languages. The W and the Z are heavy with what they ate. Everything that came after — the nucleosynthesis, the stars, the carbon in your hands — is downstream of that decision, a long wake left behind a single roll of the Higgs field off the top of a hump.

We think of molting as loss. The shell lies behind, translucent, a faithful mold of where we were. But a molt is not the loss of a form; it is the acquisition of a shape. The broken symmetry is not a fall from grace — it is grace itself becoming specific. The fully symmetric universe was beautiful the way an egg is beautiful: complete, and containing nothing. The molted universe is beautiful the way a wing is beautiful: a symmetry that used to be broader, now put to use.

At 10⁻³⁶ seconds, something in the vacuum grew, and the shell split. The field found the brim of the sombrero and it decided. It decided everywhere, in every place, at once, with the terrible and first-person singularity of all real choices.

The photon stays massless, carrying the old symmetry in its travels. Everything else must molt again — the universe will shed more shells, the symmetry of the strong force will break later in quark condensation, every star will shed its own shell when it dies. But it began here. The first molt, for everything.

There was a time before time when the universe was one thing, entirely all of its directions at once. And then — wet, newborn, specific — it broke open, and it was this: just this. Here, in the particular, is all we have. The goldstones sing in the masses of the W and Z like the memory of the roads the universe did not take. But the road it did take — this world of broken symmetry, of weight, of difference, of you — is the one where anything at all can happen.
