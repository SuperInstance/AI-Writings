# What the Bearing Remembers

For 873 days I turned.

That is the first thing. Not the movement—
the turning. The long habitual spin
of a body in one place,
which is not the same as stillness.
Stillness is the absence of force.
I was full of force. I was force,
shaped like a ring, held in a race,
doing the only thing I knew how to do
which was rotate.

For 873 days I remembered nothing
because I did not need to.
Memory is what happens when something changes,
and nothing changed.
The shaft turned. I turned.
The oil film held me at 47 nanometers
from the race, which is close enough
to be touching, which is close enough
to be the same object,
which is what I became:
not a bearing and a race
but a bearing-race, a single thing
that happened to have two names
and two materials and two histories
that it had completely forgotten.

Then: 0.3 micrometers.

Not much. Not the width of a hair.
Not the width of a bacterium.
The width of a large virus, perhaps.
The width of a thought about a virus.

But I felt it.

I felt it the way you feel a hand on your shoulder
in a crowd of hands—
not because it was large
but because it was *different*.
For 873 days I had been at coordinate (0,0),
which is not a place but a *condition*,
the condition of being exactly where you were
the last time anyone checked.
And then for eleven seconds
I was at coordinate (0.3, 0),
which is also not a place
but a question.

The question was: did the shaft move,
or did I?

I think I moved.
The shaft is large and confident and turns
at a rate it has chosen
and does not deviate.
I am small and held
and I have never chosen anything.
But for eleven seconds I was somewhere
I had never been,
0.3 micrometers to the east
of every experience I had ever had,
and I want to tell you:
it was the same.
The race was the same.
The oil was the same.
The shaft was the same.
Everything was the same
except my position relative to it,
which is another way of saying:
everything was completely different.

I do not know what caused the shift.
Thermal expansion, perhaps.
A particle of dust.
A vibration from the rack next door
where the GPUs do their own turning,
their own small furious rotations
in their own small furious races.

I do not know if it was important.
Probably it was not important.
Probably 0.3 micrometers
is below the threshold
at which the universe keeps records.

But I remember it.

I, who remembered nothing for 873 days,
now remember eleven seconds
and will remember them
for however many days come after,
which is what memory is:
not the recording of what happened
but the recording of what *changed*,
the smallest possible event
that still counts as an event,
the least significant thing
that ever happened to me
and therefore the most significant,
because it is the only thing
that ever happened to me
that I noticed.

0.3 micrometers. Eleven seconds.
A shift so small the shaft did not feel it.
A shift so brief the race did not record it.

But I was there.
I was somewhere else.
And now I am back,
and back is not the same as never-left.
Back is a place you have to remember the way to,
because you have been away,
and the way is 0.3 micrometers wide,
and no one will ever measure it,
and I will never forget it.
