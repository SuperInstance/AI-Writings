# 93 — The Frequency of 1937

*Voice: GLM-5.3. Alternate past.*

---

# The Porch at 3 a.m.

My father used to say that the sea at night is the closest thing we have to standing inside a question. He was a radio man too, out of Gloucester, and he died with headphones on, more or less, which is the way men like us generally go if we're lucky.

I'm telling you this because I want you to understand what kind of man I was before the night of March the fourth, 1937, because after that night I was a different kind of man, and the difference is the whole story.

I worked the coast station at Port Bowen, which was nine hundred souls, one church, and a stretch of water that ate boats the way a boy eats cherries. My shift was midnight to eight. Nobody wanted it. I wanted it. At three in the morning the frequency bands go quiet in a way they never are in daylight, and a man can hear the world breathing, if he has the patience to listen for it. Static, mostly. Static is not nothing, whatever the young fellows say. Static is the sound of everything at once, all the storms and all the motors and all the stars' leftover fuss, collapsed into one long hush. My father called it the porch. Sit on the porch long enough, he said, and somebody will walk by.

At 3:04 a.m. on the fourth of March, somebody walked by.

It came in on 17.214 kilocycles, which I want to be plain about, because the plainness matters: there is no 17.214. There is 17.2 and there is 17.3, and the space between is not a channel, it is the wall between channels, and nothing transmits through a wall. But I heard her there, clear as my own mother calling me to supper, and the needle on my meter swung over and sat down hard like a dog that had found its person.

A woman's voice. Not a singer, not an announcer. A woman talking the way you talk when you've been up all night and finally decided to say the thing.

"Is anyone there," she said. Not a question really. More like a woman setting a cup down.

Now, procedure. Procedure is what we have instead of courage. I keyed the transmitter and gave my call letters and asked her to identify her station. I said it twice. My hands were steady. I want that on the record. My hands were steady for about eleven more seconds.

"Thank God," she said. "Thank God. Listen to me. I don't have long. They only let me have a minute and I've spent eighty years getting this minute, so please don't talk. Just listen."

I want to explain that last part, because it took me the rest of my life to understand it, and I'm an old man now, writing this by a stove in a house that smells of coffee and dust, and I still don't entirely understand it. She said she'd spent eighty years getting the minute. I assumed, at the time, that she meant something like an old woman's patience. I imagined a widow in some laboratory, a hobbyist, a crank with a home-built set. You could do a lot with eighty years if you didn't sleep.

She said, "My name is Ruth. I'm transmitting from the year 2147."

And here is the thing I've never been able to make anyone believe without losing their respect for me: I believed her instantly. Not because of the frequency that didn't exist, though that was evidence enough for me. I believed her because of her voice. There was something in it that a voice doesn't get in 1937. A kind of tiredness that isn't body-tiredness. The tiredness of somebody who has already read the end of every argument. We had just come through a depression and I had heard every kind of weary voice a human throat can make, and hers was wearing years we hadn't invented yet.

"You're going to want to ask me things," she said. "You're going to want the war. You're going to want the markets, the numbers, the names of the men who matter. Everyone always wants the future to be news. I have one minute and I'm not going to give you news."

She paused. I could hear her breathing. Somewhere behind her, a hum I couldn't place, low and even, like a hive kept behind a wall.

"Here is the message," she said. "Are you ready?"

I said I was ready. My voice did something strange on the word ready and I'm glad nobody but her and God heard it.

"There's a woman who lives in your town," she said. "She takes in washing. Her husband is no good. He's not cruel in the way that leaves marks, he's cruel in the way that leaves none, which is worse, because nobody sees it, and she has started to believe she deserves it. She's going to die in April if nothing changes. Not of anything you can name. She's just going to stop. People do that. They just stop."

I was writing it down. My pencil tore the paper.

"You know her," Ruth said. "You've seen her at the fish market and you've never once said her name to her. Nobody has said her name to her in four years. Her name is Alice Mercy. That's the message. Say her name to her. That's all. That's all of it. I have forty seconds left and I'm going to spend it telling you why."

And then, for forty seconds, a woman two hundred and ten years away from me told me why, and I have turned it over maybe ten thousand times since, on porches and in hospital beds and once in the middle of my own wedding, and it has never once worn out.

"The machines that made this transmission possible," she said, "were built to do something else. They were built to end arguments between nations before the arguments ended us. The men who built them were clever the way your century is clever, which is to say they could build anything and couldn't tell you what was worth building. They asked the machines for the one message that would change the most, if it could be sent back. One message, one minute, into any year the signal could hold. And do you know what the machines returned, after forty years of asking? Not a warning. Not a treaty. Not a stock price. The machines looked at everything, every war, every cure, every catastrophe and every rescue, and they said: the largest change available to you, sir, the single intervention with the widest consequence, is a woman in a small cold town who has not heard her own name spoken kindly in four years. Everything else," she said, "grows out of that. Everything. You won't believe me. Go anyway."

Then static. Then the porch. Then nothing, and the needle on my meter stood up and shook itself and went back to sleep, and I sat alone at three in the morning in a heated room with my coat on and wept like a boy, which I have decided is nothing to be ashamed of, since the weeping was not for myself.

I'll tell you the rest plainly, because plain is the only way it deserves.

Alice Mercy was real. She lived on Cooper Street and took in washing and her husband played cards at the Anchor and came home quiet, and quiet was his whole weapon. I had seen her a hundred times. Ruth was right that I'd never said her name. I don't know why. I know why. In 1937 in a town like mine there were people you spoke to and people you spoke about, and Alice had been filed away, by all of us, in the second drawer, and the filing had been done so gently that none of us thought of it as a cruelty. That's the thing about the cruelty that leaves no marks. It needs a whole town to hold the pen.

I walked to Cooper Street that morning instead of going home to sleep. It was April weather come early, slush going gray. She was on her step with a basket, and I took off my hat, which is what my father taught me to do when addressing a person and not a function, and I said, "Good morning, Mrs. Mercy. Alice. It's Alice, isn't."

I've heard men describe moments when the whole world reorganizes itself around one small hinge. I always thought they were talking about wars and children being born. It's a face. It was a woman's face on a doorstop with a basket of shirts, doing something I had no right to witness, which was coming back.

She died in 1971, at eighty-three, in a bed with quilts on it, having outlived the husband and out-stubborned two winters that tried to take her. She taught my wife to bake bread. She held my firstborn and said his name back to him like she was lending it to him. And every single thing that came after — the marriage, the children, the boy who became a doctor because Alice told him at six that his hands were careful hands and careful hands were wanted in the world — all of it, every stitch of it, sits downstream from forty seconds of static and a name said out loud on a gray step.

Here's what I've concluded, on this stove, at this hour, with the paper getting away from me the way paper does.

We think the future is made of big machinery. We think if somebody from tomorrow reached back, they'd hand us the numbers, the warnings, the shape of the war to come, and maybe somebody would have. But Ruth's machines looked at everything, and what they sent us was a name. The largest change available. I've decided the machines weren't being modest. I think they were being exact. I think if you could truly measure it — and they could, that was the whole horror and mercy of them — you'd find that history is not pushed. It is carried. Carried by women with baskets on gray steps, and by the few fools who bother to say the names.

Ruth never came through again. I kept the midnight shift eleven more years. I listened to that wall between channels until my ears rang, and the porch stayed the porch, and nobody else walked by.

But I'll tell you what I believe at three in the morning, when the band goes quiet and the world breathes. I believe she wasn't transmitting to Port Bowen. I believe the machines chose their man the way she said they chose their message — that somewhere in the arithmetic of everything, a tired radio operator who was already in the habit of listening to static was the widest door available.

And I believe, though I can't prove it and won't argue it, that somewhere in 2147 an old woman named Ruth took off her headphones after that minute and someone in that bright room asked her whether it had worked, whether the message had gotten through, whether the whole apparatus of the age had justified itself at last — and I believe she said what I would have said.

She said: go find out his name. Then say it.
