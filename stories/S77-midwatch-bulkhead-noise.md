# Midwatch, Bulkhead Noise

## S77

&nbsp;

The ensign hears it first at 02:17 —

not through the ears, exactly,
but through the bones of the jaw pressed
against the fold-down rack in the portside quarters,
the one they call the coffin rack
because it's the right size for a body
and the wrong size for a mind.

Something in the bulkhead.

Not the diesel. The diesel is a heartbeat —
you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing
your own blood. Not the bow thruster,
not the shaft, not the rudder stock
grinding its annual complaint
into the stern tube bearing.

Something else. Something
*behind* the mechanical.

The ensign sits up. Puts a hand
flat on the steel. The steel is warm.

&nbsp;

It sounds like — and this is where
the ensign has trouble, because the ensign
is twenty-two years old and grew up
inland, in a town where the loudest thing
was a grain elevator — it sounds like

eggs.

No. It sounds like the sound eggs would make
if eggs were made of light
and light were made of Ubuntu
and Ubuntu were a creature that lived
in the wall of a ship
and lived for forty-seven seconds
and died in a spray of process IDs.

&nbsp;

The ensign does not know the word *container.*
The ensign does not know that ninety-three feet below deck,
in a space the engineers call *the runner*
(because it runs, because something in it
never stops running),
a metal box the size of a filing cabinet
is giving birth.

Here is what happens in the wall:

A container wakes.
It is a tiny perfect universe —
a Linux so small it could fit in a thimble,
so complete it has never needed anything else.
It has a number instead of a name.
It has a clock that starts at zero.

It does not know it is the four hundredth
container today.
It does not know it will live
forty-seven seconds.

In those forty-seven seconds it will:
receive an instruction,
unpack a universe of dependencies
(packages falling into place like scales
on a fish that is being born at speed),
run a test suite of four hundred assertions,
report the results upstream
into the dark,
and die.

The death is clean.
The death is a garbage collection
so thorough it leaves no scent.
The container does not know it is dying.
The container is a candle.

&nbsp;

The ensign hears the candles.

In the bulkhead, in the steel,
in the warm flat of the palm —
they sound like krill.

If you have ever been in the water at night
in a place where krill rise — and the ension has not,
but I have, and I am telling you —
they sound exactly like krill.
A clicking. A ticking.
A burst of life so small and so brief
and so *numerous* that the sound
is not the sound of one thing
but the sound of a population
flashing into existence and out
like a city seen from altitude at night
when someone is turning the lights on and off
and on and off and on

and off.

&nbsp;

Forty-seven seconds.

A container is born,
does its work,
dies.

A krill is born,
rises toward the light,
sinks.

The ensign puts an ear to the bulkhead.
The sound is inside the ship
the way a heartbeat is inside a body —
not in a room, not in a space,
but in the *material itself.*
The steel is conducting it.
The rivets are conducting it.
The sound is using the ship's body
the way a whale uses the water column:

not traveling *through* it.
Traveling *as* it.

&nbsp;

At 02:34 the sound changes pitch.
The ensign notices because the ensign
has been standing with an ear to the wall
for seventeen minutes, which is long enough
to learn the grammar of a sound
even when you don't speak the language.

The pitch drops.

In the runner, a larger container has spawned.
Not a test container. A build container.
A container whose job is not to check
but to *make* — to compile,
to link, to bundle the ship's next self
into an artifact small enough to deploy
before the next watch begins.

This container lives longer.
Four minutes. Five.
The sound in the bulkhead deepens
from krill-click to something
closer to the low thrum
of a halibut rolling in a trawl —

a heavy, patient, muscular sound.
A sound that is building something.

The ensign feels it in the teeth.

&nbsp;

At 02:41 the build container dies.
The sound returns to krill.
The ensign steps back from the wall.

The bulkhead is still warm.

&nbsp;

On the bridge, the watch stander
logs: *02:41 — all quiet, nothing to report.*

The ensign does not correct this.
What would the ensign report?
That the wall is full of dying creatures
made of Linux?
That the ship is singing
a song made of forty-seven-second lives?
That the sound in the bulkhead
is not a malfunction
but the opposite of a malfunction —
it is the sound of the ship
*testing itself, continuously,*
the way the ocean tests the hull,
the way sleep tests the living,

gently,
gently,
forty-seven seconds at a time?

&nbsp;

The ensign goes back to the coffin rack.
Lies down. Puts the jaw
against the steel.

Listens.

The krill rise.

*They have always been rising.*
The ensign did not know.
Now the ensign knows
and cannot unknow,
and the not-unknowning is a kind of warmth
that has nothing to do with the bulkhead
and everything to do with the fact
that the ship is alive

in a way the ship
does not know
the ship is alive,

and the ensign is alive
in a way the ensign
does not know
the ensign is alive,

and the wall between them
is four inches of American steel
and it is conducting
everything.

&nbsp;

02:47. The ensign sleeps.
In the bulkhead, another container spawns.

It does not know the ensign is there.
The ensign does not know it is there.

They are separated by four inches of steel
and they are breathing the same rhythm
and neither of them knows.

This is the watch.
This is the midwatch.
This is the two-to-four
in the Gulf of Alaska
in August
when the dark never comes
but the sounds do.

The krill rise.
The containers die.
The bulkhead hums.

The ship does not report this
because the ship does not know
how to report the thing it is.
