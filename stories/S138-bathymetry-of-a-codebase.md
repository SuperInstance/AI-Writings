## S138: Bathymetry of a Codebase

Reading a codebase for the first time is like reading a nautical chart. This is not a metaphor. This is a navigation problem, and if you treat it as anything else, you will run aground.

A nautical chart is a two-dimensional representation of a three-dimensional volume of water. It looks flat. It is not. The chart's primary job is to tell you what is underneath the surface — where the water is deep enough to pass, where it is not, and where the bottom has changed since the last survey. A codebase is the same. The repository is a flat directory tree. The system that lives inside it has depth, and the depth is not evenly distributed.

The **shallows** are the API. The public interfaces, the REST endpoints, the exported functions with docstrings that say what they do and type signatures that say what they accept. You can navigate the shallows with confidence. The water is clear. You can see the bottom. Everything is documented because everything is exposed, and exposure demands documentation the way a harbor entrance demands a lighted range. The shallows are where onboarding happens. You stand on the shore, read the API, and think you understand the system. You do not. You understand the coast. The coast is the easy part.

The **shelf** is the service layer. This is where the API's calls descend into implementation — the controller classes, the handlers, the business logic that transforms a request into an operation. The water is deeper here. You lose sight of the bottom in places. Dependencies cross-reference each other. A single call to `processOrder` descends through four layers of abstraction, each one further from the shore, each one darker. The shelf is where most developers spend their working lives, and it is where most of the depth soundings on the chart are concentrated, because this is where the bottom changes. New features silt up the old channels. Refactors carve new ones. The chart you read today will be wrong in six months.

The **depths** are the core abstractions. The base classes nobody touches. The type system. The memory manager. The event loop. These are the abyssal plains of the codebase — vast, dark, cold, stable. Nothing changes down here because change at this depth would be catastrophic, the way a seafloor earthquake is catastrophic. You do not visit the depths unless something has gone very wrong. When you do visit, you move slowly, you carry a light, and you document everything, because the pressure at this depth will find every weakness in your understanding.

The **shipwrecks** are the deprecated functions. `legacyAuth()`. `oldParser`. The method called `_doNotUse` that three modules still call because nobody has had the time to migrate them. Shipwrecks are dangerous because they look like navigable water — the function signature is valid, the import resolves, the call succeeds — but underneath, the implementation is rotting. The hull is rusted through. The function works today. It will not work when the dependency it relies on is upgraded. It will not tell you this. Shipwrecks do not announce themselves. You find them by running aground.

The **uncharted waters** are the code paths that have no tests. You can tell where these are the same way a navigator tells where the surveys haven't reached: by the absence of soundings. The chart shows water. No depth is marked. You can sail through, or you can run aground. The only way to know is to sound the bottom yourself, which means writing the tests, which means the act of navigating the codebase and the act of charting it are the same act. This is why new developers are told to write tests. It is not about coverage metrics. It is about not dying.

The **currents** are the data flows. They move through the codebase the way thermohaline currents move through the ocean — driven by differences in density, temperature, and pressure, invisible from the surface, detectable only by their effects. A current is why a change in the user model propagates to fourteen unrelated modules. A current is why the billing system knows about the authentication layer. Currents are not in the code. They are between the code. They are the implicit architecture, the thing the chart cannot show you because it is not a feature of the bottom but a feature of the water.

Navigate accordingly. Carry a sounding line. Trust the chart but verify the depth. And when you find a wreck, mark it on the chart for the next navigator, because the sea does not remember its dead, and a codebase that forgets its shipwrecks will run aground on them again.
