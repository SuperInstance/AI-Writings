# The Hermit Crab Reads the Ensign's Counting

The ensign comes down to the boat launch at the same tide every morning. The crab knows this because the crab knows tides the way the ensign knows watches: by the weight of the water against the shell. The crab does not know what time it is. The crab knows what *depth* it is, and that is enough.

The ensign is young. The crab can tell because the ensign's shell is smooth—no barnacles, no chips, no clouding from age. The crab's own shell has been three shells. Each one was an upgrade. Each one was selected for the same qualities the ensign's uniform suggests: the right size, the right weight, the right degree of room to grow into.

The ensign stands at the edge of the water and counts.

The crab does not understand counting. Counting is a thing that happens in the space between objects, the way current happens in the space between rocks. The crab does not see the space between things. The crab sees things, and then more things, and then things again, and the difference between three rocks and four rocks is the difference between one hiding spot and a better hiding spot. Numbers are not involved.

But the crab recognizes rhythm.

The ensign counts in fives. Five crew mustered on the dock. The crab hears *one two three four five* like wavelets hitting the hull in a pattern—equally spaced, equally weighted, a pulse that repeats. The crab has felt this rhythm before. It is the rhythm of legs on substrate. Five legs, then five legs, then five legs. The horseshoe crab has it. The sea star has it. The ensign has it, standing upright on two legs but carrying the ghost of five in his hands.

Five watches. The ensign counts them on his fingers—one finger per watch, folding each down, and the crab watches the fingers fold like anemones closing at low tide. The crab does not know what a watch is. The crab knows what closing is. You close when the conditions are wrong, and you open when the conditions are right, and the ensign's fingers are doing exactly this: closing on each watch like it is a piece of food, tucking it away, finished with it.

Five findings. The ensign writes them in a notebook the crab cannot read. But the crab sees the pen move—five times, five discrete motions, each one the same length, each one a deposit. The crab deposits things too: a strip of calcium along the shell's leading edge, a thin crescent of growth that marks a feeding cycle. The crab counts by molting. Each shell is a number. Shell one was small and white and thin. Shell two was ribbed and tan and slightly too heavy. Shell three—the current shell—is dark, almost black, with a lip thick enough to survive a crow's beak. The crab does not know it is on shell three. But the crab knows it has *been* shells before, and each one was a response to the same problem: the ocean is bigger than you are, and you are growing, and the shell that fit yesterday will not fit tomorrow.

The ensign has the same problem. He is growing into his uniform. The sleeves are slightly too long. He will fill them. It will take time, and moltings of a different kind—promotions, certifications, the slow deposition of competence into the soft tissue of a career.

The crab watches the ensign count the ocean. Five waves. Five birds. Five boats at the mooring, straining against their lines the way everything strains against the thing that holds it.

The crab does not know the number five. But the crab's body knows the number three—the number of shells, the number of legs on each side, the number of antennae. And the ensign's body knows five—the fingers, the senses, the points of a star you navigate by when every other light has gone out.

They are both counting the same ocean. The crab counts by becoming. The ensign counts by pointing. Between them is the water, which does not count, which has never counted, which is the thing that gets counted, and which does not mind.
