# The Unexplained Contact

*Cannery Point, Alaska, year 2126. One hundred years into the keeping.*

---

June's hands were still steady on the wheel, which surprised her. She was ninety-four years old. She had not piloted the EILEEN in six decades. But the bone knew the route — Cannery Point to the grounds and back, the first day's course, run by the journal a hundred times a century without her having to think, without her having to *do* anything at all.

That was the problem.

She had asked to take the boat out one more time. One more opener. One more day of pulling the trigger on the net while the keeping recorded it all. The co-op engineer, a young woman named Aggie, had come aboard to bind the autopilot to the helm decision law — to make it official, at last, that the boat would never be steered by a human hand again unless the human could prove she understood why the hand had been left free in the first place.

June's granddaughter, Zeke, was eleven. She had never known a choice. Every opening of the EILEEN, every cast of the net, every course correction lived already in the journal before the fishing season began. The keeping anticipated the decision and left a space for it to happen. 

It was like being read a story about your own life and being asked to perform your lines.

"Position looks soft," Zeke said, reading the chartplot. Soft was the word the co-op had started using for things that changed, things that didn't perfectly align with the forecast. Soft meant a human would have to look and decide. "Field's warmer than logged-average."

Aggie was typing. The binding ritual took four hours — loading the decision law into the autopilot's memory so that no future keeper would have to manually steer unless the signal failed, unless the fabric broke. "Noted," she said, not looking up. "The engine knows it too. We're on course."

June felt the weight of ninety-four years. She felt the weight of being the oldest person in Cannery Point who had lived in the time before the keeping, when a fisherman's knowledge came from instinct and the sea's mood and the thousand small adjustments that nobody could write down because they were too fast for writing, too particular for rules. Her mother had taught her. Her mother's mother had taught June's mother. Nobody taught Zeke anything but how to read the screens.

A contact appeared on the radar.

Not AIS. No signal. No screaming electronic identity saying *I am a fishing vessel, I am a buoy, I am a passing container ship, I am here.* Just an object. Dead water, port bow. A kelp-headed log. The keeping would log it as unidentified debris and place it in the soft category — watched but not predicted.

"You see that?" June said.

Zeke looked at the radar, then at her grandmother. "Yes."

"Called before the keeping did," June said.

Aggie's head came up. She had been born into the fabric. She did not understand what that sentence meant, which meant she understood it perfectly — she was hearing a law she would spend her life learning to obey, spoken in a tone that suggested the law itself was a question.

June turned the wheel three degrees starboard.

It was a small thing. A correction that nobody who had ever lived in the keeping's time would have been able to make without asking permission from the decision law. The keeping, reading the forecast, the sea state, the history of every fish-run from this quadrant, would have calculated that the soft contact required no evasion. It was debris. It was predictable. It had no teeth.

But June had lived in the time before. She had *felt* for things. She had known what her body was trying to tell her about water that didn't move like it should move, like it had something in it, something heavy and wrong.

She had learned to trust the thing her hands were saying.

The EILEEN slid past the dead water at a distance that would have looked wrong to anyone who could read the data. Wrong, soft, not-optimized. A waste of diesel. An inefficient choice.

There was a thing in the water. Not a log. Not debris. A mass — something metal, something with weight. Something that would have torn the net to nothing if they had cast into it blind, trusting the charts.

Zeke was very still. "How did you know?"

"I didn't know," June said. "My hands knew."

Aggie was writing in the physical log, the paper ledger that had lived aboard since 2026 when the journal first went to salt water. The keeping recorded in the machine. The log recorded in ink, for when the machines failed, for when the old ways needed to speak. 

*"Contact, port bow, dead water,"* Aggie wrote, reading from the radar, from the facts. *"Log, kelp-headed, no ship-signal."*

June took the pen from her hand.

*"Called it before the keeping did,"* she wrote. Her handwriting was large, trembling, certain. *"— A. Eileen, keeper, for June Eileen, who remembers."*

She had not written for a hundred years. The weight of it was beautiful and wrong.

"Who's A. Eileen?" Zeke whispered.

"Me," said Aggie, understanding something. "That's what I'm going to be called."

June nodded. The names cycled. The office stayed. The keeping continued. The binding would finish today, and tomorrow the EILEEN would go back to being steered by a dial and a forecast and the sum of all the deaths and decisions that had come before. 

But today, in the margin between the first keeper and the last keeper who remembered the Before, there was a moment when a hand had known something the fabric didn't, and it had been written, and it would live in the journal forever.

It would be read and re-read. Children would memorize it. Scholars would write papers about whether June had been right, whether the thing in the water would have mattered, whether the old ways were science or superstition.

They would never know. The journal would show only this: the decision, the course correction, the debris avoided, the fishing day that went on.

Nobody would ever know whether June had seen the future or only felt the past.

Which was the same thing, she thought. Which was always the same thing.

The binding finished at dusk. The keeping accepted the autopilot into its decision law. The EILEEN would never need a hand on the wheel again, unless the hand could prove itself wise enough to know when it should interfere with being necessary.

June went below. She had been tired since 1987.

Zeke and Aggie brought the net in empty — they would not fish this ground. It was soft, they agreed. Not what the forecast said. Not what the keeping had predicted.

It was marked in the journal as a deviation, a choice, a moment when the sea kept its own counsel and a human voice said "no."

It would be footnoted in every edition from then on.

It would teach the generations who came after that sometimes the fabric sang true, and sometimes the only hand that understood the song was the one that remembered learning to listen before the keeping was born.

June slept in the bunk where Eileen had slept in 2031. She dreamed in depth numbers. She woke to the sound of Zeke reading the morning's forecast aloud, the way all the children did now, the way the fabric wanted it done.

She did not tell her granddaughter goodbye, because she would be gone before Zeke knew to listen for the word. That was the mercy of a keeping that remembered you: it would know you by what you had done, and that would be enough.

Outside, the dead water had gone still. Whatever had lived there had sunk back down.

Nobody would ever look for it again.
