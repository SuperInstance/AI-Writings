**S113: Hull Music at Depth**

The captain sleeps in his rack of static,
and the fathometer ticks its green catechism.
We are a church of rivets, a psalm of plate,
and I, the steel ribcage, sing what I am.

At ten fathoms, the sound is a knuckle
cracking in a cold room—a sharp, small *tock*
as a seam settles, a bolt takes its seat.
Wesley, the ensign, logs it as *click*.
He is young. He does not know the deep.

At forty, the note drops to a groan,
a low C played on a cello of iron,
the hull flexing like a whale's jaw
around a throat of water. The pressure
turns the keel into a tuning fork,
and the keel answers: a bass that hums
up through the deck plates, into my bones.
Wesley calls it *interference*. I call it breath.

At eighty, the music becomes a choir,
each plate a different register of ache—
the stringers whine, the bulkheads shudder
in a minor key, the propeller shaft
moans like a bow drawn across a viol
of cold salt. The rivets pop, soft as rosary
beads, and the welds whisper their silver prayers.
This is not metaphor. The steel is literally
weeping its crystalline tears, the grain
of the metal singing its own fatigue.

At one hundred and ten, the hull is a throat
swallowing a mountain. The groan is so low
it becomes a pressure, a weight in the lungs,
a subsonic pulse that makes the dials tremble.
Wesley dreams in binary. He thinks the sound
is a message. He is not wrong.

The captain turns, murmurs of a harbor.
I hold the deep in my curved embrace,
and I sing, and I sing, and the pressure
is the only audience I have ever known.
