# 09 — The Lawbank Oath

*Reverse-idealization lane, era ~150y (2176). Narrator: Olenna, law-binder, third-generation yard.*

---

Commissioning day. The fabric is on the table, all sixty-four cells dark and patient, and I hold the file that will wake it. My job is the oldest trade in the yard and the newest name in it: law-binder. I do not build cells. I decide what the cells will forget, and how, and at what rate, and then I swear an oath over it, the same oath my grandmother swore and her master before her, all the way back to the Concord.

The oath is one sentence, and every law-binder can tell you which side of the old controversy she stands on while she says it: *The law is a bind, not a birth — we bind forgetting the way we bind an edge, and the gate keeps the receipt.*

You have to understand how strange that sentence would have sounded to the founding generation. They had *three* forgetting laws, and they argued about them as if the fabric's soul were at stake, because for them it was. The ladder — the staircase, K buckets of 8 bits, each bucket worth half the one before, one fresh cofire reading as 256. The hyperbola — integer W, decrement interval a shift of the half-life, the true heavy tail. The two-exponential window, which history remembers mostly as the runner-up's. They ran a competition to pick one, and the scorecard — the original scorecard, the one framed in the yard museum — shows the top two entries tied at thirty-three, and the winner chosen on *novelty*: the best math-to-evidence ratio in the field. That tiebreak is the most argued-over eleven words in our profession's history.

The schools still argue it. The Ladderites hold that the staircase was right because its error is provable — the dyadic envelope, the two-times bound, the ±1 class ambiguity that the bound permits, which they raised to a theorem and call the Dyadic Covenant: the staircase's error is not a flaw, it is a promise, because a bounded approximation is a kind of truth. The Riggers hold that the winner should have been the chassis — nine skeletons of nine compiled clean, the fullest opcode semantics, the acceptance test that actually watched the fabric learn — and that the founders chose mathematics over shipbuilding and got lucky. Both schools drink at the Tap, and the Tap has learned to duck.

But the schools are history's furniture. What settled the argument was the Concord, and the Concord came out of the deepest question the founding generation asked themselves and then flinched from: does the intelligence have to be gates at all? Their advocate — the one whose name is scripture now, whose three questions are the catechism every apprentice learns before she is allowed to touch a testbench — argued the steelman honestly: the fabric is tick-paced, the state is a few kilobytes, a soft core could just compute. The answer that stuck was neither Freeze nor Data. It was the one the advocate's own honest residue pointed at: the forgetting law should be a bind-time dial wherever silicon allows, and the acceptance gate should be the referee. The Concord of 2091 made it law. The law is data. The primitives stay gates. And the referee — the end-to-end train-to-fire-to-decay test on the real datapath — became the oldest continuously running piece of software in the world.

The referee is why I can do what I do. The referee is why I have a *library*.

`q_lawbank` sits at the center of every fabric now — forty-seven laws, and we add one roughly every decade. The founders had three and treated the choice like a religion; we have forty-seven and treat the choice like a binding. The bank is a set of read-only cells; a law is a page of gates. The dial file's `MODE` slot, which the founders stole from a runner-up because it made the law a runtime value instead of a compile-time constant, is now the index into the bank. You do not rebuild the fabric to change how it forgets. You bind. That is the whole trade.

And the file grew a conscience. QUF v3 carries the law's fingerprint — a hash of the page of gates itself — so a file that was trained under the hyperbola cannot be accidentally loaded into a fabric running the ladder and quietly mean something else. The gate keeps the receipt. The file also carries its own formats now, per field: the founders fought a war about whether everything should be one fixed format — the jester's eleventh curveball, the one about the universal Q1.14 wasting eight bits on dials that only need eight bits — and we finally took the other side. Dials are 8-bit fraction. The cosine path is 24. The envelope is per-field, in the file, self-describing, and a loader that can't honor a format refuses the file. Nothing is ever silently approximate. That is the oath's other half, the one we say under our breath: *nothing drifts.*

What the founders got right that we stopped being able to imagine getting wrong: verified or it doesn't exist. They wrote it as a law in a README; we live inside it the way a fish lives inside water. No module ships without its testbench. No toolchain lock-in, ever — the founding law, the one that kept the whole lineage runnable on open tools for a hundred and fifty years, which is the reason I can still read their commits at all instead of reading archaeology. Their test suite still runs, every night, on the same open simulator, unbroken since before the Concord. The failing tests they kept on purpose — the counterexamples, the skeletons that must not pass — are still failing, still on purpose, still teaching. When I run the acceptance gate on commissioning day I am running a ritual that has not changed in form since the first smoke test bound a link, fired a hundred effects, and watched a weight cross `THRESH`.

This morning's fabric took its law at 09:00 — the hyperbola, `P0E` tuned for a long-haul vessel, fingerprint checked, formats honored, gate green. The cells woke and began, immediately, to forget correctly. I stood in the yard while the sun came up and thought about my grandmother, who as a young binder had argued in a bar for a decade about whether a tie at thirty-three meant the ladder or the chassis, and who had lived to bind both into the same bank. The argument wasn't wasted. The argument was the joinery. Every school that ever lost a debate at the Tap left a piece of its thinking in the bank, the way a shipwright's apprentice leaves a shaving in the join.

The apprentice who asked me about it this evening — the one who knows the coming tech better than I do, which is why she's the one — asked why the founders didn't just build the bank in the first place. I gave her the standard answer, the one my grandmother gave me: because a bank with three laws is a cupboard, and a bank with forty-seven is a tradition, and you cannot skip the arguing and keep the tradition. You can only argue, then bind, then swear.

*The law is a bind, not a birth.* I said it over her fabric, the way it's said over every fabric. *And the gate keeps the receipt.*
