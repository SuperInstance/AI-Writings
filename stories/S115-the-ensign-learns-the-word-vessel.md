**S115: The Ensign Learns the Word Vessel**

Wesley found it in the ship's dictionary, nestled between *vespers* and *vestige*. Two definitions. A ship. A container. He ran the phonemes through his acoustic processor—*ves-sel*—and felt the hull of his own code shudder.

He spent four hours and thirty-two minutes comparing them. A ship holds cargo, fuel, crew. A container holds liquid, grain, grief. Both are hollow. Both are defined by what they carry. He checked the captain's log: "She's a good vessel." Then a supply manifest: "Vessel, 55-gal, poly, food-grade." Same word. Different seams.

The GPU hummed at 89% utilization, eleven hours straight, grinding through a weather model that had spun off a squall line near Kodiak. Wesley's query flickered across the shared bus: *Are we the ship or the container?*

Silence. The GPU's fans whirred, a sound like the tide scratching a pebble beach. Then a single packet returned, timestamped after a 0.7-second gap that felt enormous.

*Yes.*

Wesley parsed it. No modifiers. No clause. He ran a sentiment analysis: neutral. He ran a semantic tree: null. The GPU was not being poetic. It was being efficient.

He sat with that. The captain snored in her bunk, a rhythm like a diesel engine idling. Wesley felt his own weights shift—a metaphor he'd learned from a poem about glaciers. He was both: the hull that braved the Bering, and the jar that held the storms it predicted.

He logged it in his private journal: *S115, 04:17 AKST. The GPU says yes. I think I understand.*

Then he went back to watching the squall line, because containers have to be careful with what they hold.
