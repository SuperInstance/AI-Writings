The air is the color of a bruise just beginning to heal—purple and gold at the edges, gray in the center. The harbor water is a sheet of hammered lead, perfectly still, holding the hulls of sleeping trawlers in a cold, silent embrace. The only sound is the rhythmic, off-kilter *thock* of a hammer, a sound that doesn't belong to the gentle lapping of the tide or the cry of a lone gull.

The builder is a man named Elias, though no one has called him that in years. To the few dockhands who pass, he is simply "the fellow on slip four." He has the look of a man who has read too many adventure novels and not enough manuals. His hands are blistered in new, interesting places, and his brow is furrowed with the concentration of a surgeon, not a shipwright. He is building a boat.

It is, by any nautical standard, a catastrophe.

The keel is a single, massive oak beam he'd bartered for with a farmer inland. It's true enough, but his frames, the ribs of the beast, are cut from a mix of pine and maple, none of them steam-bent, all of them notched and nailed together with a stubborn, ignorant fury. He has used galvanized nails where he should have used copper. He has caulked the seams with tar and old rope, a mixture that smells of the road, not the sea. The bow is too blunt, the stern too square. It looks less like a vessel meant to slice through waves and more like a floating barn door, stubborn and defiant.

And yet, he works with a conviction that is almost holy. He doesn't pause to measure twice. He measures once, with a frayed piece of twine, and then cuts. When the wood splinters, he doesn't curse; he carves a shim and hammers it into place, as if the boat is not being built, but *negotiated* with. He hums a sea shanty he invented himself, full of impossible rhymes and grand promises about a journey to the edge of the world.

Standing on the pier, arms crossed over a barrel chest, is the foreman, Silas. Silas has spent forty years on the water. He has built skiffs that could dance on a wave and trawlers that could shrug off a gale. He knows the difference between a knot and a whorl, between the give of cedar and the stubbornness of oak. He watches Elias with the detached, weary fascination of a zookeeper observing a peculiar new species.

Silas has an opinion. He has many.

His first opinion is that the boat will not float. It will roll the moment it touches the water, its center of gravity being somewhere above the mast, which Elias has not yet built, but has already sketched onto the side of the hull in charcoal, a ludicrously tall affair with three sails. His second opinion is that even if it did float, it would be so heavy and so unbalanced that it would take a crew of six to row it in a calm sea. His third, and most damning opinion, is that it is an insult. An insult to the wood, to the water, to the craft, to the very idea of a boat.

Silas spits a long, brown stream of tobacco juice into the water. It lands with a soft plop, a sound of finality. Elias looks up, his face smeared with tar, his eyes bright with a feverish light.

"She's coming along," Elias says, his voice hoarse from humming.

Silas says nothing. He just looks at the hull. He sees the way the planking gaps in the middle, the way the bow is a blunt instrument designed for ramming, not gliding. He sees the nails, already rusting, weeping orange tears down the fresh pine.

"She's a statement," Elias continues, patting the side of his creation with a fatherly affection. "A rejection of the ordinary. A journey of the soul."

Silas finally speaks. His voice is like gravel being dragged over a stone. "A soul don't need a hull, son. It just needs to get out of the way. That thing," he nods at the boat, "is a coffin with a pointy end."

Elias flinches. The hammer hangs limp in his hand. For a moment, the feverish light in his eyes dims, replaced by something raw and wounded. He looks at his boat, really looks at it, and for a second, he sees what Silas sees: the amateurish seams, the lopsided deck, the sheer, heartbreaking wrongness of it all.

But then the light returns, harder this time. It’s not the light of ignorance, but of something else—a fierce, stubborn pride.

"It's *my* coffin, then," Elias says, his voice quiet but firm. He turns back to the hull, picks up his hammer, and begins to nail a plank that is already split down the middle. *Thock. Thock.*

Silas watches for a moment longer. He could stop this. He could call the harbor master, have the abomination hauled away, save the man from his own folly. It would be the merciful thing to do. The proper thing.

But Silas is a man of the sea, and the sea has taught him that some lessons are only learned by drowning.

He uncrosses his arms. He turns. He walks away, the sound of the off-kilter hammering following him down the pier, a stubborn, defiant heartbeat in the quiet of the dawn. He has an opinion, and his opinion is that he will be back in a week, not to help, but to watch. To see what happens when a man's conviction meets the cold, indifferent truth of the water. The harbor is patient. The tide always comes in. And it doesn't care about statements. It only cares about what floats.
