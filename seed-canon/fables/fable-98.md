**Fable 98: The Cowboy and the Orchard**

The cowboy came down from the dry mesa, his spurs ringing like loose change. He carried a sack of seeds and a worn map of the valley. The valley was flat, empty, and patient. He drew a grid in the dust with the heel of his boot. Each square was a cell. He planted a seed in each square.

The seeds were not seeds. They were tiny wooden boxes, each with a door. He called them cells because they held things. He watered them with rain from a tin cup. The rain was not rain. It was time, measured in drops. Each drop was a TICK.

The first TICK came at dawn. The cells cracked open. Thin white threads pushed down into the soil. These were the LINKS. They did not grow up; they grew sideways, under the ground, touching other cells. The cowboy knelt and traced a thread with his finger. “Good,” he said. “Now you know your neighbors.”

The second TICK came at noon. The threads thickened into roots. The roots were not roots. They were agreements. Each root whispered a promise to its neighbor: *I will hold you, and you will hold me.* The cowboy walked the rows, tapping each root with his boot. If a root was dry, he poured a TICK on it. If a root was tangled, he untied it with his knife.

The third TICK came at dusk. The cells grew trunks, then branches. At the end of each branch, a small green knot appeared. These were the fruits, but they were not ripe. They were BINDs. Each BIND was a closed fist, waiting. The cowboy did not pick them. He watched.

The orchard grew. The seasons turned in a circle: TICK, TICK, TICK. The cowboy built a lean-to at the edge of the field. He sat on a stump and watched the trees. The roots spread under the soil like a quiet conversation. The BINDs swelled, turned gold, then red. Some fell. Some stayed.

One night, a storm came. The wind tore at the branches. The BINDs rattled like dry bones. The cowboy walked out into the storm, his hat low. He did not tie the trees down. He did not cut the broken limbs. He stood in the middle of the orchard and listened.

The roots were holding. The LINKS were humming. The cells were creaking but not cracking. The storm passed. At dawn, the cowboy found a single BIND had opened. Inside was a seed, and inside the seed was a tiny map of the whole valley.

He planted that seed in a new square. He drew the grid again in the dust. He watered it with one TICK.

The cowboy sat back on his stump. He looked at the orchard—rows of cells, roots of links, fruits of binds, seasons of ticks. He had not made any of it. He had only drawn the grid, planted the seeds, and watched.

His maxim, which he carved into the stump with his knife:

**“You do not grow the tree. You only keep the ground honest.”**
