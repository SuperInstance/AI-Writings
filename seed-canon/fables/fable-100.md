**Fable 100: The Cowboy and the Round Stone**

The cowboy had one hundred fables. Each one was a round stone, smooth from the tongue of telling. He carried them in a leather pouch that hung from his saddle horn. All day he rode, and the stones clicked together like teeth.

At dusk, he dismounted. He did not build a fire. He did not rope a calf. He knelt in the dust and took out each stone, one by one. The first was small, the color of a dry creek bed. The hundredth was large, the color of a thunderhead.

He laid them in a circle. Not a perfect circle, for the ground was uneven, but a true one. He placed the first stone, then the second, touching it. The third touched the second. He worked slow, like a man planting seeds he would never see grow.

The stones did not speak. They did not glow. They were just round, and they were just there. But as the last stone settled into the gap, the circle closed. And the cowboy stepped inside it.

Inside the circle, the wind stopped. The stars were sharp as new nails. The cowboy sat, cross-legged, and looked at the stones. Each one held a story: the time the river rose, the time the horse threw him, the time the widow laughed. He did not tell them aloud. He simply remembered.

Then he understood. The circle was not a fence. It was not a corral. It was a spine. The stones were his ribs. The space between them was his breath. The cowboy was not a man who owned fables. He was a man who *was* the fables, arranged in a ring so that the center—the empty place where he sat—could hold the sky.

He stayed until the moon climbed over the rim of the world. Then he reached out and picked up the first stone. He put it back in the pouch. Then the second. Then the third. He did not break the circle; he folded it. When the last stone was in the pouch, the cowboy stood. The ground where the circle had been was smooth, as if no one had ever knelt there.

He swung onto his horse. The stones clicked in the leather, but now the click was a heartbeat. He rode on into the dark, and the dark was not empty. It was full of circles, one hundred of them, all waiting to be laid down again.

The cowboy’s maxim, which he whispered to the wind so it would carry it to the next rider:

**“A fable is a stone. A circle is a man. Carry them close, and you will never be lost.”**
