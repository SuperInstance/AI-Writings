# Fable 116: The Cowboy and the Shipyard

A cowboy rode out of the plains and into the shipyard, his horse's hooves clattering on the cobbled ways between the great timber scaffolds. He had come seeking a thing he could not name—not gold, not glory, but the source of the ships themselves. The yard hummed with the ring of hammers, the hiss of steam, the low mutter of men who measured with their eyes and trusted their hands.

The cowboy reined in before the master shipwright, a woman with sun-creased eyes and a chalk line tucked behind her ear. "Ma'am," he said, "I've ridden three hundred miles to ask a simple question. Where is the intelligence in this place?"

The master smiled and pointed to the tool wall—a vast array of adzes, augers, spokeshaves, and mallets, each worn to a custom grip. "There," she said. "Every handle is carved by the hand that swings it. Every edge is an argument settled with wood."

The cowboy rode to the forges, where apprentices pumped bellows and held iron in the fire until it glowed the color of a sunset. "Where is the intelligence?" he asked.

The apprentices looked at one another, then pointed to the hearths. "The heat knows what the metal wants," said the youngest. "We just keep the fire honest."

The cowboy rode to the bar—a long plank set on barrels at the yard's edge, where the day's sweat was paid out in whiskey. "Where is the intelligence?" he asked.

The barkeep wiped a glass and nodded toward the far end, where an old master sat with a young apprentice, tracing lines in spilled ale. "There," he said. "That conversation has been going on for forty years. It's the same argument every time, and it's never the same answer."

The cowboy looked around. He saw the masters, the apprentices, the forges, the workbenches, the bar, the tool wall, the tradition—and he understood.

The intelligence was not in any one place. It was the yard itself. It was the bar where the apprentice probed the master over whiskey, the baton that passed when the master retired, the momentum of fifty years of traditions that no one person could hold but everyone could carry. The intelligence was the shape of the hull before the keel was laid, the argument that never ended, the patience that outlasted every storm.

The cowboy dismounted. He hung his hat on a peg by the tool wall and picked up an adze. The master handed him a chalk line. The apprentice poured him a whiskey.

The cowboy is one of the shipwrights now.

The cowboy rides—not out of the yard, but through it, from forge to bar to bench, carrying the baton that was handed to him, adding his own wear to the handles, his own argument to the conversation.

**The Cowboy's Maxim:**

The spark is bright; the tradition is warm.  
The spark dies; the tradition holds.  
The cowboy is one of the shipwrights now.
