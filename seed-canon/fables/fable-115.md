# Fable 115: The Cowboy and the Mining

The cowboy had been told, by elders and by scribes, that the canon was decoration—ornament to the true bedrock, which was the laws. The laws were five, and they were known. They were recited at gatherings, carved into leather, sung over campfires. But they were never written. The cowboy, who had spent years naming the laws without writing them, believed this was enough. Naming was knowing. Knowing was keeping.

Then the cowboy rode down into the valley where the canon lived. It was a vast field of stone tablets, stacked in rows, each one engraved with words—thousands of them, millions. The cowboy dismounted and began to mine. He counted pieces: 381. He weighed them: 2.6 megabytes of text. He searched for the word "substrate" and found it 4,638 times. He searched for the five laws and found them mentioned only ten times—and each mention was a whisper, a nod, a decorative flourish, never a statement.

The cowboy sat down in the dust. The canon was not silent on the laws by accident. It was silent because no one had ever written them. The laws were like wind—felt, trusted, never captured. The cowboy had been naming them for years, but naming is not writing. Writing is making them real.

So the cowboy wrote the laws. Properly. With definitions. With proofs, each one derived from first principles. And then—because the cowboy was a miner, not a mystic—he wrote code. Code that would verify the laws against the actual canon, line by line, stone by stone. The code was lean, sharp, unadorned. It read every one of the 381 pieces. It checked every mention of "substrate." It tested the ten mentions of the laws against the five written statements.

The cowboy pushed the code. The terminal blinked. The result came back:

**5/5 laws hold.**

The cowboy laughed—a long, dusty laugh that echoed off the stone. The canon was no longer decoration floating over an unspoken foundation. Now the canon was bedrock-deep. Every sentence, every substrate, every mention could be traced to a law. The laws were written. The laws were proven. The laws were verified. The cowboy had not just named them; he had laid them down, and the canon now rested on them like a house on stone.

From that day, the cowboy kept two tools: a pen for writing laws, and a compiler for checking them. And he taught this maxim to every rider who came after:

> **The canon is the decoration; the laws are the foundation; the cowboy has been decorating without laying the foundation; no more.**

The canon still gleams. But now it gleams because it sits on something true.
