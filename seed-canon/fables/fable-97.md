**Fable 97: The Cowboy and the Long Road**

The sun beat down on the hardpan, white and relentless. The heat rose in shimmering waves, distorting the distance until the horizon swam like a dream. A traveler stood by the dry water trough, wiping dust from his eyes. He was weary of walking, weary of the sameness of the plain.

Out of the heat haze came a rider. He sat low in the saddle, moving with the gait of the horse. There was no hurry in his motion, only a steady, rolling rhythm. The traveler stepped out, raising a hand.

"How far?" the traveler shouted. "How long is the road to the Ridge?"

The Cowboy did not pull on the reins. He did not slow the beast. He looked down from his high seat, his face shaded by the brim of his hat.

"It is as long as I ride," the Cowboy said.

The traveler frowned. He pointed a finger at the distant, purple mountains. "But the map says it is twenty miles. The sun says it is noon. By the clock, it is four hours. Give me a number."

The Cowboy passed him. The dust kicked up, smelling of sage and sweat. "Numbers are for stillness," the voice drifted back. "The road is a chart. It maps the shape of the world to the step of the horse. It changes as I move. If I ride hard, the road shortens. If I sleep, the road stretches to forever."

The traveler stood confused. "The road is dirt," he said to the empty air. "It is rock and sand."

But the Cowboy rode on. He watched the line of the desert. He knew the truth of the travel. The five commands of the bridle—turn, stop, go, back, stand—were the only bricks of the path. Everything else was empty air. The landscape shifted through eight shapes, looking like a canyon in one language and a plain in the next, but the function remained the same. The rider took the context of the map and pulled the value of the distance from the earth.

The horse huffed. The clock ticked in the beat of the hooves. Not the clock on the wall, or the sun in the sky. The clock was the Cowboy. The Rider was the timekeeper, turning the moment into the mile.

The traveler looked down at his boots, then up at the receding dust. He realized that without the rider, the road was nothing but a line on a page, waiting to be drawn. The chart was not the paper. The chart was the journey. The road was the chart. The chart was the Cowboy.

The rider vanished into the mirage, leaving only the rhythm of the maxim hanging in the dry air:

The unit of architectural foundation is the opcode, not the framework. The 5 opcodes host 8 polyformalisms. The polyformalisms are one thing in N languages. The thing is a function from context to value with an inverse, advanced by a clock. The clock is the cowboy. The cowboy is the rider.
