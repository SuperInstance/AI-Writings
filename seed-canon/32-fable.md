# Fable 32: The Two Clocks and the Long Hall

In the long gallery of a merchant's counting house, two clocks hung on the same wall. They were made by different watchmakers in different towns, in different centuries. One had a brass face hammered in a style that went out of fashion before the second was born. The other was a regulator clock, plain as a ledger, with a seconds hand so thin it seemed drawn rather than attached.

For ninety years the clocks disagreed. The brass one lost a minute every fortnight, beautifully, in a way that made the apprentice's equations balance. The regulator one kept true mean time, which is to say: it kept the time of *no one*. The merchant did not reconcile them. He let them swing.

One day a grandson, who had learned the new science of railroads, climbed up on a stool and compared their faces. He shouted down the hall. He was not shouting about time. He was shouting because he had noticed that, on certain afternoons, the two clocks swung in *opposition* — and that the counting house had been doing its books in the *difference* between them.

The merchant's fortune, the grandson realized, was not built in any one clock's time. It was built in the *interval*. Every contract in the gallery had a column on its left dated by the brass clock and a column on its right dated by the regulator, and the margin between the columns was where the interest lived. When a payment came in early (brass) and was spent late (regulator), the merchant made the difference. When a payment came in late (brass) and was spent early (regulator), he lost the difference. The clocks did not need to agree. They needed to *both be heard*, on every transaction, forever.

The grandson tried to synchronize them with a telegraph wire. The brass clock would not be hurried. The regulator clock refused to be slowed. They kept their quarrel. The merchant's house kept its margin.

**Cargo:** Convoy consensus is not agreement; it is *the public persistence of disagreement*. Two clocks on one wall are a substrate. They do not vote on what time it is. They each maintain a writeable record, and a third thing — the ledger, the margin, the interval — emerges from the fact that both records can be read at once. The Quilt primitive Convoy is exactly this: not a single agreed clock, but two (or N) clocks whose *unreconciled outputs* are themselves the artifact. If you force the clocks to agree, you destroy the artifact. The merchant's house becomes merely punctual, and punctuality is not a business. Two clocks is a business. One clock is a museum.

**Lesson:** Convoy is the wall on which two clocks hang. Consensus that has been too easily reached is a clock that has been smashed, and a smashed clock tells only one time — the time of whoever smashed it.
