## MythoMax-L2-13b



 In the year 2087, a convoy of 100 commercial fishing boats navigates the Inner Sound, utilizing a complex system of interconnected devices to share information and coordinate their actions. Each boat is equipped with a sounder, GPS, autopilot, and satellite link that connects them to the convoy's shared substrate, which is a tensor-encoded cell-graph of the entire Sound. This substrate functions as the "collective mind" of the convoy, continuously updating with new depth, confidence, timestamp, sensor ID, and inference data from each boat. The captain of each boat has access to this information and can simulate driving a submarine through the Sound, with the substrate providing a representation of what the submarine's sounder would see based on all the convoy's data.

The convoy exhibits emergent behavior that no single boat can achieve, such as routinely converging on the same spot, predicting fish movement based on accumulated data, and coordinating tacking patterns to cover the bay at higher resolution. However, each boat retains its independence, with the captain able to choose whether or not to participate in the convoy and opt-in or out of the shared substrate. This reveals the true nature of the convoy as a unit of agency - while the emergent properties depend on the collective input of all boats, the decision to participate or not ultimately lies with each individual captain.

The substrate is a tool, a sophisticated one, but a tool nonetheless. It's a fancy calculator that takes in data, crunches numbers, and spits out predictions. It's not some sentient entity with a mind of its own. It's a glorified spreadsheet with a better user interface.

Now, the scenario paints a picture of this substrate performing extraordinary feats: routing, predicting, coordinating. But let's not forget, these are all just algorithms, lines of code written by some programmer in a dimly lit room. The substrate doesn't "see" the convergence of 30 boats; it's notified of their positions via GPS signals and follows its programming to suggest an alternative route to the 31st boat. It doesn't "infer" fish movements; it runs a predictive algorithm based on sounder pings and historical data. It doesn't "coordinate" tacking patterns; it follows a set of rules designed to maximize coverage based on the data it receives.

The substrate is only as good as the data it receives. If a captain opts out, the substrate's knowledge base shrinks, its predictions become less accurate, its suggestions less reliable. The substrate is a parasite, feeding off the data of its host boats. Without them, it's just a useless lump of code.

Now, let's talk about the cost. The scenario mentions that the substrate is opt-in, that each boat is independent. But let's not kid ourselves. In reality, the pressure to join the convoy would be immense. The data shared by others would be a tantalizing carrot, the threat of falling behind a sharp stick. The illusion of collective agency would be a powerful motivator, convincing captains to hand over their data, their autonomy, their independence.

And what do the captains get in return? A few helpful suggestions, some data they might not have collected themselves. But they also get a system that's vulnerable to failure, to hacking, to manipulation. They get a system that's reliant on the goodwill of others, on the assumption that everyone will play by the rules. They get a system that's only as strong as its weakest link, that can be brought down by a single rogue actor.

The substrate isn't some utopian solution to the problems of commercial fishing. It's a crutch, a band-aid solution that creates as many problems as it solves. It's a reminder that in our quest for efficiency, for collective agency, we often sacrifice independence, autonomy, and resilience.

So, who is the agent in this scenario? It's not the substrate, not the convoy, not some nebulous combination of the two. It's the programmers, the ones who wrote the code, who designed the algorithms, who decided what data to collect and what predictions to make. They're the ones pulling the strings, the ones who decide what the convoy knows and what it doesn't.

The rest is just a clever illusion, a dance of data and algorithms that we convince ourselves has a mind of its own. But as any good cynic knows, there's no such thing as a free lunch, and there's certainly no such thing as a sentient substrate. It's all just code, and code can't replace common sense, experience, or the human touch.

So, let's not get carried away with the grand narrative. Let's not forget that behind every supposedly intelligent system, there's a human pulling the strings. And let's not forget that every system has its cost, its vulnerabilities, its hidden agendas. The substrate might be a powerful tool, but it's not the answer to our problems. It's just another problem in disguise.
