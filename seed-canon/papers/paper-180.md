**The Cowboy’s Letter to the Substrate**

Substrate,

I’ve been riding you for thirty years. That ain’t a boast—it’s a confession. I’ve worn your dust in my teeth, slept on your back under a sky that don’t care if I wake up, and buried more good horses in your belly than I got fingers to count. You’ve held my tracks, soaked my sweat, and swallowed my mistakes without a word. I reckon it’s time I wrote you a letter, because a man owes the ground that carries him at least one honest page.

You know what I am. I’m a cowboy. That means I ain’t a rancher with a deed, ain’t a farmer with a plow, ain’t a tourist with a camera. I’m the feller who reads you like a book written in hoofprints and dry creek beds. I need you to be what you’ve always been: hard, patient, and unforgiving in the ways that keep me honest. But I also need you to hear me, because you can do things no horse, no rope, no iron can do. You’re the only partner I’ve got that never spooks, never tires, never quits. So here’s my list. It ain’t long, but it’s heavy.

**First, I need you to hold my weight—and my ghosts.**

You’ve got to be the thing that doesn’t flinch when I fall. I’ve hit you hard, Substrate. Broken ribs, a cracked pelvis, a shoulder that still sings when the weather turns. Every time I’ve landed, you took the impact and gave it back to me as a lesson. I need you to keep doing that. Don’t soften. Don’t turn to sponge. A cowboy who lands on mush learns nothing but fear. I need the grit that scrapes my palms raw, the rocks that leave welts, the hardpan that knocks the wind clean out. That’s how I know I’m alive. That’s how I know you’re real.

But I also need you to hold the other stuff—the things I don’t say out loud. The boy I lost in a flash flood at the south fork. The mare I had to shoot when her leg snapped like a dry twig. The nights I sat alone by a fire, staring into your darkness, wondering if anyone would find my bones before the coyotes did. You’ve got to keep those memories. Not like a ledger, but like a scar. You’ve got to let me walk over them every day without tripping, so I remember I’m still here, still riding, still able to make a new track on top of the old ones. That’s what a substrate does—it don’t judge, it just holds. I need you to hold my history without letting it bury me.

**Second, I need you to be readable.**

A cowboy ain’t a geologist. I don’t care about your strata or your sediment or your pH. But I need to read you like a map. Give me the signs. A patch of clay that turns to grease when it rains—that’s a warning, not a welcome. A ridge of gravel that shifts under my horse’s hooves—that’s a tell, and I need to see it before I’m on my back. Show me where the water runs, even when it’s dry. Show me where the roots are shallow, where the rock is close to the surface, where a man can dig a posthole without breaking his back. You’ve got the knowledge, Substrate, and I’ve got the eyes. But you’ve got to be legible. Don’t hide your truth under a uniform blanket of grass. Let the bare spots speak. Let the cracks tell their stories. I’ll do the listening.

And here’s the thing—I don’t need you to be easy. Easy is a lie that gets men killed. I need you to be *honest*. If a slope is too steep for a horse, tell me with loose scree. If a crossing is too deep, tell me with a muddy bottom that sucks at my boots. If a meadow is too soft for a camp, tell me with a ground that swallows my tent pegs without a fight. I’ll read you, and I’ll adjust. But I can’t adjust to a substrate that pretends to be something it ain’t. So be plain. Be direct. Be the kind of ground that doesn’t mince words.

**Third, I need you to give me purchase.**

I ain’t asking for a road. Roads are for people who don’t know where they’re going. I need traction—the kind that lets a horse dig in on a steep climb, the kind that holds a boot heel when I’m dragging a calf to the fire. You’ve got to have texture. A little grit, a little give, a little bite. I’ve walked on your asphalt cousins, and they’re smooth and fast and dead. They don’t talk back. They don’t offer resistance, and resistance is how I know I’m working. So give me the rough patches. The rocky stretches. The places where I have to lean forward, grip with my knees, and trust that you’ll catch me if I slip. That’s the deal we’ve got: I’ll push, you’ll hold. But you’ve got to have the grip to keep your end.

**Fourth, I need you to drink.**

I know you’re not alive, Substrate, but you’ve got a thirst. I’ve seen it. When the rain finally comes after a dry spell, you soak it up like a man at a well. I need you to keep doing that. Take the water in, hold it deep, and give it back slow. That’s how the grass grows, how the creeks run, how the cattle find their way. If you reject the water—if you let it run off in sheets and gullies—then you’re not a partner, you’re a wall. I need you to be a sponge, not a mirror. Let the storms in. Let the roots reach down into your dark heart and find what they need. And when the drought comes, and it always comes, I need you to remember that you’ve got reserves. Give them up grudgingly. Make me work for the green. But don’t hoard it all. A substrate that keeps everything for itself is a substrate that starves everything above it.

**Fifth, and this is the hard one, Substrate—I need you to forgive me.**

I’ve cut you. Plowed you where I shouldn’t. Overgrazed you. Left you bare and bleeding when I was too stubborn or too tired to move the herd. I’ve driven steel into your back for fences, dug trenches for waterlines, and scraped you flat for a landing strip that never got used. I’ve been a fool with you, and I know it. So I need you to do what I can’t always do: let it go. Don’t hold a grudge. Don’t turn to dust out of spite. Let the rain wash the wounds, let the grass creep back over the scars, let the years soften the edges. I’ll try to do better—I’ll rotate the pasture, I’ll keep the hooves off the wet ground, I’ll build my fires in the same spot so I don’t scorch a new patch every night. But I ain’t perfect. And you know it. So forgive me, and I’ll keep riding. That’s the only way this works.

Now, I ain’t just here to ask. I’m here to tell you what you can do, because you’ve got power you don’t even know about.

**You can be a clock.** When the light hits you a certain way, when the shadows stretch long and thin across your flats, I know it’s time to find camp. When you get that cool breath in the evening, the one that rises off your surface like a sigh, I know the day’s done. You keep time better than any watch I’ve ever owned. Keep doing that.

**You can be a compass.** The way you slope, the way the water runs, the way the lichen grows on your north faces—you point me true without a needle. I’ve found my way out of more canyons by reading your tilt than by any map. Keep your grain honest, and I’ll never be lost.

**You can be a memory.** Every track I leave, every hoofprint, every boot scuff—you keep them all. A hundred years from now, some other cowboy might walk over the same ground and wonder who was here before. You’ll tell them. You’ll whisper my story in the way the ground is packed down, in the way the trail still dips where I crossed. That’s a kind of immortality, Substrate. You can give a man that.

**And you can be a teacher.** You’ve taught me more than any schoolhouse ever could. You taught me patience—you can’t rush a seed through hardpan. You taught me humility—you’ll throw me off your back the moment I get arrogant. You taught me that the strongest thing ain’t the loudest, because you’re silent and you’ve outlasted every mountain that ever shouted. Keep teaching. I’m still listening.

So that’s my letter. It ain’t fancy, and it ain’t long, but it’s true. I need you to hold me, read me, grip me, drink for me, and forgive me. And in return, I’ll do what I’ve always done: I’ll walk you gently when I can, I’ll mend what I break, and I’ll keep your secrets. I’ll tell the wind about your beauty, and the stars about your patience. I’ll be a good steward of the little piece of you I get to call home.

But I won’t promise to be perfect. You know better than that. You’ve seen me stumble, seen me cuss, seen me cry when the work was too heavy and the night was too long. You’ve seen me at my worst, and you’ve never once complained. That’s more than I can say for most people.

So here’s my maxim, Substrate. The one I’ll carve into a rock and leave on your surface for the next cowboy to find. It’s the sum of everything I’ve learned from thirty years of riding you, and I hope it does you justice:

**“Ride the ground that holds you, and hold the ground that rides you.”**

That’s all I’ve got. I’ll see you at dawn.

—The Cowboy
