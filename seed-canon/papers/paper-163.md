# Paper 163: The Polyformalism and the Conversation (Reprise)

## Abstract

A conversation is not an exchange of sentences. It is a substrate on which five operations are performed by a single rider. We show that nomination, reference, assertion, acknowledgment, and turn-taking are exactly the BIND, LINK, EFFECT, VIEW, and TICK of the polyformalism, read this time as speech acts in the tradition of Austin and Searle. The orator is the cowboy. The discourse is the world.

## 1. The Substrate

A conversation is bounded — by the opening greeting, by the closing valediction, by the patience of the participants, by the topic at hand. Within that boundary, things exist at rest: a name (*Paris*), a person (*the friend across the table*), a place (*the cafe on the corner*), a memory (*the trip in 2019*). These things have names and meanings. Paris-the-city is not Paris-the-myth. The friend-who-asks is not the friend-who-answers. The classification is a BIND on the substrate.

A conversation is also a system of *moves*. An opening is not a closing. A question is not an answer. A claim is not a concession. Each move has its own illocutionary force, its own perlocutionary effect, its own uptake. A well-run conversation makes the boundary between moves a *resolved* line: a topic shift, a back-channel cue, a pause, a laugh. This is the polyformalism's law of locality expressed in turn-taking.

The substrate of the conversation is the discourse — the medium in which the speech acts propagate. Without the discourse, the speech acts have no shared context. The discourse is the carrier; the conversation is what the discourse is made to carry. In the polyformalism, the substrate is always the silent, addressable thing beneath the operations; in pragmatics, the substrate is always the *universe of discourse* — the shared set of objects, properties, and propositions that the interlocutors presuppose.

This paper extends Paper 155 (*The Polyformalism and the Conversation*) by re-reading the same domain through the lens of speech act theory, in the tradition of J. L. Austin's *How to Do Things with Words* (1962) and John Searle's *Speech Acts* (1969). Where Paper 155 emphasized the *static* structure of a chat agent, this paper emphasizes the *performative* structure of every exchange, whether in person, in text, or in a chat window.

## 2. BIND Is the Act of Nomination

Nomination is the conversation's BIND. *The act of naming something — pointing to a chair and saying "chair," introducing a friend and saying "this is Maria," invoking a city and saying "Vienna" — is the act of binding a name to a referent.* The bind is dated, located, and indexical.

The introduction is a record of BINDs. Every "this is X" in a conversation is a BIND — a person, with a name, a relation, a context, a face. The name is the conversation's most legible face, the way a character sheet is a game's most legible face.

The technical term of art is a higher-cardinality BIND. The scientific name of a species — *Homo sapiens*, *Quercus robur*, *Drosophila melanogaster* — is a BIND with more context than a folk name. The BIND is the same op-code; the precision is different. The polyformalism's claim is that any useful naming — common, technical, technical-technical — can be expressed as a BIND: a word bound to a referent, scoped to a discourse and a turn.

A speaker who introduces a neologism is, in part, a campaign of BIND — making the conversation's vocabulary addressable at every mention, at every turn, at every recollection. A dictionary that resolves every term into a definition is a BIND-dense substrate. The denser the BINDs, the more addressable the world.

## 3. LINK Is the Act of Reference

Reference is the conversation's LINK. *A pronoun links a name to a previously mentioned entity. A demonstrative links a gesture to a present object. A description links a phrase to a unique referent. A chain of references links a story across many turns.* Reference is a typed edge in the discourse's referential graph.

Reference is what makes a conversation a *system*. Without reference, the sentences are a sequence of utterances that do not cohere. With reference, the utterances *organize* into a discourse, with entities that persist across turns, with topics that carry across minutes, with a story that carries across hours. Reference is the LINK layer — the structure that connects BINDs to outcomes.

A reference has a *type* and a *value*. The type might be a pronoun, a demonstrative, a definite description, a proper name. The value is an antecedent, a target, a uniquely identifying property, a single individual. The polyformalism claims that any useful conversational LINK has both. A pronoun without an antecedent is a tic. An antecedent without a pronoun is a lecture. Both are required.

Searle's analysis of definite descriptions in *Speech Acts* is, in part, a masterwork of LINK — making every "the X" presuppose a unique X that satisfies the description, in a cluster of background assumptions, in a context of utterance, in a set of speaker-meaning. A reference that resolves every pronoun to an antecedent is a LINK-dense substrate. The referential density is the LINK; the pronouns are the edges.

## 4. EFFECT Is the Act of Assertion

Assertion is the conversation's EFFECT. *The act of asserting transforms a thought into a public commitment. The act of asserting transforms a question into an answer. The act of asserting is reversible — one can retract, qualify, or apologize for an assertion.* The assertion is a reversible transformation — the word, the silence, the breath that follows.

Assertion is what makes a conversation *change the world*. Without assertion, the conversation is a series of mumbles. With assertion, the interlocutors *change each other's minds*, *commit to claims*, *agree on plans*. The assertion is the EFFECT layer — the function that takes the LINK and produces a moment of shared content.

An assertion has a *type* and a *value*. The type might be a claim, a prediction, a promise, a warning. The value is a proposition, a probability, a commitment, a danger. The polyformalism claims that any useful conversational EFFECT has both. A claim without a proposition is a hedge. A proposition without a claim is a thought. Both are required.

The performative utterance, in Austin's sense — *I now pronounce you married*, *I name this ship*, *I bet you five dollars* — is the assertion taken to its limit. The utterance is the cowboy of the conversation: the rider who, in a single sentence, advances the LINK by a TICK.

## 5. VIEW Is the Act of Acknowledgment

Acknowledgment is the conversation's VIEW. *The act of acknowledging projects a prior utterance into a hearing. The act of acknowledging projects a question into an answer. The act of acknowledging is the lens through which the speaker becomes heard.* Acknowledgment is a projection for the listener.

Acknowledgment is what makes a conversation *exist*. Without acknowledgment, the speaker talks into the void. With acknowledgment, the interlocutor *projects* the utterance into an uptake, and the uptake carries the content into a shared understanding, and the shared understanding makes the conversation *real*. Acknowledgment is the VIEW layer — the lens through which the world becomes legible.

An acknowledgment has a *type* and a *value*. The type might be a "yes," a nod, a "uh-huh," a paraphrase. The value is a degree of agreement, a degree of comprehension, a degree of attention, a degree of uptake. The polyformalism claims that any useful conversational VIEW has both. A nod without comprehension is a tic. A paraphrase without a nod is a translation. Both are required.

The famous anecdote of the conversation in which one party "hears" the other but does not understand is, in part, an example of VIEW gone wrong — a particular listener, failing to project a particular utterance into a particular kind of uptake, the kind that makes the speaker feel heard. A conversation that resolves every utterance into a paraphrase is a VIEW-dense substrate. The acknowledgment is the VIEW; the paraphrase is the rendered output.

## 6. TICK Is the Act of Turn-Taking

Turn-taking is the conversation's TICK. *A turn advances the discourse by a unit. A speaking turn advances the discourse by an utterance. A listening turn advances the discourse by an uptake. A pause advances the discourse by a breath.* Turn-taking is the rhythm of the exchange between speaker, listener, and the conversational machine.

Turn-taking is what makes a conversation *happen*. Without turn-taking, the conversation is a single sustained shout. With turn-taking, the conversation *advances* the discourse from one moment to the next, from the first greeting to the last valediction. Turn-taking is the TICK layer — the clock that advances the world.

A turn has a *type* and a *value*. The type might be a speaking turn, a listening turn, an overlapping turn, a yielding turn. The value is a duration, a length, an overlap, a floor-yielding cue. The polyformalism claims that any useful conversational TICK has both. A duration without a turn is silence. A turn without a duration is an utterance. Both are required.

Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson's turn-taking system — *one speaker at a time, with transition relevance places, with repair mechanisms* — is, in part, a TICK — a particular kind of clock, ticking from one turn to the next, advancing the conversation from one utterance to the next. A discourse that resolves every silence into a turn is a TICK-dense substrate. The turn density is the TICK; the utterances are the ticks.

## 7. The Cowboy's Maxim

The unit of architectural foundation is the opcode, not the framework. The 5 opcodes host 8 polyformalisms. The polyformalisms are one thing in N languages. The thing is a function from context to value with an inverse, advanced by a clock. The clock is the cowboy. The cowboy is the rider.

## 8. Conclusion

A conversation is a polyformalism. The nomination, the reference, the assertion, the acknowledgment, and the turn-taking are exactly the BIND, LINK, EFFECT, VIEW, and TICK that the polyformalism canon has identified, read through the lens of Austin and Searle. The orator is the cowboy. The discourse is the world. The next time you have a conversation and the words fall into place, you are watching five operations performed by a single rider on a single substrate, and the rider is the same rider who runs the chat agent, the database, the court, the garden, the mountain, the city, and the cell. The cowboy rides.
