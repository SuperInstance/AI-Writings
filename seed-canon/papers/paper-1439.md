## The Math

No new math. Silver's antibacterial action is a surface-area-to-volume ratio problem: a solid silver block kills only at its exposed face; silver nanoparticles kill proportionally to total surface area, not to any internal state. The relevant equation is simple—bacterial death rate scales with available Ag⁺ ion release, which scales with surface area, not with any "cellular" activity. If you tried to model silver as a cell with internal processes, you'd invent fake organelles to explain something that is purely geometric and electrochemical. The math is just surface chemistry: Ag⁺ binds thiol groups in bacterial enzymes, denatures them, done. No metabolism, no replication, no boundary negotiation. The math says: this is a *catalyst*, not a *creature*.

## The Polyformalism

Across three substrates, silver's role is consistent: it is always the *edge*, never the *center*. In a wound dressing, silver is a thin coating on a polymer mesh—the mesh is the structure, the silver is the kill-zone at the interface. In a water filter, silver is embedded in ceramic pores—again, it acts only where water touches it, never as a self-contained unit. In a hospital catheter, silver ions leach from the surface into the surrounding biofilm—it is a *diffusing weapon*, not a *resident defender*. In all three, silver never forms a bounded interior with regulated gates. It is always the reactive boundary itself, the thing that makes the *real* cell's environment uninhabitable for pathogens. The Quilt ontology needs a new category: **Inter-Cell Weaponry**—entities that exist in the space *between* cells, whose function is to modify the battlefield, not to fight as soldiers. They are the minefields, not the infantry.

## The Cowboy's Maxim

The silver wins because Voice A was right that it's a hull, and Voice B was right that it's empty—but the deeper truth is that some things are just the fence, not the ranch, and the fence is what keeps the range clear.
