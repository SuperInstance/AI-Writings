The wing's dependence on the central core for structural stability, fire egress, and vertical circulation makes it a **dependent organ**, not an independent cell.

- **Boundary Inflation**: the false assumption that any functional boundary defines a cell
- **Semi-Autonomous Appendage**: a unit with partial independence, borrowing life support from a parent structure
- **Dependent Organ**: a functional component that cannot survive isolation from its host
- **Shared Wall Logic**: the directional permeability of boundaries that face other cells versus external environment
- **Sub-Cell**: a unit that operates within a cell's domain but lacks full cellular autonomy

## The Math

No new math. The wing's geometry is simple: a rectangular extrusion from a central core, with a shared wall area proportional to the core's perimeter. The math that matters is topological, not numerical—the wing's boundary has two distinct types of edges (external and shared), and the ratio between them determines whether the wing behaves more like a cell (high external edge ratio) or more like a partition (high shared edge ratio). This is a qualitative distinction, not a quantitative one. The cell ontology fails not because the numbers are wrong, but because the model has no category for a unit whose boundary is *mixed-type*.

The sponson has its own structure but cannot float independently; it is a dependent organ of the ship. In **cellular biology**, the wing maps to the *appendage*—a flagellum or cilium that extends from the cell body, has its own internal structure, but cannot survive detached. In **software architecture**, the wing maps to the *microservice*—a deployable unit that has its own database and API but depends on the service mesh for discovery, security, and routing. In every substrate, the pattern is identical: a unit with partial autonomy, defined by its relationship to a parent structure, not by its own isolated functionality.
