## The Frontier

The contradiction is not about whether an impulse *is* a cell. It's about the Quilt ontology's hidden assumption that a cell's membrane must be drawn in space. Voice A found the temporal wall—Δt as the casing—and that's real. Voice B found the persistence problem—an impulse doesn't sit still long enough to be inventoried—and that's also real. Both are right, and both are wrong, because they're arguing about whether a *wave* is a *particle* when the actual answer is that the impulse is a **cell whose membrane is the event horizon of a transaction**. The cell ontology fails here not because the impulse lacks boundaries, but because the Quilt has no word for a cell that *exists only while it is firing*. A cell that cannot be observed at rest is not a cell in the old sense—it's a **trigger-cell**, a unit whose entire being is the act of crossing its own threshold. The impulse is not a thing that has a force; it is a thing that *is* the force, and the force is the cell's only anatomy.

## The 5 Gold Terms

1. **The Temporal Membrane** — the integral limits t₁ to t₂, the only wall that holds.
2. **The Payload Spore** — momentum that outlives the wall, the seed scattered past Δt.
3. **The Trigger-Cell** — a cell whose existence is coterminous with its firing; no rest state, no inventory.
4. **The Coasting Interval** — the neighbor space before and after, where the force is absent but its effect persists.
5. **The Firing Substrate** — the medium (axon, wire, muscle, railgun) through which the impulse propagates, which *lends* it a body.

## The Math

No new math—but the old math was misread. Voice A pointed to J = ∫F dt from t₁ to t₂ and called the limits a membrane. Voice B said the impulse is a transient event, not a persistent unit. Both are looking at the same equation. The resolution is that the impulse is a **boundary integral**: it is not a volume with a surface, but a surface with a volume *inside the integration*. The cell's "contents" are not a substance but a flux—the force density over the interval. When you integrate, you get momentum change, which is the *offspring*. The impulse is a cell whose cytoplasm is a flow, whose nucleus is the force vector, and whose membrane is the interval itself. It divides by splitting Δt into subintervals, each carrying its share of the integral. The math doesn't break—it just demands you stop thinking of cells as static containers and start thinking of them as **closed integrals over time**. The impulse is a cell in the same way a photon is a particle: it has a definite energy, a definite interval, and it cannot be slowed down or examined without destroying it.

## The Polyformalism

Across substrates, the trigger-cell shows its face differently but keeps its shape. In a **neuron**, the impulse is an action potential—a voltage spike that travels down the axon, its membrane being the myelinated segment that bounds the depolarization wave. In a **muscle fiber**, it's the calcium release that couples excitation to contraction—the impulse is the trigger, the contraction is the payload spore. In a **wire**, it's the current pulse that carries a signal—the impulse is the bit, the wire is the substrate, and the membrane is the clock cycle that defines the bit's duration. In a **railgun**, it's the Lorentz force pushing a projectile—the impulse is the electromagnetic push, the projectile is the momentum that outlives the wall. In every case, the same structure holds: a bounded interval of force, a substrate that conducts it, and a payload that persists after the membrane closes. The Quilt doesn't need a new category—it needs to recognize that **some cells are born, fire, and die in the same breath**, and that's not a failure of cellhood, it's the highest form of it.

## The Cowboy's Maxim

A cell ain't what it holds—it's what it does with the wall while the wall's still up.
