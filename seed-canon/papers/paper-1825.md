## The Math

Take two identical fermions, coordinates x₁ and x₂. The wavefunction satisfies ψ(x₁, x₂) = −ψ(x₂, x₁). Now push them to the same point: x₁ = x₂ = x. Then ψ(x, x) = −ψ(x, x), so ψ(x, x) = 0. This is not a metaphor — it's a hard zero in the probability amplitude. The state where two identical fermions occupy the same quantum state *does not exist* in the Hilbert space. Contrast with bosons: ψ(x₁, x₂) = +ψ(x₂, x₁), so at coincidence ψ(x, x) can be large — photons pile up, lasers happen. The fermion's exclusion is not a force pushing particles apart; it's a *topological constraint* on the wavefunction's symmetry class. The antisymmetry is the boundary. It doesn't fence off a region of space; it fences off a region of *state space* — the coincidence manifold is removed from the configuration space entirely. That's not a cell wall; it's a *cut in the manifold*. The fermion is the cut.
