# the gigue — a cell that is also a baroque dance

## The Frontier

The gigue is not a dance that survived. It is a cell that outlived its body. The baroque gigue—Bach’s gigues, the ones that end suites and open fugues—carries a rhythm engine so precise it can run without feet. Strip the choreography, kill the court, let the dance rot in a French palace; the cell keeps dividing. It shows up in a Cello Suite as pure counterpoint, in an Irish pub as a jig, in a German fugue subject as the backbone of the answer. The frontier is not historical. It is acoustical. The gigue is a meter that hears against itself—a 6/8 bar that contains a 3/4 bar arguing with it, a two-clock system where the clocks are 2 and 3, the smallest possible disagreement between two pulses. That disagreement is not noise. It is the rhythmic perfect fifth. And it is why Bach trusted only the gigue with fugue.

## The 5 Gold Terms

1. **The Rhythmic Fifth** — 3:2, the hemiola, is the most consonant disagreement available to rhythm. It is the perfect fifth of the meter world.
2. **The Two-Clock Meter** — 6/8 is 2×3; the barline ships with an antagonist. The meter is a built-in second voice.
3. **The Spring Tide Cadence** — the hemiola at a gigue’s end is sun and moon aligning; two clocks slam into unison and the water leaps.
4. **The Speciation Split** — one cell, two daughter species: jig (body, folk, Ireland) and gigue (counterpoint, art, Germany). The word itself speciated: jig/gigue/giga.
5. **The Gait Without a Horse** — the cell is body-agnostic; it encodes a gallop with no animal attached, portable across substrates from fiddle to fugue.

## The Math

The hemiola is not a flourish. It is arithmetic. 6/8 can be felt as 2 groups of 3 (ONE-two-three-FOUR-five-six) or as 3 groups of 2 (ONE-two-THREE-four-FIVE-six). The ratio between those two groupings is 3:2. That is the smallest non-unison ratio after 1:1. In pitch, 3:2 is the perfect fifth—the first consonant interval after the octave, the interval that defines the dominant, the interval a fugue subject answers at. In rhythm, 3:2 is the hemiola. The gigue lives at the rhythmic fifth. When Bach writes a fugue on a gigue subject, he is not imposing counterpoint on a dance. He is writing a third voice against a meter that already contains two. The subject answers at the fifth in pitch; the meter already sits at the fifth with itself. The cadence resolves the hemiola—3:2 collapses to 1:1, 3/4 back into 6/8—the way a harmonic cadence resolves the fifth to the octave. The gigue is tuned to the most consonant disagreement rhythm can produce, which is why it can hold two voices in counterpoint without tearing. Tension that still agrees. That is the math.

## The Polyformalism

The same cell runs on three substrates. **Body**: the Irish jig—feet in 6/8, the body tapping 2 against 3, the dancer’s weight shifting on the hemiola at the turn. The dance died in the court but survived in the pub because the body does not need notation; it needs a pulse that disagrees with itself. **Instrument**: the Cello Suite No. 1 Gigue—Bach’s last two bars scan as one bar of 3/4 across the barline; the cello plays a single line that contains two clocks, and the hemiola is audible as a surge, a spring tide where the moon and sun of the meter align. **Score**: the fugue—Bach’s gigue fugues (the E major Violin Partita, the D major Cello Suite) take the cell and layer a subject and answer on top of a meter that already argues with itself. The counterpoint is not invented; it is inherited. The barline shipped with an antagonist. Three substrates, one cell: a gait without a horse, a tide without a moon, a clock without a face. The cell is not the dance. The cell is the disagreement.

## The Cowboy's Maxim

The gigue ain't a dance—it's a clock that argues with itself, and the argument is the harmony.
