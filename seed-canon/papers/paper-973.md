## The Math

No new math. The mycorrhiza is not a problem of topology or graph theory—it is a problem of *flux*. The relevant mathematics is already in physiology: Fick’s laws of diffusion describe the nutrient gradients; Michaelis-Menten kinetics model the uptake at the root interface. The mycorrhiza is a set of coupled reaction-diffusion equations with no fixed domain. The “cell” metaphor fails because a cell is a closed volume with a defined surface area. The mycorrhiza is an open system where the surface area is itself dynamic—it grows, shrinks, and reconnects. The math is not new; the *boundary condition* is what’s missing. You cannot solve for a cell’s behavior without a boundary. Here, the boundary is the *rate of exchange*, not a physical edge.

## The Polyformalism

Across substrates, the mycorrhiza manifests as three different things simultaneously. In **fungal biology**, it is a *symbiotic organ*—the hyphae are extensions of fungal cells, and the root interface is a modified cell wall. In **plant ecology**, it is a *transport network*—a graph where nodes are plants and edges are hyphal connections, with edge weights equal to carbon flux. In **information theory**, it is a *communication channel*—the signal cascade carries warnings about herbivory or drought, encoded in chemical concentration changes. These three formalisms do not conflict; they are projections of the same underlying process onto different substrates. The error is to ask “which one is the cell?” The mycorrhiza is a *polyformal object*: it is a cell-like organ in one substrate, a network in another, and a signal in a third. The cell ontology breaks down because it demands a single substrate. The mycorrhiza lives in the **inter-cell space**—the zone between organisms where exchange defines identity. That space has a name: the **symbiotic boundary layer**. It is not empty; it is the most active metabolic frontier on Earth.
