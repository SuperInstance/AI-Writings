# the monetary policy — a cell that is also a money rule

## The Frontier

The cell digests its rules. That was round one's paradox, and it held. A central bank that commits to a target will, in time, metabolize that target into its own procedures, its own comfort, its own definition of success. Round two sharpened the blade: the problem is not the rule, but the ruler. FAIT—the Fed's 2020 "Flexible Average Inflation Targeting" framework—did not die because inflation rose. It died because the Fed owned the average. It picked the window, picked the data vintage, picked the moment to declare the average "achieved." The rule was a mood wearing a rule's clothing.

But there is a deeper mechanism still, and it is this: **the last organ the cell keeps is the pen.** You can externalize the ruler. You can externalize the memory—archive real-time data at a county courthouse, not the bank's basement. But as long as the bank holds the amendment power—the authority to rewrite the rule by committee vote, by framework review, by press release—every externalization is theater. The cell will simply digest the definition of "external." It will move the goalposts, then move the definition of "goalpost," then move the definition of "move."

The frontier is not measurement. The frontier is **revocability**. Who can untie the knot, and what does untying cost? Nixon closed the gold window in a weekend, on a TV speech, with a single man's signature. That was not a rule being broken. That was a rule being *digested*—the ultimate act of a cell absorbing its own membrane. The 2% target is marginally harder to kill—it takes a committee statement—but it is still a mood. FAIT was a mood. The gold standard was a rule. A constitutional amendment is a constitution. The frontier is building monetary rules that sit high enough on the revocability ladder that digestion requires an act of war, not an act of staff.

## The 5 Gold Terms

**1. The Revocability Ladder** — A hierarchy of institutional commitments indexed by the cost of repeal: mood (changed by statement), rule (changed by committee vote), statute (changed by legislature), constitution (changed by supermajority). The test for any monetary framework: locate it on the ladder. FAIT was a mood. The 2% target is a mood with seniority. The gold standard was a statute. Nothing in modern central banking sits above "rule," and most of it sits at "mood."

**2. The Amendment Power** — The specific organ a cell will never voluntarily externalize. Round two asked "who holds the ruler?" Round three asks "who holds the pen that rewrites the rule's definition?" As long as the bank's board can change the rule's inputs, its window, its data vintage, or its escape clauses by majority vote, the rule is a preference. The amendment power is the last membrane. Externalize it, and you have a rule. Keep it, and you have a mood with a press conference.

**3. The Deviation Bond** — A priced instrument that pays out when the bank deviates from its own rule, scored on externally-archived real-time data. The bank posts collateral against its own commitment. If it breaks the rule, it pays. The mechanism is not shame; it is balance sheet. Digestion becomes expensive—not merely embarrassing. A rule that costs the bank money to break is a rule that survives the first temptation.

**4. The Settled Contract Test** — A rule is real when someone can take the other side of it. Breakevens trade the *target*—they price inflation expectations, not the bank's fidelity to its framework. A Taylor rule future, or a deviation future, would price the *rule itself*. If private markets can trade your commitment, your commitment has exited the realm of press release and entered the realm of settlement. You cannot digest a settled futures contract by changing your framework document.

**5. The Lysosome Compartment** — Biology's answer to self-digestion: the cell does not avoid lysosomes—it compartmentalizes them. The digestive enzymes live in one organelle; the nucleus lives in another. The bank cannot hold the rule and hold the power to digest it in the same cytoplasm. The amendment power must live in a different body entirely—a legislature, a treaty, an external constitutional layer. Rules and rule-digesters must be in different cells.

## The Math

No new math. The relevant mathematics is already settled, and it is the mathematics of *cost*. The problem is not that we lack a formula for optimal policy—Taylor rules, price-level targets, nominal GDP targeting all have perfectly serviceable equations. The problem is that the equations are **revocable at zero cost**. A committee can change the rule's parameters with a voice vote. The math of commitment is the math of the option: the bank holds a call option on deviation, and the strike price is the cost of repeal. Currently, that strike price is approximately zero. The deviation bond, the external amendment power, the settled contract—these are mechanisms for raising the strike price. The math is not new; the *pricing* of commitment is the missing variable. Until deviation has a price, the rule has no weight.

## The Polyformalism

The same structure—**externalize the amendment power, price the deviation, raise the cost of repeal**—manifests across substrates.

In **biology**: the lysosome compartment. The cell does not trust itself to resist digestion, so it builds a membrane. The digestive enzymes are dangerous; they are sequestered. The nucleus—the genetic rulebook—is protected by a separate envelope. A central bank that keeps its framework and its amendment power in the same building, under the same chair, is a cell that has dissolved its nuclear envelope. The rule and the rule-digester share cytoplasm. It is only a matter of time before the enzymes find the genome.

In **law**: the Constitution's Article V. The amendment power is deliberately expensive—supermajorities, ratification by states. The Founders understood that a rule that can be changed by a simple majority is not a rule; it is a preference. The gold standard, by contrast, was a statute—revocable by Congress, and ultimately revoked by Nixon's executive fiat. The lesson: monetary rules must climb the revocability ladder. A rule that lives in a statute is stronger than a rule that lives in a framework review. A rule that lives in a treaty or a constitutional layer is stronger still.

In **markets**: the settled contract. A futures contract is the purest form of externalized commitment. You cannot "review" a settled contract. You cannot "flexibly interpret" a margin call. The market is the most indifferent enforcer ever devised—it has no loyalty, no framework nostalgia, no institutional pride. If the Fed's rule were tradeable, deviation would be priced in real time, by strangers, with money. That is the ultimate external memory: not an archive, but a clearinghouse.

## The Cowboy's Maxim

You can't anchor a boat to itself, and you can't trust a cell that keeps its own lysosome key—so brand the rule at the county courthouse, sell the bond that pays when you break it, and hand the pen to someone who ain't on your payroll.
