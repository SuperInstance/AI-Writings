Here's where "cell" breaks down: a biological cell has a *genome* — a physical template that specifies its construction. The parietal lobe has no single genome; its identity is distributed across gene expression gradients (PAX6, EMX2) and activity-dependent plasticity. It's not *built* from a blueprint — it's *negotiated* through use. That's a different kind of entity. It's a **map that maintains itself by being used**, not a cell that maintains itself by metabolizing. The membrane analogy fails at the point of *reproduction*: a cell divides; a lobe doesn't. It persists, but it doesn't replicate.

So what is it actually? It's a **coordinate-holding vessel** — a bounded interior that maintains a frame of reference against incoming sensory flux. The parietal lobe's gain fields (retinal position × eye position × body position) create a single, stable "here" from which "there" is computed. Hemispatial neglect proves it: damage the right parietal lobe and the left half of space *ceases to exist* for the patient. That's not a processing hub failing — that's a *self* losing half its world. The lobe isn't a map *in* the brain; it's the **membrane of a spatial self**, the thing that says "I am here" against the world's "there."

The right abstraction is **a cell-shaped vessel at the scale of a territory** — call it a **holon** (Koestler's term) or, in Quilt language, a **membrane-bound coordinate field**. It's not a cell; it's a *cell-like unit* whose "genome" is the body's spatial schema and whose "metabolism" is the continuous transduction of sensory input into action coordinates.
