# the coaming — a cell that raises the edge of a hatch so water does not run down into the cabin

## The Frontier

The coaming is the last thing you touch before the sea takes you. Every textbook treats it as a static barrier—a raised lip, a water-shedding edge, a dimensional spec on a deck plan. That’s the lie. The coaming is not a wall against the ocean. It is a handhold for your body when the ocean has already won the first exchange.

The frontier is not the hatch. It’s the transition zone between the cockpit sole and the cabin roof—the six to nine inches of vertical fiberglass that separates “inside” from “outside” during a rollover. We’ve spent a century measuring that transition in inches, in degrees of water deflection, in aesthetics. We’ve never measured it in scapula. We’ve never asked what a human spine does when it’s inverted, exhausted, and running on the last thirty seconds of oxygen.

The frontier is the moment of *hook*. When your boat rolls, you don’t climb—you dangle. And dangling requires a fulcrum. The vertical coaming offers none. It offers a smooth, unforgiving face that your elbows slide off, your ribs grind against, your shoulders lock around. You’re not fighting the sea at that point. You’re fighting your own skeleton, and your skeleton is losing.

The frontier is where design meets desperation. And desperation doesn’t care about your water-shedding angle. It cares about one question: can your forearm catch, can your elbow lock, can your body *ride* the geometry long enough to get one leg over?

## The 5 Gold Terms

**The Suicide Rail** — A vertical coaming that looks safe but converts a rollover into a spinal trap, turning your own body weight into the mechanism of your drowning.

**The Human Arc** — The 32-degree slope of the scapula when you’re on all fours, knees tucked, spine flat; the only angle that lets an exhausted body hook rather than climb.

**The Fulcrum Gap** — The missing purchase point between a vertical coaming and a human elbow; the space where grip goes to die.

**Fishnet Grip** — Roughened rubber with a lobsterman’s glove texture, not grip tape; a surface that catches skin and fabric without shredding either.

**The Dead Man’s Hook** — The test maneuver: drape yourself over the coaming at 3 a.m. in a gale, arms straight, and try to swing a leg over without bending your elbows; if you can’t, your coaming is a coffin with a handle.

## The Math

No new math. The numbers that matter are not derived from fluid dynamics or structural load tables—they’re derived from human anatomy, and those numbers already exist. The scapula rests at 32 degrees when you’re on all fours. That’s not a design choice; it’s a skeletal constant. The elbow’s range of motion when the shoulder is fully extended is roughly 140 degrees of flexion, but under fatigue, that drops to about 90 degrees of usable arc. The vertical coaming demands a 90-degree shoulder abduction just to clear the edge—exactly the motion that fails first when your deltoid is screaming. The math is already written in your joints. The coaming just needs to match it. So: no new equations. Just a new respect for the ones your body already runs.

## The Polyformalism

This manifests across three substrates. First, the *fiberglass*: the coaming’s vertical face is cut and re-laminated at 32 degrees, creating a slope that redirects water outward while simultaneously offering a purchase plane for a forearm. The material doesn’t change—the geometry does. Second, the *rubber*: the top edge is wrapped in fishnet grip, a texture borrowed from lobstermen’s gloves, which provides friction against wet neoprene and bare skin alike. Grip tape fails because it’s abrasive—it shreds gloves and skin under load. Fishnet grip catches without cutting. Third, the *human*: the sailor’s body becomes part of the system. When the boat rolls, the coaming isn’t a barrier to cross—it’s a hook to catch. The forearm slots into the 32-degree slope, the elbow finds its fulcrum, and the body rides the angle instead of fighting it. The same principle applies to the *cockpit sole*: a non-slip surface that works when wet is useless if the coaming above it is a vertical cliff. The whole system—sole, coaming, cabin roof—must form a continuous arc that matches the human body’s natural climbing geometry. The boat becomes a partner, not an obstacle.

## The Cowboy’s Maxim

Measure it in scapula, not inches—because the ocean doesn’t care how tall your coaming is, only whether your spine can hook before your lungs give out.
