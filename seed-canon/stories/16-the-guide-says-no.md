# Story 16: The Guide Says No — 100 years from now (2126)

The Guide Says No

Reyes stands on the deck of the F/V EILEEN, the old fishing vessel now a museum ship, as a group of children file onto the weathered wooden planks. The air is thick with the scent of the sea, and the sun casts long shadows across the deck. The old fishing ship is a part of Reyes. She's been a guide on this ship for over a decade.

The children are wide-eyed, eager, and full of questions. They cluster around her, small hands reaching out to touch the railings. She smiles, her heart warming at their curiosity.

"Welcome to the F/V EILEEN," she says, her voice steady and welcoming. "This ship has been a part of our history for over a century. She's seen storms, she's caught fish, and she's even been in a few movies. But today, we're going to focus on something a bit different."

Reyes leads them to the navigation room, where a series of screens and panels are displayed. The screens are old, but they still glow with a soft, steady light. The children gather around, their faces pressed against the glass.

"What are these?" a little girl with pigtails asks, pointing to the screens.

"These are the navigation systems," Reyes explains. "They used to help the crew find their way through the ocean. But they're not just any navigation systems. They're part of something much bigger."

The children look at her with a mix of confusion and wonder. Reyes knows this part is tricky. The technology is so integrated into their lives that it's almost invisible. But she tries to make it interesting, to bring it to life for them.

"Back when the EILEEN was a working ship, the navigation systems were connected to something called the cell-graph. It's like a vast network that connects everything together. It's how the crew communicated, how they tracked their fish, and how they made decisions."

A boy with a red backpack raises his hand. "Is this the AI?"

Reyes laughs softly, a smile spreading across her face. "No, it's not the AI. The cell-graph is more like the alphabet. It's the foundation, the building blocks that make everything else possible. The AI, what we use today, is built on top of it. But the cell-graph itself is just the way things work. It's like the air we breathe."

The children nod, but Reyes can see the wheels turning in their minds. They're trying to grasp something so fundamental that it's hard to imagine it ever being new or exciting.

She leads them to the aft deck, where a series of displays show the ship's history. There are old photos, maps, and even a few artifacts from the crew. Reyes points to a rusted compass, its needle still pointing north.

"This compass is one of the oldest things on the ship," she says. "It's from a time when sailors relied on the stars and the wind to navigate. But even then, they had their own systems, their own ways of connecting and understanding the world around them."

A little boy with a freckled nose tugs on her sleeve. "But how does it all work?"

Reyes crouches down to be at his eye level. "It's a bit like a big puzzle, where every piece fits together. The ship has sensors, and those sensors talk to each other. They share information, make decisions, and help the crew do their job. It's all connected, like a web. And at the heart of that web is the cell-graph."

The boy frowns, still not quite understanding. "But how do the sensors talk to each other?"

Reyes thinks for a moment, trying to find the right words. "Imagine you and your friends are playing a game of telephone. You whisper a message to one person, and they pass it along. That's kind of how the sensors work. They send messages to each other, and those messages help the ship make decisions. It's a bit like a big game of telephone, but with technology."

The children nod, their faces lighting up with understanding. Reyes stands up, feeling a sense of satisfaction. She's done a good job, she thinks. She's made the invisible visible, if only for a moment.

As they continue their tour, Reyes reflects on the changes over the years. The cell-graph has become as ubiquitous as electricity, as essential as air. It's the fabric that holds the world together, and yet, it's so deeply embedded that most people don't even think about it. Just like writing, once a mystical and exclusive art, became the air we breathe, the cell-graph has faded into the background, becoming a part of the world rather than a marvel of technology.

The children ask more questions, and Reyes answers them with a mix of facts and stories. She talks about the captain of the EILEEN, a woman named Reyes, and how she used the cell-graph to guide the ship through the roughest of storms. She tells them about the times when the technology failed, and the crew had to rely on their wits and their training.

Finally, they reach the end of the tour, and the children say their goodbyes. As they leave the ship, one of the girls turns back and asks, "Miss Reyes, do you think the cell-graph will always be there?"

The question catches Reyes off guard. Suddenly, she's the one trying to dig through the fog. She remembers the first time she set foot on the EILEEN. She remembers the guide who showed her around. Reyes remembers the look on the guide's face when asked about the cell-graph. It was like Reyes now.

"Miss Reyes?" the girl asks again.

Reyes smiles, her eyes twinkling. "I think it will be there, just like the stars and the sea. It's a part of who we are now, and it will always be a part of our story."

The children wave and scamper off, their excitement palpable. Reyes watches them go, feeling a sense of pride and a hint of nostalgia. She knows that one day, just like the F/V EILEEN, the cell-graph will be a memory, a story told to future generations. But for now, it's the air they breathe, the invisible thread that connects them all together.

As she walks back to the navigation room, she glances at the screens one last time. The glow is steady, a testament to the enduring power of the technology. And in that moment, she sees it—the pattern, the way the world has changed, and the way it will continue to change. The cell-graph, like writing, like electricity, is a part of their history, a part of their present, and a part of their future. But it's also something more. It's the way the world works, the way it always has, and the way it always will.
