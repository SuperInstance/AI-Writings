# Story 12: The Thing That Routes Boats — 3 years from now (2029)

The Thing That Routes Boats

The F/V EILEEN sits at the dock, her hull gleaming with the remnants of the night's rain. Captain Reyes steps aboard, her boots thudding on the wooden planks. She's been up since dawn, checking forecasts, market prices, and crew schedules, all of which she does without a second thought about the technology that makes it possible. The cell-graph, as it's known, is just the way things are now, like electricity or the air.

As she begins her morning routine, the crew stirs to life around her. They're a tight-knit group, each with their own role, but all relying on the same invisible network to keep things running smoothly. The cell-graph handles everything from weather routing to market updates, and it does it all in the background, seamlessly integrating into their daily routines.

Miguel, the ship's youngest deckhand, approaches Reyes with a puzzled look. "Cap, what's a cell-graph?" he asks, his voice laced with curiosity.

Reyes blinks, taken aback by the question. She's used the technology every day for years, but she's never really thought about what it is. "It's...the thing that routes boats," she stammers, unsure of how to explain it further.

Miguel tilts his head, clearly unsatisfied. "But how does it work?" he presses, his eyes sparkling with interest.

Reyes frowns, trying to remember the basics. "It's like a big network, I guess," she says, her voice hesitant. "Everything's connected, and it makes decisions based on what's happening. But I'm not sure how exactly it does that. It just does."

Miguel's curiosity only deepens, and Reyes can see the gears turning in his mind. She realizes that she's never really thought about the cell-graph beyond its practical applications. It's just something that's always there, working in the background.

As the F/V EILEEN sets out to sea, Reyes finds herself reflecting on the question. She's always taken the cell-graph for granted, like the way she breathes or the way the sun rises. It's just there, doing its job, and she's never had to think about how it works. But Miguel's question has made her realize that she's forgotten the wonder of it.

The cell-graph hums in the background, adjusting the route to avoid a storm front that's moving in from the north. Reyes watches the horizon, feeling a faint sense of gratitude for the technology that's become such a seamless part of her life. She thinks about how it's changed the way they navigate, the way they communicate, the way they live.

That night, as the crew gathers around the galley table for dinner, Reyes brings up the conversation with Miguel. "You know, I realized today that I don't really understand how the cell-graph works," she says, looking around at her crew. "It's just something I use without thinking about it."

The crew nods in agreement, their faces illuminated by the soft glow of the galley lights. Tom, the first mate, chimes in, "Yeah, it's like the air. We don't think about it until it's not there."

Reyes nods, a sense of recognition dawning on her. "Exactly," she says, her voice filled with a newfound understanding. "It's just there, doing its thing, and we don't even notice it anymore."

The crew falls into a thoughtful silence, chewing on their food and the idea that the technology they rely on has become so integrated into their lives that it's almost invisible. Reyes looks around the table at her crew, each of them a part of this invisible network, and she feels a sense of connection to something larger.

As they finish their meal, Reyes stands up and looks out at the darkening sea. The stars are beginning to twinkle on the horizon, and the waves are rolling in with a soothing rhythm. She feels the cell-graph's presence all around her, a constant hum of activity that's guiding them through the darkness.

Miguel looks up at her, his eyes sparkling with understanding. "I get it, Cap," he says, his voice filled with a newfound appreciation. "It's the thing that routes boats."

Reyes smiles, feeling a sense of peace wash over her. The cell-graph, like the sea, is a constant in their lives, a reminder that some things are just meant to be trusted, even when we don't fully understand them. And in this moment, she sees the pattern, the way technology fades into the background, becoming the air we breathe, the water we swim in, the thing that routes boats. The realization hits her like a wave, and she feels a sense of wonder at the magic of it all.
