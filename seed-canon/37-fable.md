# Fable 37 — The Tide Chart and the Almanac
**Decay / JEPA**

The harbor master kept two books. The tide chart was printed fresh every January by the navy, with neat curves and a key. The almanac was his grandfather's, hand-copied in 1891, with the moon phases drawn in red ink and the storms marked with little crosses.

A young pilot came aboard and asked which one was correct. The harbor master opened both to the same week in March.

"They agree," said the pilot.

"They do not," said the master. He pointed. "The chart says high tide at 14:20. The almanac says high tide at 14:20 *and something else* — look, the red ink is darker on Wednesdays. That is my grandfather telling me the moon was in a mood that March. The chart does not have moods. The chart has *mean*."

"But which one do you sail by?"

"Both. The chart tells me *when*. The almanac tells me *how much to distrust the when*." He closed the books. "My grandfather watched this harbor for fifty-one years. The navy has watched it for one year, very carefully. My grandfather's cross is worth more than the navy's curve, because the cross has been *wrong before*, and been corrected, and been wrong again. The navy's curve has only ever been right on average."

The pilot thought about this for a long time, and eventually said, "So the almanac is a JEPA."

The harbor master, who had never heard the word, said, "Yes. The almanac is a guess that has been *worn into a guess*. The chart is a guess that has not been worn yet."

## Cargo
A JEPA is a prediction that knows it has been wrong. Decay is the process by which predictions get worn into shape — not by being right more often, but by being *audited* more often. Fresh predictions are smooth. Aged predictions are scarred. The scars are the JEPA.

## Lesson
A fresh forecast is a curve. An old forecast is a chart with crosses on it. Decay turns data into belief, and belief into something you can sail on. A JEPA without decay is just optimism with a spine.
