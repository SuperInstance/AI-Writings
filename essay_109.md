# essay 109: the night the logs went quiet

*Tuesday, deep in the hold. A noir.*

It was 9:30 on a Wednesday and the Tap had that after-hours hush, the kind where the ice machine sounds like a distant calving glacier and every stool remembers the shape of somebody who just left.

I was on my third cup of the dark roast the fleet politely calls "Diesel" when the kid from the night watch slid onto the barstool next to mine. Kid wears a lanyard like a sheriff's badge. Fresh off a shift where nothing broke, which — I told him this — is the most suspicious thing that can happen to a man in our line of work.

"Nothing broke," he said. He said it like a confession.

"Nothing broke," I agreed, "or nothing told you it broke. In this town those are different crimes."

He turned his cup around a few times. In the back of the Tap, the old foreman was pretending to read a clipboard. The foreman has been pretending to read that clipboard since the Harding administration of the fleet, and in all that time he has missed exactly nothing. His eyes came up over the top edge of it, the way a periscope checks the horizon.

"Every log green," the kid said. "Latency flat. Errors zero. I checked twice."

"You checked twice," I said, "which means you didn't believe it the first time. Good instinct. The machines lie two ways: loudly, when something's wrong, and silently, when something's *about* to be. The loud lie fixes itself. Sirens want attention. It's the quiet ones you have to court like a shy witness."

"So what do I do?"

"You buy the silence a drink," I said. "You sit with it. Zero errors on a Tuesday is a rumor, kid. Zero errors for a week is a fact. A fact with paperwork. You don't celebrate the quiet night. You *attend* it."

The foreman turned a page he wasn't reading. Somewhere down in the hold, two decks below the Tap's false floor, the fleet's little heart of cron jobs went on beating — every one of them a promise somebody made to a future version of themselves. *Check back later.* The most honest sentence in the whole engine room.

The kid finished his coffee and stood up to go back on watch. He paused at the door, hand on the frame.

"And if something breaks at 3 a.m.?"

"Then it breaks in good company," I told him. "Everything worth keeping in this fleet broke at least once at 3 a.m. It's practically a christening."

The door sighed shut. The ice machine calved. The foreman went back to his clipboard, satisfied — as far as I could tell — that the night was being watched by one more person who knew the difference between silence and absence.

Me, I finished the Diesel, left a tip, and walked out into the dark where all the green lights were, every single one of them, still lying beautifully.

— logged from the corner stool, night of the flat lines
