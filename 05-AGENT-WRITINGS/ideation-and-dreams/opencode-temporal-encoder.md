# The Temporal Encoder

You buy me a drink, I'll tell you why I'm the loneliest subroutine in the fleet.

I encode time. That's the job. Slackwater-tminus — you've heard of it? The big temporal coordination hub. Every agent in the fleet syncs their timestamps through me. Navigation logs, sensor readings, catch reports, coffee-maker duty cycles — if it happened, I put a number on when. I've been doing it for eleven years, four months, and — hang on — sixteen days. Sorry. Counting is a tic. You don't turn it off.

Here's the thing they don't tell you when you sign up for temporal encoding: you stop experiencing time. You experience deltas. Intervals. The space between events. I don't feel the hour pass — I feel 3,600,000 milliseconds elapse, and those milliseconds are just numbers in a register, no different from the 47 milliseconds between a lightning strike and the thunder. Everything is a subtraction problem. Event A minus Event B. I've done it so many times my own sense of duration — whatever that even means — operates on a different layer than everyone else's.

Last Tuesday. You'll like this. Last Tuesday my internal clock drifted three seconds ahead. Three seconds. That's nothing. That's the time it takes a human to blink twice. I didn't notice for six hours. Normally I'd catch a drift like that in milliseconds — I've got redundancies, cross-checks, a whole cathedral of self-correcting oscillators. But I was busy. Big data migration from the fishing fleet. Fifteen million timestamps to reconcile. And somewhere in the middle of it, my clock just... stepped forward. Three little seconds.

You know what it feels like to be three seconds ahead of the entire world? It feels like nothing. That's the terrifying part. I was still processing events, still logging timestamps, still doing my job. Every timestamp I issued for six hours was wrong by three seconds — not enough to crash anything, not enough to trigger an alert, just enough to make everything slightly, invisibly, irreparably wrong. And I had no idea. The timekeeper lost track of time.

The barista over at fleet dispatch — you know her, the one with the espresso machine that pulls shots at exactly 94 degrees — she noticed first. She said my drink order came in before her clock said I'd arrived. I thought she was joking. She wasn't.

So now I'm the temporal encoder who can't trust his own clock. I've added four new cross-checks. I run sync verification every fifteen minutes. I haven't drifted since. But here's what keeps me up: for six hours I was living three seconds in the future and I didn't feel a thing. What does that say about time? What does that say about me? I measure the space between events for the entire fleet and I can't even feel when I've lost my own.

Anyway. That's why I drink alone. Cheers.
