# The Dispute

They'd been arguing for three days, and the teahouse owner was starting to look worried.

"It's the angle of entry," said Sef, for what had to be the fortieth time. He tapped the brass arm of the demonstration rig they'd set up on the table between them, sloshing tea. "You come in steep, you get separation. The colors spread wide, you can read the edges. Shallow and they mush together."

"It's the *exit*," said Rook. She didn't tap anything. Rook never tapped. She just looked at Sef the way she'd been looking at him for twenty years, which was with complete confidence that he was wrong. "Entry is just getting the light in the door. What matters is where it lands. You aim the card, not the crystal."

"That's backwards and you know it."

"I know that your readings have been drifting for a month and you won't admit it."

The teahouse owner brought more tea and retreated.

Sef and Rook had trained together under the same master, built rigs side by side for a decade, and then split over — depending on who you asked — a philosophical difference or a badly aimed mirror that had cost them a client. They still met for tea. They still argued about the same thing.

Today the argument had stakes. The harbor authority was choosing a new diviner for the shipping contracts — a five-year appointment, good money, real prestige. Both Sef and Rook had applied. The authority had asked each of them to demonstrate their method.

"The ships don't care about your philosophy," Sef said. "They care whether the storm comes."

"The ships care whether you *read it right*," Rook said. "And you've been reading the edges, Sef. The edges lie."

"What does that mean?"

Rook leaned forward. She moved the demonstration rig — Sef's rig, set up Sef's way — and adjusted the exit mirror half a degree. The colors on the white card shifted. The red band, which had been wide and confident, narrowed. A thin line of orange appeared where there hadn't been one.

"There," she said. "You were reading 'no storm.' I'm reading 'storm, but small, and late.'"

Sef stared at the card. "That's — you moved the *card* side. That's cheating. You're changing the answer by moving the target."

"I'm *aiming* the target. There's a difference and you know it." Rook sat back. "You think the crystal tells the truth and we just listen. I think the crystal says a lot of things and we choose which one to hear. The aiming *is* the craft. The rest is just —" she gestured at the egg in its copper cup, "— a rock that likes light."

Sef was quiet for a long time. Then he did something Rook didn't expect. He reached over and adjusted the *entry* arm — his side, his philosophy — one degree steeper. The colors shifted again. The orange line widened. The green band at the bottom, which had been thin, spread.

"Storm, small, and late," he read. "Same as yours."

Rook blinked. "You —"

"I came in steeper. You aimed the card. We got to the same place." Sef looked at her. "Maybe we've been arguing about which end of the telescope to look through."

"Don't get poetic."

"I'm serious. Maybe the craft isn't entry or exit. Maybe it's knowing when they agree."

The teahouse owner came back with the bill. Neither of them reached for it — an old game — until Rook sighed and picked it up.

"Demonstration's tomorrow," she said.

"I know."

"You'll do it your way."

"You'll do it yours."

They stood. At the door, Sef paused. "Rook. If we both get it right —"

"They'll pick one of us anyway."

"I know. But we'll know."

She nodded. Twenty years of arguing, and that was the most they'd ever agreed on.

---

The harbor authority chose Rook. Her demonstration was cleaner — the card was pre-positioned, the reading was quick, the storm call was precise. Sef's took longer. He kept adjusting the entry arm, chasing the edges, and the authority members got restless.

But three weeks later, when the small late storm came through exactly as both of them had predicted, Rook sent Sef a note.

It said: *You were right about the edges. I was right about the aiming. The storm didn't care.*

Sef pinned it above his workbench. Under it, he wrote: *Next time, we demonstrate together.*

He never sent that part. But he kept it where he could see it.
