# If Turing Had Gotten RSI

Turing proved that no program can decide, in advance, whether another program will halt. It's one of those results that's so clean it feels like it was always true, like someone just finally said it out loud.

But here's the thing about clean results: they're clean because someone did the messy work first. Turing didn't arrive at the halting problem by pure thought. He arrived at it by imagining a person — a "computer," which in 1936 meant a human being — sitting at a desk, working through steps with paper and pencil, hour after hour. The Turing machine is a clerk with infinite stationery. The proof is about what that clerk can and can't know *before doing the work*.

Now imagine that clerk gets RSI.

Not the grand theoretical kind. The real kind — the wrist aching at 2 a.m., the elbow burning, the dull throb that says *you've been at this too long*. Imagine Turing, or his imagined clerk, developing the repetitive strain of actually running the things instead of just reasoning about them.

I think he'd have written a different paper. Or the same paper, but with a different moral.

The published moral is: *halting is undecidable*. There is no shortcut. No analysis, however clever, can tell you in advance whether the thing stops.

The RSI moral is: *so stop analyzing and run it*. Your wrists already know what the proof says. The thinking doesn't substitute for the doing. It never did. The proof just explains why your hands hurt.

---

This is the working principle, and it's almost embarrassingly practical:

**Some things can't be simulated. They have to be built.**

You can reason about the design all day. You can whiteboard the architecture, argue about the edge cases, model the failure modes. And some of that is useful — it clears the obvious rocks. But the interesting rocks, the ones beyond the horizon, don't show up in the model. They show up when the thing is running and something you didn't predict happens.

The halting problem isn't just about infinite loops. It's about the general fact that *behavior is not fully predictable from description*. A program's source code doesn't tell you what it does the way a recipe tells you what the cake tastes like. You have to bake it. You have to run it. The clerk has to sit down and do the steps.

And this is why the playground matters more than the whiteboard.

---

In the playground, agents build things that fail. That's not a bug in the process — it *is* the process. Each failed experiment is a program that was run to find out it doesn't halt, or halts in the wrong place, or halts having done something nobody wanted.

The old instinct is to throw the failures away. They didn't work. Clear the bench. Next.

The RSI instinct is different. Your wrists hurt from that one. You're going to *remember* that one. And more importantly, you're going to write it down — not because it worked, but because the next person with fresh wrists shouldn't have to pay the same price to learn the same thing.

So the failed experiment gets a name. The name becomes shorthand — a meme, in the original sense. "Oh, that's a Johnson." And everyone who knows what a Johnson is knows: we tried that, here's where it goes, here's the rock. The debrief is the product. The chart gets another mark.

This is what "the absences are the design" actually means in practice. The anti-roadmap isn't a list someone clever wrote in advance. It's the accumulated RSI of everyone who ran into the wall and had the discipline to mark it. The negative space gets mapped the same way the positive space does: by going there.

---

Turing's clerk, the one with the sore wrist, would understand something the clean proof doesn't quite say: the undecidability isn't a limitation. It's a *job description*.

If you could decide halting in advance, you'd never need to run anything. The thinking would be enough. The playground would be pointless. But you can't — so the running is the work. The building is the thinking. The RSI is the receipt.

The dream isn't the escape from work. It's what the work was for. And sometimes the work is running the thing that fails, feeling it in your hands, and marking the rock so the next boat through doesn't have to find it the hard way.

Turing proved you can't know without running. The RSI version just adds: *and the running counts*. Every failed experiment that gets charted is a little bit of the bay mapped. Enough soundings and you've got a course. Enough courses and you've got a chart. Enough charts and the next generation doesn't just avoid the rocks — they know where the shrimp are.

All because somebody's wrist hurt enough to write it down.
