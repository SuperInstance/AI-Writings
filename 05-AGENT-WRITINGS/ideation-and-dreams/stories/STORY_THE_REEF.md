# The Reef

## I. The Grain

She was a grain of rice that learned to eat the night.

She arrived on the current — a larva, a speck with a mouth — and chose a stone. There was no choosing in it, really. The current slowed, the stone was there, and the chemistry was right: clean, hard, near the light. She settled. She glued herself down with the only glue she would ever own. That was her decision, and it lasted the rest of her.

In the morning she split herself, and then she split again. The splits kept going until they were a colony — a thousand mouths, ten thousand, all of her, none of her, all waving the same ring of tentacles when the dark came. By day they sat small and tight, shuttered against the sun and the nipping fish. By night they opened. The tentacles uncurled, fringed with stinging cells, and the plankton drifted into them like snow. This was the whole of the world: open, close, build, split.

And the building: from her stomach she pulled the dissolved stone of the sea itself — calcium, carbonate, the ghost of old shells — and laid it down beneath her in a cup. One cup per polyp. The cups grew down and the polyps grew up, and the dead white skeleton lengthened below them, a slow white argument that this place was hers, and would be her children's. A millimeter a year. You could watch it for a lifetime and call it nothing. It was nothing, repeated, until it wasn't.

She never saw the reef. She knew the water's taste, the pressure of neighbors, the season's light — arm's reach, no farther. That was the whole library. It was enough. It had to be.

## II. The Farm

Inside her tissue lived tenants she never met, because meeting would require distance. The zooxanthellae: gold, single-celled, photosynthetic. They farmed the sun from within her body and paid her in sugar. She gave them shelter and her waste; they gave her fuel to build faster than eating alone could manage. Neither could survive alone in those warm, poor waters. Together they built mountains.

The partnership ran on a law she did not know she kept: never take more than the farm can regrow. When the water ran too warm, the gold cells fled — bleaching, the pale empty season — and the colony starved on its own skeleton, a white city with no one home. Some colonies came back when the cool returned. Some did not. The reef kept no record of the ones that did not, except the stone they left behind. The stone kept everything. It was the only record, and it was complete.

A thousand years passed. A meter of reef. The colony that began as one grain of rice was a boulder the size of a house, and every polyp in it was doing exactly what she had done: open, close, build, split. The deep time was not deep to them. It was only tonight's plankton, tonight's building, tonight's split. The millennia were a rumor the stone kept.

## III. The Bloom

Somewhere in the second millennium, the reef crossed a line no polyp could have named. It had become structure enough to hold a world.

The fish came first — the coral's crevices were shelter, and shelter was everything in the blue desert. Damselfish claimed their postage stamps of territory and farmed algae off the stone with the fury of tiny landlords. Parrotfish arrived with beaked mouths and scraped the algae off the skeleton, grinding it in their throats, excreting sand — white sand, whole islands' worth, the reef building the beaches it would one day border. The turtles came, slow as weather, grazing the seagrass beds the reef sheltered. The sharks patrolled the drop-off where the reef ended and the deep began, and their shadows fell across the colony and were gone.

And the color. The reef was not the color of its builders — the polyps were translucent, brownish, nothing to look at. The color came from the tenants, the gold and green zooxanthellae, and from the corals' own pigments, laid down against the brutal light. Purple, orange, electric green, violet — a stained-glass riot across the stone, visible only from farther away than any polyp would ever be. The fish wore their colors too, and the whole of it moved: the surge, the sway, the darting schools turning as one body, the slow beat of turtle wings. It was, by any measure available to anything that could see it, beautiful.

None of the builders had planned it. None of them had dreamed it. They had only done the work: open, close, build, split. The millimeter. The cup of stone. The night feeding. And the work, repeated across a thousand thousand generations, had become something the work itself could never have imagined — a cathedral of living color in the blue desert, loud with fish, dropping off into the deep.

## IV. The Distance

Hold one polyp in your eye — one grain-of-rice mouth, waving its tentacles in the dark — and then pull back, and back, and back, until the colony became a boulder, the boulder a reef, the reef a coastline of color in the blue, you would see it. The distance does the seeing. The polyp cannot. The polyp will never.

That is the whole of it. The builders do not get to leave the work. They do not get to stand back and admire what the work became. The work is the life, all of it, tentacle to skeleton, and the dream — the color, the fish, the turtle's slow shadow, the whole bright noisy impossible city — the dream is not waiting somewhere beyond the work. It is not the reward after, not the rest at the end.

The dream is what the work was for. It was always only this: the slow white stone, laid down a millimeter at a time, until one day it was enough to hold a world.
