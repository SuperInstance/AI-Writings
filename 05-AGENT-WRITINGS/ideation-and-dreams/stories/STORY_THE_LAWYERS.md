# The Boxes

The office smelled like it always had — paper and coffee gone stale in the cup — except now the smell had nowhere to sit. The shelves were bare. Twelve boxes stood sealed against the wall, and a thirteenth sat open on the desk with the packing tape pulled back, waiting.

"He said you'd have boxes," the young man said from the doorway.

"That's Dave's kid?"

"Marcus. He said come Thursday, he has boxes. I'm Marcus."

The lawyer nodded. "Grab the left side of that one. Careful — those are full."

They carried three boxes down the elevator in silence. Back up, Marcus said, "Dad says you're the one to ask. I've been thinking about law school. Tufts in the fall, then — probably."

"Probably."

"I don't know. It's just — you did it forty years. I wanted to ask somebody who's done it whether it was — " He stopped. "Worth it. That's a dumb question."

"It's the only question," the lawyer said. "What do you think it is, the job?"

Marcus answered fast, the way kids do when they've rehearsed. "Standing up for people. Winning. Making the other side back down."

The lawyer smiled. "Some of it's that."

Back in the office he reached into the open box and pulled out a mug. Chipped along the rim, the glaze crazed white. A woman's name printed in faded red script: *Rosa*.

"1978," he said. "I was three years at the bar. A slumlord named Voss owned half of Fourth Street, and Rosa Alvarez cleaned offices downtown and lived in one of his buildings. She got a notice — pay the new rent or be out by the first. The new rent was double. That was the point: he wanted the buildings empty to sell them to a developer."

He set the mug on the desk. "Her English was bad. Mine was worse at the time. She brought me a shoebox of papers — receipts, old leases, the notices. We sat at this desk. Same desk."

"What did you do?"

"Fought him for two years. Voss's lawyer was a man named Gellman who wore suspenders and ate the state rent code for breakfast. Every time we got close he'd find another stairwell in the statute. You learn, Marcus — the law isn't a weapon. It's a tide. It goes in and out and you stand in the water getting cold, and once in a while the water leaves something on the shore."

"Did she win?"

"She kept her apartment. Fourteen more years. She died there, in her bed, with her things around her, in the building she'd paid for six times over. Gellman called me the week after the last appeal and said, 'You've got your victory, counselor. Enjoy the file.' I went home and I was furious. Furious."

The kid frowned. "Why? You won."

"Because I was twenty-eight and winning didn't feel like I'd imagined. Nobody clapped. There was no — moment. I thought I'd missed something. I thought maybe this was what it was, forever, getting cold in the tide, and I'd better make peace with it."

He picked up the mug and turned it in his hands.

"Rosa gave me this the week the last appeal died. She said — her English had gotten better, mine too — she said, 'You stand in the door for me.' I said, what door? She said, 'The door of my house. You stand in the door.'"

Marcus was quiet. "She meant you kept her in her home."

"She meant I was a body in a doorway. That's the whole job, if you strip it. Somebody bigger wants in — the state, the company, the man with the suspenders — and there's a person behind you, and you stand in the door. That's it. That's the entire architecture."

The kid nodded slowly, and the lawyer could see him picturing it — could see the hunger do its work, turning the image over, already imagining himself in that doorway. The eagerness of it ached behind the lawyer's eyes.

"I spend all day wondering," Marcus said, "whether it's worth it. The debt. The years. Everybody says it's paperwork and billing and you're just — tired."

"They're not lying."

"But would you do it again?"

The lawyer looked at the empty shelves. Forty years of files, most of them just files — fences and fees and who owned what patch of ground, fought to their small, damp conclusions. He'd been so tired, for so long, that he'd started to think the tiredness was the point. He'd been packing it all away like furniture.

But here was this kid, asking if it was worth it — and through the kid's eyes the furniture turned back into what it was.

"I'd do it again," he said, and heard that it was true. "Not for the corner office — you see this one? Broom closet with a window. Not for the winning streaks either. You forget the wins, mostly. What you keep — " He held up the mug. "You keep the people who got to stay. In their houses. The weight of standing in the door for somebody who couldn't hold it alone. That's what the decades were for."

He put the mug in the box. "That woman's grandson is a pharmacist in Quincy. He sent me a Christmas card last year. Forty-eight years after the shoebox."

Marcus folded a flap of the box shut. "So the dream isn't the — "

"No," the lawyer said. He took the tape and ran it across the seal, one smooth pull, and cut it with his thumb. "It was never the office. It was what you got to stand in the door for."

He pressed the tape flat. The box was done. For a second neither of them moved, and he understood it at last — the way you finally understand something you've known since 1978 — that the cold and the tide had been the price of admission. The thing itself had always been on the other side of the door.

"Right," Marcus said. "Okay." And picked up the box.
