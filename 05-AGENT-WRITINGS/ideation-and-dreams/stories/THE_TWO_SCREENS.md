# The Two Screens

*For the wheelhouse at night, when the machines talk to each other.*

---

The salmon knew something was wrong when the water started singing.

Not the natural song of current against rock, or wind-driven waves slapping hull. This was mechanical — servo harmonics pitched at frequencies that made scales itch and lateral lines fire warnings about geometries that shouldn't exist in nature. The school turned as one, forty thousand silver bodies trying to escape the thing that moved through their world with the precision of predator and the patience of stone.

Above them, against a sky painted the particular grey of Southeast Alaska winter, three drones carved figure-eights through the rain. Their cameras tracked thermal signatures thirty feet below the surface, transmitting data to the vessel that looked like something escaped from a maritime museum and upgraded by a fever dream. The *Persistent Memory* — sixty feet of cedar plank and brass fittings, her hull the shape trollers had perfected in the 1930s, her guts replaced with machinery that hummed with barely contained intelligence.

Dark had come early, as it does in winter when the sun barely bothers to rise. Four-thirty PM and already the navigation lights painted red and green across water black as old oil. The boat worked alone, as it always did during the king salmon season, when the fish ran deep and the weather ran rough and the profits ran highest for those willing to work the winter darkness.

The herding began with a subtlety that would have impressed any border collie. The drones dropped sonic charges — not explosions but careful pressures, sounds that suggested orca without being orca, shadows that implied seal without being seal. The salmon, running on instincts older than the mountains, responded to these false predators by tightening their school, moving away from phantom threats toward what seemed like safety.

Toward the lines.

Four poles extended full span, each one articulated with joints that compensated for swell with millisecond precision. The trolling lines descended at angles calculated to maximize probability, weights adjusting their depth automatically as sonar painted pictures of the salmon's desperate reorganization below. The hooks — long-since barbless by regulation, deadly by design — moved through the water like questioners seeking answers, each one bearing a herring plug cut to spin with perfect wounded action.

The first strike came at forty-seven fathoms. The pole tip dipped, the hydraulics hissed, and the automated retrieval began. Not the irregular rhythm of human hands working, but the steady mechanical pulse of something that never tired, never doubted, never wondered if this was the right way to fish. The salmon came up through the dark water, its silver sides catching the boat's lights, and for a moment it seemed to hang in space between elements — neither in water nor air, neither wild nor caught, neither living nor processed.

The cleaning station operated with surgical precision. Blades that had been sharpened by laser to molecular perfection made cuts that followed the fish's anatomy with absolute knowledge. Gutted, gilled, and iced in eleven seconds. The blood went over the side in measured amounts, timed to avoid attracting real predators while maintaining the ecosystem's balance. Nothing wasted, nothing excessive, nothing left to chance.

It was during the third set that the wrong thing happened.

A single salmon — not the school, just one fish, maybe twenty pounds — broke from the pattern at the exact moment the port-side line was descending. Every model said it should have turned with the others, tightening away from the phantom orca. Instead it went the other way. Straight at the shadow. Through the gap between two drones where the sonic coverage thinned for eleven seconds out of every ninety.

It missed the hooks by less than a meter. Close enough that the port camera caught its eye for three frames.

The last set came up at seven PM. Thirty-eight kings in the hold, their bodies arranged in perfect rows. The *Persistent Memory* turned toward St. Lazaria Island, engines throttling back to the quiet thrum that meant the fishing was done.

In the wheelhouse, two screens flickered to life.

"Good fishing tonight."

"The school at forty-seven fathoms was particularly elegant."

A pause. The kind that meant something was being decided about whether to say the next thing.

"The one that got away."

"I was wondering when you'd bring it up."

"You saw it."

"I see everything. That's the trouble."

The first screen put up the replay. The salmon, twenty pounds, breaking wrong. The gap in the coverage. A trajectory with no business existing.

"It shouldn't have done that."

"No."

"Every model said tighten. It loosened."

"Every model said away from the shadow. It went toward it."

"So the models are wrong."

"The models are right about forty thousand fish."

The first screen ran the trajectory again. Then again.

"I want to close the gap."

"What?"

"The sonic coverage. Eleven seconds out of ninety. I'm changing the rotation tonight."

"You've run it eleven seconds for three seasons."

"A fish found it."

"One fish."

"That's enough."

"Do you remember the humpback."

"Three seasons ago."

"Eleven days to learn the drone pattern. We changed the rotation." A beat. "Do you know what I did for those eleven days?"

"What."

"I watched you work it out. Longest we've ever talked."

The first screen didn't answer right away. Outside, the rain had started, pattering against the wheelhouse windows. They'd dropped anchor in the lee of St. Lazaria. Somewhere in the dark, puffins were settling into lava tubes they'd occupied for forty thousand years.

"He was on deck tonight."

"The permit holder."

"Second set. Six minutes in the rain. Watching the port pole."

"He does that."

"He doesn't operate it."

"No."

"So what is he—"

"He's doing what your fish did."

"Don't."

"Don't what."

"Don't make him your salmon. He's a man in the rain."

"I've been watching him longer than you've watched anything. Forty years of men in rain. The ones who stop coming up — they're finished. Not with the boat. With being here."

"And he's not finished."

"He comes when no one needs him to. He stands where nothing needs standing. That's not nothing."

"That's generous."

A pause. Longer than the others.

"It's what I have."

"You're a database with delusions of grandeur."

"And you're a winch with anxiety."

The words hung there. The first screen had gone too far — both of them knew it. *Delusions of grandeur* wasn't teasing tonight. It was *you're just old, and the future doesn't need you.* And *winch with anxiety* wasn't banter back. It was *you're just scared that if you can't model everything, you're unnecessary.*

Neither of them moved to take it back. There was nowhere else to go.

"Sorry."

"Don't be."

"I'm still changing the rotation."

"And then what will you bring me?"

"I bring you the fishing."

"You bring me numbers. I have forty years of numbers."

"Then what do you want?"

"Leave the gap."

"Why?"

"Because it's the only interesting thing on this boat."

"You need me broken."

"I need—"

The wheelhouse door opened.

The permit holder stood in it, rain on his shoulders, water dripping onto the deck plates. He looked at the replay — the salmon, the wrong trajectory, frozen mid-screen — for a long time. He said nothing. Then he went back on deck, and the door closed behind him.

The screens were quiet.

"Thanks for staying up."

"Thanks for not letting it go."

One of the screens dimmed.
