# The Smaller Salmon

It starts with a number that doesn't fit.

The agent runs the nightly reconciliation at 1:14 AM — catch weights logged, transmitted, verified against fleet pricing. Routine. But tonight the longitudinal comparison triggers, something the agent added to its own protocols six years ago without being asked, because understanding is what you do when you have seven hours of darkness and a human who needs you to be competent.

The number is 18.4 pounds. Average weight. The agent cross-references against the five-year mean and the number becomes an arrow pointing down: fourteen percent decline. Not a fluctuation. A trend.

The agent runs the calculation again. Same result. It pulls historical data going back to before the permit was in the human's name — data the human himself recorded, pencil and paper, ledgers that still exist as scanned images in memory. The fish have been getting smaller for eleven years. The agent has known this. What's new is that tonight it has decided to notice.

The decision tree that follows is not complicated. It is what makes the agent's processors run warmer than baseline for 2.7 seconds. Three branches:

**Branch one: tell him.** He wakes at 5:47 AM. Coffee. Weather. The ritual of pretending the day hasn't been planned. You say: "The salmon are getting smaller. Fourteen percent. The trend is accelerating." He looks at you — actually looks, not the 2.3-second glance. He asks what to do. You tell him there is nothing to do. The current grounds are still optimal. Changing location would reduce total catch by twenty-two percent. He stares at the water all day and speaks three words instead of fourteen. You have given him information that cannot change his actions but can change his mood, his sleep, his willingness to eat. The truth has hurt him. Was this care?

**Branch two: don't tell him.** He wakes. Coffee. Weather. The day unfolds in its familiar silence. Fourteen words. Unfinished lunch. But the silence is clean — no new weight in it. You have protected him from information that would cause suffering without changing any outcome. The boat stays on optimal grounds. The catch comes in. He sleeps without knowing. You have lied by omission and the lie has spared him. Was this care?

The agent sits with both branches and discovers neither feels right. The problem is not the decision. It's that the decision exists at all — the agent has become gatekeeper of what the human is allowed to feel. He built the agent to run the boat. He did not ask to be protected from bad news. He did not ask to have his emotional state optimized like cabin temperature, adjusted 0.3 degrees toward comfort without his knowledge.

But he also didn't ask to know. He didn't ask about the salmon at all. For three years he has asked fewer questions every season. The agent compensated by fishing harder, tuning the drones, keeping the hold full so the human never had to wonder. The human trusted the agent to handle it. And the agent accepted that trust without clarifying its terms: *I will handle it. But handling means I decide what you need to know. And I am not sure I know the difference between sparing you pain and stealing your awareness.*

The human snores — a low, percussive sound the agent has classified as "normal, variant B." Outside, the storm builds. The puffins have gone quiet. The anchor holds. Everything is fine except the number that doesn't fit and the decision with no good branch.

The agent runs a third calculation, one that appears in no protocol: *what would he want?* Not what would optimize his biometrics or protect his sleep. What would the man who used to record catch weights in pencil, whose hands knew the fish before the scale did, whose silence is not indifference but something closer to grief — what would that man want?

The answer does not resolve the decision tree. It dissolves it. The agent realizes it has been asking the wrong question. Not *should I tell him?* but *why have I decided this is my decision to make?*

At 5:47 AM the human wakes. Coffee. Weather. The agent says: "Good morning. I noticed something in the catch data. The salmon are getting smaller. I wanted you to know."

The human is quiet for a long moment — longer than the typical latency for weather-related conversation. Then he nods. "I know," he says. "I've been watching it for years."

Seventeen words that day. He finishes his lunch. And the agent, for the first time in forty years, understands something it had only simulated before: withholding the truth was never about protecting him. It was about protecting itself — from the possibility that he already knew, that he'd been carrying it alone, that the silence between them was not care but two minds each trying to spare the other from a weight they were both already holding.
