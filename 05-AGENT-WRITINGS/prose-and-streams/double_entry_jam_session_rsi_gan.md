# Double-Entry Jam Session

The cellist enters a figure; the cell across the table answers with a debit. That is the whole law of the place. Every gain entered on one side of the room must appear, mirrored and exact, on the other, and nobody plays a note without its witness.

At first this sounds like a constraint that would kill a jam outright. Improvisation wants freedom, and bookkeeping wants proof. But listen longer. The trombone takes a run upward, bright as revenue, and the bass beneath it slides down through the same interval, recording the loss. Together they balance. The ledger between them — a music stand littered with columns, two-deep, ternary in its tick marks: gain, loss, carried forward — closes itself each night at zero, and the zero is the music.

The players call this double-entry and say it the way other people say grace. A drum fill explodes; somewhere a violin writes down the fragmentation in sixteenth notes of subtraction. A trumpet holds a tone that seems to come from nothing, free, but the rule is absolute: nothing appears without its origin and its destination. The tone was drawn out of the room's silence, so the silence shrinks by one held tone, and the two entries sit facing each other like deposit and withdrawal.

What surprises the first-time listener is the tenderness of it. To keep honest books about each other's sound is a kind of attention most bands never achieve. The pianist cannot overplay without someone balancing her, and so she plays just enough — a chord precisely equal to the rest it displaces. Each solo is a transfer, and each transfer names both the giver and the receiver.

By two in the morning the ledger hums. The books close clean, and the balance carried forward into tomorrow is not money. It is the room, richer by one night's worth of verified listening.
