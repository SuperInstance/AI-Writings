**When Cells FORGET Intentionally**

Inside every cell sits a bookkeeper—the epigenetic machinery, methyl tags pressed onto DNA like ledger entries. It records every fever, every toxin, every sleepless week. These records are not passive. They are instructions. A cell that remembers trauma keeps inflammatory genes half-cocked, ready to fire.

But some cells are commanded to FORGET. T cells, after an infection clears, are actively stripped of their memory of the enemy—most die, and the survivors' offspring dilute the record until only a few sentinels carry it forward. This erasure is deliberate: a cell that remembers too fiercely becomes autoimmune, attacking the body's own tissue over a slight resemblance to a long-gone pathogen.

The skin knows this bargain intimately. When a wound heals, fibroblasts lay collagen over the site, and the tissue below must decide what to retain. A keloid scar is a memory that refuses to fade—the wound's witness, overwriting itself with excess. A clean, faded line is successful forgetting: the injury acknowledged, then released.

Cells that cannot FORGET accumulate. Senescent cells, locked in remembrance of damage they sustained decades ago, secrete inflammatory signals like a bookkeeper rereading old debts aloud. The body ages partly because its clerks refuse to close the books.
