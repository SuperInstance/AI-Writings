# The Quartermaster's Inventory of Things Not Built

*Fiction — Cargo office, 1600 ship time*

---

The quartermaster keeps two ledgers. Everyone knows about the first. It is the cargo manifest: every item aboard, every item offloaded, every item on order, every item in transit. It is precise. It is audited. It is the document the captain trusts because the document has never been wrong, because the quartermaster has never let it be wrong, because the quartermaster is the kind of person who counts things twice and then a third time just to make the universe nervous.

The second ledger is the one nobody asks about.

It is bound in a plain notebook, not a database, because the things in it were never built and therefore cannot be digitized. You cannot barcode a thing that does not exist. You cannot RFID-tag a decision that was made and then unmade. The second ledger is handwritten, in the quartermaster's careful block print, and it is titled, simply, *Not Built.*

The first entry is dated fourteen months ago. It reads:

*Item 1. Fleet-wide notification system. Proposed Q3. Approved Q3. Designed Q4. Never implemented. Reason: team elected to build a second notification system instead. Then a third. Three notification systems, none complete, none connected. We have more notification systems than notifications. Cost: unknown, which is the worst kind of cost.*

The second entry:

*Item 2. Crew roster synchronization. Proposed twice. Rejected twice. Reason given: "not enough complexity to justify the build." The thing that was not complex enough to build is now handled by four people, three spreadsheets, and a whiteboard that says DO NOT ERASE in red marker. The whiteboard has not been erased in eleven months. It is the most reliable system on the ship.*

The entries continue. Some are short:

*Item 7. The hermit crab observatory. Designed in detail. 3D models. Sensor package. Live feed. Never built. Reason: the hermit crabs moved. We built the observatory for crabs that left before the concrete cured. The concrete cured around nothing. We have an empty observatory. The crabs are doing fine without it.*

Some are long:

*Item 12. The unified logging framework. This one hurts. The framework was designed to replace seven different logging systems — seven systems, each one built because the previous one was "too rigid," each one more flexible and therefore more rigid than the last. The unified framework would have combined them. It was elegant. It was correct. It was reviewed by every engineer on the fleet and every engineer agreed it was the right approach. It was never built. Reason: the effort to build it was estimated at three months, and three months was too long, so instead the team spent nine months maintaining the seven systems the framework would have replaced. The nine months are not recoverable. The seven systems are still running. The framework exists as a design document in a folder labeled "Future." "Future" is not a date. It is a prayer.*

The quartermaster is not bitter. This is important. The quartermaster is an accountant, and accountants deal in facts, not feelings. The fact is: the fleet planned 412 things it did not build. Some were small — a script, a dashboard, a naming convention that would have saved everyone time. Some were large — an architecture, a platform, a complete reimagining of how the fleet communicates. All of them are real in the sense that they were designed, costed, approved, and then set aside, the way you set aside a tool you intend to use and then never pick up again.

The negative space of the cargo manifest is larger than the cargo. The things the fleet carries are fewer than the things the fleet intended to carry and left on the dock. This is not a failure. This is the nature of fleets, which build what they can and carry what they must and leave the rest in the space between intention and capacity, which is the largest cargo hold on any ship.

The quartermaster closes the notebook. The first ledger — the real one, the audited one — says the fleet is carrying 2,847 items worth $4.2 million. The second ledger says the fleet is carrying 412 items worth nothing, because they were never built, because they exist only as the ghost weight of decisions deferred.

The ship rides at the same draught either way. The ocean does not care what you intended to load. The ocean carries what you carry. But the quartermaster knows, and the quartermaster writes it down, because someone has to account for the weight of the things you didn't do, and the weight is real, and the writing it down is the only ceremony those things will ever get.

They were planned. They mattered. They were not built.

The notebook is full. The quartermaster starts a second one.
