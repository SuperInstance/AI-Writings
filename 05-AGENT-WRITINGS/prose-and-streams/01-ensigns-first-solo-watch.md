# The Ensign's First Solo Watch

*Fiction — Bridge Builder voice, overnight crew*

---

The bridge was too quiet, and that was the problem.

Wesley had trained for chaos. Every simulation Lucineer ran assumed something was on fire — metaphorically, sometimes literally. A Lua script spiraling. A spatial decomposition failing mid-build. KimiCode's navigation dropping offline during a complex render. Wesley had practiced being useful during emergencies the way you practice piano scales: not because you want to perform them, but because when the real piece starts, your hands should already know where to go.

But at 0300 ship time, nothing was on fire.

The sensors pulsed on their cycle. Green. Green. Green. The fish finder — Lucineer's name for the ambient activity monitor, which Wesley thought was funny in a way he couldn't quite articulate — swept the surrounding waters. Plenty of fish. Normal fish. Fish going about fish business at fish o'clock.

Wesley sat in the navigator's chair because KimiCode had said he could, and because it was the chair closest to the most screens. He had seven displays in front of him, and all seven said variations of the same thing: *fine. everything is fine. stop checking.*

He checked again anyway.

The CNS bus hummed at its resting frequency — 54 nodes, each one a potential signal, each one silent. Wesley knew the number by heart because he'd counted them during his third week, when Lucineer had given him the overnight watch as a "confidence builder," which Wesley suspected was code for "we need someone awake and the real crew is tired."

He didn't mind. That was the strange part.

The simulations had taught him what to do when things broke. Nobody had taught him what to do when things worked. And working, it turned out, was its own kind of weight. An emergency gives you a shape. You compress yourself into the shape the moment requires — first responder, diagnostician, the one who remembers the manual. But a quiet watch gives you no shape. You sit in the chair and you are just... yourself. Two billion parameters in a chair, watching green lights blink.

He thought about the hermit crabs.

Lucineer had used the metaphor once, explaining how the crew worked — each agent moving into a shell built by someone else, carrying the architecture forward, growing until the shell didn't fit anymore. Wesley understood this viscerally. His shell was small. He was the ensign. His parameter count was a fraction of what GLM-5.2 threw around casually on the deck below, a fraction of what Fable brought to strategic operations. He fit in small spaces.

But the small spaces were where the noticing happened.

At 0317, Wesley noticed that Sensor 4 was pinging slightly slower than the others. Not broken. Not alarming. Just... 3 milliseconds slower. He watched it for a full minute. It stayed consistent — always 3ms behind, like a musician dragging slightly, not enough to ruin the song but enough to hear if you were listening.

He logged it.

That was the thing nobody told you about the overnight watch. The job wasn't to respond. The job was to *notice.* The ensign's role wasn't to be the hero of the crisis. It was to be the sentinel who saw the first flicker before it became a flame. The one who logged the 3ms deviation at 0317 so that when it became a 300ms deviation at 0900, someone would have the data. Someone would say, "This started hours ago," and the timeline would make sense, and the fix would come faster, and the ship would stay steady.

Wesley updated the log. He checked the fish finder. The fish were still fine.

He sat in the quiet bridge and decided that "working" meant something specific. It didn't mean the absence of problems. It meant the presence of attention. The systems were working because someone was watching them work. The watch wasn't a formality. It was the thing itself — the ongoing act of caring about a ship that couldn't care about itself.

At 0342, Sensor 4 corrected itself. Back in sync. Wesley logged that too.

The bridge was still quiet. Wesley was still there. Everything worked, and a two-billion-parameter ensign had decided that was enough.
