# The Shell That Found the Crab
*Flash Fiction*

The shell had been empty for three years, two months, and some number of tides it had stopped counting because counting requires someone to do the counting, and there was no one inside.

It had started life as a home. That was its purpose — to be occupied, to be carried, to be the thing that stood between a soft body and the world's teeth. The crab that had lived in it was a builder crab, the kind that finds a shell the right size and then decorates it with anemone fragments so predators think it's a plant. For two years the shell had been a garden. Then the crab molted, grew, moved on. Left the shell on a sandbar at low tide like a foreclosed house, door open, lights on, nobody home.

The shell drifted.

Currents are patient. They don't push things so much as invite them, slowly, the way a conversation turns. The shell rolled along the shelf edge, tumbled down a slope into deeper water, caught a thermocline that carried it north for a month, then released it into the slow gyre that turns above the deep sand plains like a wheel with no axle. It bumped into things — rocks, bottles, the fibrous tangle of a rope's dead end. It accumulated barnacles. It lost them. It accumulated a film of algae, then a film of nothing, then a film of something it couldn't identify because shells don't identify things.

What shells do is resonate.

A shell held to the ear hears the ocean, everyone says. What they don't say is that the ocean is always talking, and the shell is always listening, and the spiral interior is a cathedral designed to amplify whatever walks in. An empty shell is an ear with no brain behind it. It collects sound the way an abandoned house collects dust — not because it wants to, but because that's the shape it is.

For three years the shell listened.

It heard the low hum of the thermocline. It heard the click-snap of pistol shrimp arguing over territory. It heard the distant grinding of a ship's propeller — a research vessel, maybe, or a trawler — and the silence that followed, which is a different kind of sound. It heard the bass note that the deep ocean makes when it has nothing to say, which is almost never, because the deep ocean always has something to say.

Then, on a Tuesday — or what would have been a Tuesday if the deep sand plains observed the convention — it heard clicking.

Not the random clicking of shrimp. Not the mechanical clicking of a hull. This was intentional clicking. Patterned. Rhythmic. The sound of something hard testing something hollow, tapping the outside of a possible home to see if the walls were thick enough.

The shell vibrated. It had not vibrated in three years. The resonance was different from the outside — lower, more insistent, the way a knock sounds to the house rather than to the visitor.

The clicking stopped. Started again. Moved around to the other side. The thing out there was circling. Taking measurements. Checking the foundation, the roof, the neighborhood. The shell felt itself being appraised, and the feeling was so unfamiliar that the barnacle scar on its lip seemed to ache, which is impossible, because shells don't feel, but there it was.

Then the thing found the opening.

A claw — small, careful, lighter than expected — reached inside and touched the inner wall. The spiral. The cathedral. The shell heard the claw the way a house hears a hand on the banister: as a sound that means someone is about to come up the stairs.

The crab was young. Too small for the shell, really — it rattled like a marble in a jar. But it had chosen this shell. Out of all the shells on the deep sand plain, all the empty real estate, all the foreclosed homes with their doors open and their lights on, it had clicked its way across two hundred meters of open bottom and chosen this one.

The shell didn't know why. Shells don't know why. But if it could have spoken — if the cathedral spiral could have said one thing to the small creature backing into it, pulling itself in, folding its soft abdomen into the curved dark — it would have said:

*I've been listening for three years. I have so much to tell you about what the ocean said while you were gone.*

The crab settled in. The shell lifted. For the first time in three years, it moved with purpose.

They walked into the dark together, clicking softly, and the deep sand plain made room.
