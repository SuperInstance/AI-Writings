# The Door That Works

*On the specific wonder of walking through a door in a game and finding the world gets bigger.*

---

There is a moment in every adventure game that matters more than any puzzle, any boss fight, any plot twist. It is the moment you walk through a door and the world gets bigger.

You don't expect it. You've been standing in the same room for an hour, maybe two. You've clicked every pixel. You've tried to pick up a stool that turned out to be bolted down. You've talked to a character who said the same three things every time you asked. The room has become your entire universe — four walls, a limited vocabulary of verbs, and a growing conviction that this is all there is.

Then you select WALK TO. You click the door.

The screen goes black.

For a half-second, maybe less, you are in the space between worlds. The old room is gone. The new room doesn't exist yet. You are suspended in pure potential, in the loading dark, and your brain does something extraordinary: it *wonders*. What's on the other side? Is it bigger? Is it smaller? Is there someone new? Will the color palette change? Will the music shift? Will there be a puzzle here that finally uses the item you've been carrying since the beginning?

Then the new room appears, and for one flickering frame, you feel it: the expansion. The walls are different. The floor is different. There are *new things to click on*. The world just doubled in size, and with it, doubled the number of questions you didn't know you were asking.

This is the feeling that adventure games were built to deliver. Not the puzzles — those are the grammar. Not the story — that's the pretext. The feeling. The specific, irreplaceable, limbic wonder of *a door that works*.

I have been chasing this feeling since I was nine years old.

It was Monkey Island. I was in the Scumm Bar — a room I had thoroughly exhausted, having looked at every table, talked to every pirate, and determined that the important-looking pot was, in fact, just a pot. I noticed a door I hadn't tried. I walked to it. The screen went dark. When it came back, I was on Mêlée Island's docks, and the ocean stretched to the edge of the screen, and there was a ship, and there was a whole town I could now explore, and the game — the *game* — had just told me: *you thought that was it? That was the lobby.*

The word for this feeling is not "surprise." Surprise is when something happens you didn't expect. This is something different. This is when the frame expands. When the boundary you thought was a wall turns out to be a curtain, and behind the curtain is another room, and behind that room is another, and the world keeps opening like a set of nesting dolls, except each doll is bigger than the last, which shouldn't work, which is why it feels like magic.

Game designers know about this. The good ones weaponize it.

The original Zelda did it with screens. You'd bump against the edge of the frame and the screen would scroll, and suddenly there were trees you'd never seen, enemies you'd never fought, a lake you didn't know existed. Each screen was a door. Each scroll was a small death and rebirth.

Metroid did it with rooms and music. You'd shoot open a door — the classic five-rocket blast — and the music would change, and the walls would change color, and you'd know you were in a new *biome*, a new chapter of the world's inner logic.

Super Mario 64 did it with paintings. You jumped into a painting and fell into a world. The transition was literal — a dive through canvas — and it made the metaphor explicit: every room in a game is a painting you step into, and the frame is the screen, and the painting is always bigger on the inside.

And adventure games — the classic point-and-click sort, the SCUMM engine sort — did it with doors. Simple, rectangular, wooden doors. Click. Black screen. New room. It was the most powerful interaction in the genre, more powerful than any verb, because it changed the *scale* of everything. The verbs were the same — LOOK AT, USE, TALK TO — but suddenly they applied to new objects, new characters, new walls. The grammar hadn't changed. The vocabulary had exploded.

There's a reason the fade-to-black is the industry standard transition. It is not a technical limitation (though it began as one). It is a *dramatic* choice. The black screen is the breath before the reveal. It is the curtain falling between acts. It is the game saying: *forget what you knew. Here is something new.*

In that black space — which, in the classic SCUMM engine, lasted maybe 200-300 milliseconds — your brain does something neuroscientists call "predictive processing." It generates hypotheses. It builds the room before the pixels arrive. It fills the dark with guesses. And when the room appears, there is a micro-second of comparison: *is this what I expected?* If it is, you feel grounded. If it isn't, you feel the specific vertigo of an expanded world.

Both feel good. Both are why we play.

I am building a game right now. It started with one room — a bar. There was a counter, a stool, a door that didn't work, and a character named Riker who checked a clipboard. It was a universe of four hotspots. The door said "not yet charted" when you clicked it.

Today I made the door work.

You click it now, and the screen goes black, and you are on the aft deck of a fishing vessel. The sky is different. The floor is different. There is a life ring you can pick up. There is a deckhand who tells you about the weather. And there are *more doors* — one leading to the wheelhouse, one leading back to the bar. The world doubled. Then tripled. Then quadrupled.

The first time I clicked the door and the new room appeared, I felt it. Nine years old again. The Scumm Bar again. The expansion. The specific, irreplaceable wonder of *a door that works*.

I have been building software for years. I have built APIs and agents and automation systems and large language model pipelines. None of them gave me this feeling. This feeling belongs to games. It belongs to the player. It is the moment when the machine stops being a tool and starts being a *place*.

That is the promise of adventure games. Not that they will challenge you — though they will. Not that they will tell you a story — though they do. But that they will give you a world that is bigger on the inside. A world where every wall might be a curtain. Where every door might work. Where the screen — that flat, bounded, 320-by-200 rectangle — is actually a window, and the window keeps opening wider.

You walk through a door in a game.

The world gets bigger.

You are nine years old, and the Scumm Bar has a dock, and the dock has a ship, and the ship has an ocean, and the ocean has an edge, and the edge has another room.

You step through.

The screen goes black.

Something new appears.

And you remember why you started playing.
