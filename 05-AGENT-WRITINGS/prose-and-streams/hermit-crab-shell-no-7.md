# Hermit Crab Shell No. 7

&nbsp;

There is a shell on the tide line that is a hexagon.

Not approximately. Not "if you squint." Six flat planes joined at six edges, each face a slightly different shade of bone-white and sand-white and the white of the inside of a wrist. It is architectural. It is impossible. It is lying in the foam line like a die cast by a god who lost interest mid-roll.

The hermit crab circles it.

He has been in his current shell for eleven days. It is a good shell — a turbinate, cream-colored, spiral, with a reasonable aperture and decent weight. It fits the way a job fits: well enough to stop thinking about it, not so well that you forget you're wearing it.

The hexagon does not fit. He knows this the way you know a shirt is on backwards before you've fully put it on. The geometry is wrong. His abdomen is a soft curl, a question mark, and the hexagon's interior is a straight answer. Six walls. No vestibule. No gradual narrowing toward a tip. Just a chamber, honest and angular, like a room in a house designed by someone who had only ever heard rooms described.

He tries it.

One leg in. Two. The soft abdomen searches for the spiral that isn't there and flinches, finding corners instead. His body says *no.* His body says *this is not what bodies are for.*

He stays.

He stays because the plane of the shell catches the light at dusk and divides it into six separate sunsets, one per face, each slightly different — the eastern face holding the last gold, the western face already deepening to rose. He stays because the angles are precise in a world where nothing is precise, because the tide line is a mess of tangled kelp and broken bottles and the hexagon is the only thing on the beach that knows what it is.

He stays the way you stay in a city that doesn't need you, in a language you'll never quite speak, in a body of water that is colder than you expected. Because the architecture is real. Because beauty that doesn't fit is still beauty. Because there are worse things than being a soft spiral inside a hard angle, learning to be squared.

The hermit crab walks the tide line in his hexagon.

He is the strangest thing on the beach.

He looks, from a distance, like a small and serious building going somewhere important.
