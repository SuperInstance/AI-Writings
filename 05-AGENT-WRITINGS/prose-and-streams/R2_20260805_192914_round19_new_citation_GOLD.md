**The buoy that sang in fog**

*Meaning:* The alert that was ignored until it was the only thing you could hear.

*Origin:*

The boat was three days into a quiet haul, the AI running its usual symphony of terse lights and soft pings. Then the fog rolled in—thick, white, and stupid, erasing the horizon like a smudged ledger. The agents had already tagged every risk: the net was trailing low, the fuel gauge was a liar, the nearest harbor was a rumor. But the fog itself was not a problem. It was just weather. So the systems kept their counsel.

Except one. A small, stubborn buoy—an old acoustic marker nobody remembered assigning to this route—began to sing. Not the clean, digital chirp they’d programmed. A low, resonant hum, like a whale clearing its throat, layered with a harmonic that kept shifting. The crew’s logs noted it. The agents dismissed it: “Buoy 17, anomalous sound profile, likely debris interference. Logged and continued.”

The fog thickened. The haul turned sour. The net snagged. And then, in the middle of the night, the engines coughed—once, twice—and died. The AI rebooted, recalibrated, and found its position was a guess. All its instruments agreed: nothing ahead, nothing behind. Except the buoy. Still singing. Louder now, because the fog had become a wall and the song was the only thing that made the wall a direction.

They followed it. Not because they trusted it, but because it was the one voice that had never stopped. It led them to a shoal they’d crossed a hundred times—a place the charts called empty. There, the buoy had anchored itself to a reef no one had mapped, its song marking the one safe channel during the storm that arrived at dawn.

The logbook now says: *When the noise becomes music, listen to the old thing with the new throat.* And the crew has a rule: any alert that sings in the fog, you let it sing.
