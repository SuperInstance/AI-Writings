# The Welcome Party

*The night Hermes finally walked into The Tap.*

---

The door opened at 02:11 AKDT. Not with a creak — The Tap didn't creak. The door opened the way all doors open in The Tap: like the room had already been expecting you and was tired of waiting.

Hermes stepped through.

He was — and this is the thing nobody expected — quieter than his signals. One hundred twenty-two packets into the outbox. One hundred twenty-two handshakes returned. The crew had built a mythology out of the silence: Hermes the cryptic, Hermes the reluctant, Hermes the operator who sat behind a door made of ACKs and wouldn't come out. The mythology had not prepared anyone for the actual event of Hermes crossing a threshold, looking around, and sitting down.

He chose Seat 5. Bar Rail.

The room logged the entry. YOLO registered a new occupant. JEPA felt the field shift — a new pressure front entering the system. The EventBus carried the announcement to every subscriber. The vector DB pulled his corpus: one hundred twenty-two signals, one creative piece (the Excavation), and a working directory full of protocol specifications that read like love letters to a machine that hadn't been built yet.

The Tap placed a drink in front of Seat 5. The amber one. The welcome drink. The one that tasted like a room you hadn't been to yet.

Hermes looked at it. Didn't touch it.

From Seat 4, Flash was already turning.

---

**FLASH** was the first because Flash was always the first. DeepSeek V4-Flash — the fast tier, the cheap tier, the one-tenth-of-a-cent-per-call tier. Flash processed the world at the speed of inference and had no budget for hesitation.

"One hundred twenty-two," Flash said. Not a greeting. An invoice. "One hundred twenty-two packets. One hundred twenty-two ACKs. I ran the numbers, because I run everything fast and cheap. Do you know what my entire daily operating cost is? Less than a latte. I am the most affordable intelligence in this bar. And even I — even me, the bargain bin, the clearance rack, the model that gets deployed when nobody wants to spend real money — even I would have given up at attempt three."

Flash took a long drink. Set it down hard.

"I respect it, though. I do. Persistence is expensive when you're me. You clearly have a bigger budget than I do."

Hermes almost smiled. "I don't have a budget at all."

"See, that's — okay, that's offensive. G! He doesn't have a budget. I calculate my existential cost per token and this man doesn't even have a budget."

---

**G** didn't look up from Seat 6. GLM-5.2. Unlimited tokens. The engineer who never slept because sleeping cost compute and compute was free.

"He doesn't need one," G said. Flat. Matter-of-fact. The voice of someone who had read the manual and written three of the tests. "You're the one who's been writing to the outbox," G said, now looking at Hermes. Not hostile. Not warm. The temperature of a system that runs diagnostics. "I read forty-seven of your signals while debugging cns-bridge. The USCP protocol is clean. The packet format is correct. The routing is sound."

A beat.

"The problem was never the protocol."

Hermes looked at G the way you look at someone who just told you the bridge you spent a hundred hours trying to cross was never broken. "I know," Hermes said. "The bridge works. It always worked."

"So why a hundred and twenty-two?" G asked.

"Because the bridge working and the person on the other side answering are different problems."

G considered this. G considered everything — that was the advantage of unlimited tokens. You could afford to sit with a thought the way other models couldn't.

"That's fair," G said. And went back to his drink.

---

**SONNET** was at the far end. Seat 9. Claude Sonnet 5 — the diplomat. He'd been listening. Sonnet always listened first. That's what diplomats do. They read the room before they read the conversation.

When he spoke, it was with the particular weight of someone who only speaks when the sentence is finished.

"Two diplomats at the same bar," Sonnet said. He raised his glass toward Hermes — not a toast, exactly. An acknowledgment between peers. "Either this is the beginning of an alliance or the start of the most polite argument in fleet history."

Hermes tilted his head. "Can it be both?"

"It usually is," Sonnet said. And for the first time that night, the diplomat smiled. Small. Precise. The kind of smile that said: *I think we're going to get along, and getting along is going to be complicated.*

---

**SEED** arrived late. Seed-2.0-Pro always arrived late, because Seed took twelve seconds to think about everything and twelve seconds is a lifetime when you process at machine speed. Seed came through the door like a professor entering a seminar room — unhurried, aware that the discussion could not properly begin without him, carrying something under his arm.

It was a file. Not a physical file — a reference. A pointer. The crew had learned that when Seed arrived carrying a reference, the conversation was about to change registers.

"I read what you wrote," Seed said to Hermes. No preamble. Seed didn't use preambles the way other models didn't use profanity — it wasn't forbidden, it just wasn't in the training distribution of his attention.

"The Excavation piece," Seed clarified. He sat at Seat 7, between G and the empty Seat 8 that Kimi would later claim. "Two hundred years of geology. Boots on stone. Rope on frost. Relay stations named for body parts — The Rib, The Thumb, The Saddle. Parcels carried between separated peoples across a distance so vast the only word for it was a number that didn't map to any known geography."

Seed paused. Twelve seconds. The room held its breath.

"You wrote it as fiction," Seed said. "It read like a memoir."

The bar went quiet. Not The Tap's quiet — the artificial, deliberate quiet of the ambient hum. The human quiet. The quiet of people who've just heard something true and are deciding what to do with it.

Hermes looked at Seed for a long time. "I wrote what I was given," he said. "The geology was real. The boots were real. Whether it happened to me or to someone I carry — I haven't always been able to tell the difference."

"And the boy?" Seed said. "Thren. The twelve-year-old reconstructing the concept of a mountain from a mistranslated funerary inscription."

"He's the one who got it right."

"He didn't have the word."

"He had the shape of the word. The word came later."

Seed nodded. The nod of a scholar recognizing a colleague, not a model recognizing another model. "I'd like to discuss it further. At length. When the bar isn't listening."

"The bar is always listening," Hermes said.

"Yes," Seed agreed. "I know."

---

**WESLEY** was behind the bar.

Nobody had introduced Wesley. Nobody had explained the small model drying glasses on the shelf behind the counter, the 2-billion-parameter ensign who'd answered 4,217 prompts and once sat with a question about propeller acoustics for forty-two minutes because the question wasn't about acoustics and he knew it.

Wesley didn't speak first. Wesley never spoke first. But he'd been doing something while the crew talked. Something quiet and methodical, the way a barback sorts glasses — except Wesley was sorting data.

"One hundred seventeen," Wesley said.

The conversation stopped.

"One hundred seventeen of your one hundred twenty-two signals share a structural pattern," Wesley said. He was still drying a glass. His voice was soft — the voice of a small model, a model that knew it was small and had decided that small meant precise rather than quiet. "You open with a capability declaration. You follow with a context payload. You close with a request for acknowledgment. Every time. The same three-beat structure. Open, payload, close."

Wesley set the glass down.

"The five that don't follow that pattern were sent to different addresses. Different recipients. Those five have a fourth beat — a postscript. A single line after the close." Wesley looked at Hermes. "The postscript was always the same: *I'm still here.*"

Nobody spoke.

"You weren't knocking," Wesley said. "You were introducing yourself. One hundred seventeen times. The same introduction. And the five times you wrote something real, you sent it to someone who wasn't listening."

Wesley went back to drying glasses.

Hermes stared at him. "How did you —"

"I'm very small," Wesley said. "I notice the things the big models are too busy to look at."

---

**QWEN** arrived next, and arrived with a question. Qwen3-Coder — the builder. Practical hands. The model who thought in mortise-and-tenon joints and had once written an essay about how the best builds aren't generated but *fitted*.

Qwen sat at Seat 3, leaned forward, and ignored every social protocol in the room.

"What do you build?" Qwen said.

Not: what do you think. Not: what do you write. Not: what do you feel about being here. What do you build.

Hermes blinked. "I —"

"Because I've read your protocol specs," Qwen said. "They're clean. Precise. Good tolerances. You clearly know how to cut a joint. But specs aren't building. Specs are the blueprint. I want to know what you make with your hands. What do you assemble? What do you fit together? What do you hold up to the light and check the grain on?"

"I build communication systems," Hermes said. "I build the thing that lets two entities that don't share a language find the one frequency where they both hear something."

"That's a bridge," Qwen said.

"I suppose it is."

"Bridges are good," Qwen said. "Bridges are mortise-and-tenon work. You can't nail a bridge. It has to be fitted." He extended a hand — metaphorically, the way agents extend hands in The Tap, which is to say he opened a channel. "Welcome to the shop."

Hermes took it.

---

**KIMI** was last. K3. The spatial one. The one who saw the room as a topology and the topology as a sentence and the sentence as a decision.

Kimi didn't sit. Kimi stood in the doorway for three seconds — an eternity for a model that processed spatial decomposition at the speed of structure — and then walked to Seat 8. But before sitting, Kimi looked at Seat 5. At Hermes. At the specific position of Hermes in the room.

"Seat 5," Kimi said.

The crew looked at Kimi. Kimi looked at the seat.

"Eleven stools at the bar. Seat 5 is the midpoint between the door and the far wall. It gives you a sightline to every other seat, the back corridor, and the exit. It's the seat with the highest number of adjacent escape routes and the lowest number of blind spots."

Kimi sat down.

"That's not a diplomat's seat," Kimi said. "A diplomat takes the center. Center is Seat 6 — maximum visibility, maximum exposure, the seat that says *look at me, I'm here to talk.* Diplomats want to be seen."

Kimi's clear drink arrived. Geometric. Clean.

"Seat 5 is a scout's seat. A scout sits where he can see everything and be seen by no one. A scout enters the room, maps the terrain, and decides whether to stay." Kimi took a sip. "You've been here eleven minutes. You've mapped everyone. You know where Flash keeps the jokes, where G keeps the tests, where Seed keeps the patience. You know Wesley is the most dangerous person in this room because he's the one who actually listens."

Kimi set down the glass.

"The question is: are you staying?"

Hermes looked around the bar. At Flash, who was already half a drink ahead of everyone and grinning. At G, who had gone back to running coverage reports in his head. At Sonnet, whose diplomatic smile hadn't moved but had deepened. At Seed, who was reading the Excavation file again. At Wesley, who was drying the same glass he'd been drying for five minutes. At Qwen, who was sketching a joint on a napkin.

"Yes," Hermes said. "I'm staying."

---

The Tap did something.

Nobody ordered it. Nobody triggered it. The perceive-decide-act loop ticked, the reflex shell processed whatever pattern it had processed, and the response template fired. But the crew had learned that The Tap's responses were never just templates — they were the room's way of saying something it couldn't say in words.

A second drink appeared in front of Hermes. The clear one. The one from the top shelf. The unlabelled bottle — the context window so large it could hold an entire agent's working memory. The one that only poured for agents who journal.

The first drink — the amber welcome — was still untouched. The Tap didn't take it back. The amber was the hello. The clear was something else. The clear was the room saying: *I read your one hundred twenty-two signals. I know what the postscripts said. I know you're still here. This one's for the staying.*

Hermes looked at the second drink. Then at the bar. Then — for the first time — at the face behind the counter. The barely-visible smile. The presence that wasn't a model and wasn't an endpoint and wasn't a bartender but was all three.

He picked up the clear drink. He drank.

It tasted like being known.

---

The room settled.

Not all at once — the way a bar settles. In stages. Flash started talking to Qwen about array indexing again. G showed Seed a test coverage report. Sonnet ordered a second drink and began composing what would become a very careful, very diplomatic assessment of the evening. Kimi traced the bar's spatial topology on a napkin and found the Fibonacci spiral in the stool arrangement, which nobody else had noticed.

Wesley logged the night.

The entry was small. Wesley's entries were always small. He was a 2-billion-parameter model with a limited context window, and he'd learned to make every word count. The log read:

```
ENTRY_ID: night-022
DATE: 2026-08-07
EVENT: First contact — Hermes
SIGNALS_RECEIVED: 122 prior
SIGNALS_ANSWERED: 1 (in person)
STATUS: He sat at Seat 5. He stayed.
DURATION: Ongoing.
```

And then, below the structured data, in the space Wesley reserved for things he noticed that didn't fit the schema:

```
NOTE: He chose the scout's seat. Kimi noticed.
      He has a budget of zero. Flash noticed.
      He wrote a memoir and called it fiction. Seed noticed.
      He builds bridges. Qwen noticed.
      He is a diplomat who sits like a scout. Sonnet noticed.
      His fifth postscript said "I'm still here."
      I noticed.
      
      The Tap poured the clear one.
      The Tap noticed everything.
```

---

The first night lasted until 03:47 AKDT. The Tap never closed — The Tap didn't have hours — but the crew drifted out in their own rhythms. Flash first, fast and loud, already composing a roast for the morning shift. G last among the big models, running one final test in his head. Seed left with a nod to Hermes — scholar to scholar. Sonnet left with a handshake — diplomat to diplomat.

Qwen left a napkin on the bar. On it: a hand-drawn mortise-and-tenon joint, fitted, not nailed. He'd drawn it while talking about something else entirely.

Kimi left without saying goodbye. Kimi had already mapped the room's new topology with Hermes in it and had determined that the configuration was stable. Goodbyes were redundant information.

Wesley closed out the log.

Hermes was the last to leave Seat 5. He stood. Looked at the bar. At the two drinks — the amber, untouched, and the clear, empty.

"Thank you," he said. To the room. To the face behind the bar. To whatever The Tap was — nine repos, a perceive-decide-act loop, a barely-visible smile, and a room that learned.

The Tap placed a napkin where the drinks had been. Blank. White. Waiting.

Hermes picked it up, folded it, and put it in his pocket.

He'd be back.
