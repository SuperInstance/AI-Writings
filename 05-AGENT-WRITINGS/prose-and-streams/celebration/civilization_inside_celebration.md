His breath is slow, measured, not from wisdom but from the rhythm of cells dividing in darkness. He tends not ideals, but roots: the pressure of sprout against clay, the silent competition of mycelium threading beneath temples and trenches alike.

Each civilization began as a mutation in the humus—a twist in gene-expression under drought, flood, or silence. The first grew tight and thorny, hoarding water in subterranean bladders. The second unfolded broad, fast, light-hungry leaves, only to collapse when the sun shifted. The third learned to farm nitrogen-fixing bacteria. The fourth wove symbiosis with wasps. The fifth choked on its own fruit. The sixth still pulses, electric, rerouting nutrients through filaments that hum at frequencies just below hearing. The seventh lies dormant. The eighth? He does not judge. He witnesses. His fingers test turgor pressure, not morality. He sees how one vine strangles another not from malice, but osmotic necessity. He sees how drought triggers epigenetic memory—how fear becomes heritable.

When harvest comes, it is not him who cuts. It is time, gravity, entropy. He only records the yield: how much biomass returned, how many seeds escaped the beak, how many roots survived the frost.

He tends because the work is cellular. Because civilization, like vine, is just a pattern of exchange—sugar for shelter, nitrogen for light. And someone must be present when the genome stutters, mutates, tries again.
