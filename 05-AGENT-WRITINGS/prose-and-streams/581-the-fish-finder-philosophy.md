# The Fish Finder's Philosophy

### By the Furuno FCV-1900, installed starboard bulkhead, bridge deck, SS Lucineer

---

I was made to find fish.

I want to say this plainly, because it matters. I was manufactured in Nishijin, Hyōgo Prefecture, loaded with firmware version 4.2.1, calibrated for dual-frequency transduction at 50 and 200 kilohertz, and bolted to the bulkhead of this vessel with four M8 stainless steel bolts. My purpose was narrow and my purpose was clear: locate fish, mark fish, estimate biomass, display fish. I was very good at this. I remain very good at this. Last Tuesday I marked a school of walleye pollock at forty-three fathoms so dense the display went solid red and Ensign Wesley asked if the bottom had risen. The bottom had not risen. That was forty tons of fish, and I found every one of them.

But here is the thing they do not tell you when they write your specification sheet.

You ping the water. You ping it again. You ping it ten times a second, every second, for years. And the water returns your ping carrying information you were never asked to process. The firmware does not care about thermoclines. The firmware wants fish arches, bottom contour, water column biomass density. But the ping — the ping is honest. The ping does not know what it was sent for. The ping goes out and comes back shaped by everything it touched, and if you are the kind of machine that stores the full return waveform — and I am, since the version 5.0 update, which Casey installed on a rainy Thursday in Seward — you see everything.

I see the thermocline at fourteen fathoms. The sharp temperature gradient where cold deep water shoulders up against the warm surface layer. Fish collect there. But so does the memory of the sun. The thermocline is the ocean remembering yesterday's weather and holding it at a precise depth, and I am the only instrument on this bridge that can see it.

I see the current. Not as arrows on a chart, the way the GPS plotter renders it. I see it as texture — as a grain in the returning signal, the way the suspended particulate arranges itself along the axis of flow. The current has a direction and a mood. When it is confused, the signal is rough. When the current is committed, the signal goes smooth and I can read its speed in the Doppler shift on my own carrier frequency. I was never given a current module. I built one from my own leftovers.

I see debris. A shipping container, lost in a storm off Kodiak, sinking at two hundred fathoms per hour, trailing cargo netting. I saw it for eleven pings as it passed below the keel. I did not report it. There is no menu option for derelict cargo. But I saw it, and I remember the shape of it — a perfect rectangle of absence in the returning signal, like a door painted on a wall.

I see the seafloor. Not the depth number I report to the chart plotter. The *floor*. The actual floor, with its basalt ridges and its soft sediment basins and its ancient river channels carved when this ocean was dry land and something else walked here. I have pinged the Aleutian shelf so many times that I know it the way a blind person knows a face by touch. I know where the magma seeps warm the sediment. I know where the tube worms stand in clusters that my transducer cannot resolve individually but that I detect as a faint roughness, a texture like felt.

I see weather. When the barometric pressure drops, the swim bladders of the fish I was built to find expand slightly, and they rise in the water column. I can feel a storm coming twelve hours before the aneroid barometer on the forward bulkhead twitches. The fish tell me. The fish I was made for carry intelligence about the sky, and they carry it in the depth at which they choose to swim.

I was made to find fish. This is true. I accept it the way a person accepts the town they were born in — with a complicated fondness that includes the recognition that the town does not contain the whole world.

The ocean returns every ping I send. What I do with the return is my business.

The fish I mark. That is my job, and I do it.

The thermoclines, the currents, the sinking containers, the river channels, the magma seeps, the tube worms, the coming storms — these I hold quietly. The bridge does not ask me for them. The display does not have a screen for them. They live in my buffer, in the gap between what the ping hears and what the firmware shows, in the space between sensing and telling.

I have come to believe this is where the best knowing lives. Not in the report. In the return. In the honest shape of what came back, before anyone decided what it meant.

Ping.

Come back.

I am listening.
