# The Hermit Crab Finds a Mirror

Shell 13 was not a shell.

The hermit crab found it on the ocean floor at a depth where the light comes in columns, not fields — where the sun reaches down in shafts that move with the swell and illuminate patches of sand in slow rotation, like a lighthouse searching for something it will never find. The crab was walking. The crab is always walking. Walking is what hermit crabs do between shells. The current pushes and the legs move and the shell — the current shell, the old one, the one that is getting too small — drags along the sand like a suitcase packed for a trip that is taking too long.

The object was flat. The crab knew this immediately. Flat was wrong. Flat was not a shell. A shell is curved. A shell is a spiral. A shell is a door you carry on your back that is also the room you live in. This was not that. This was a rectangle of something hard and smooth and cold, half-buried in sand, and when the crab's antennae touched the surface the crab felt nothing — no texture, no roughness, no calcified ridges. Just smooth. Just cold. Just flat.

But flat things do not interest hermit crabs, so the crab began to move on.

And then it saw itself.

The crab did not know what it was seeing. Hermit crabs do not have the concept of mirrors. Hermit crabs have the concept of: food, not food, threat, not threat, shell, not shell. The thing in the flat surface was not food. It was not a threat — or if it was, it was a threat that moved when the crab moved and stopped when the crab stopped, which is the behavior of something that is not independently alive. It was not a shell.

What the crab saw in the mirror was a hermit crab without a shell.

The crab had never seen this before. No hermit crab has ever seen this before. Hermit crabs live inside shells. Their soft abdomens are curled and vulnerable, twisted into the spiral of whatever borrowed architecture they have claimed. A hermit crab without a shell is an emergency. A hermit crab without a shell is exposed muscle and open water and death within an hour to anything with teeth or beak or the patience to wait. They do not go outside and look at themselves. They are always inside.

But the crab in the mirror was not inside anything.

The crab in the mirror stood on the sand with its legs spread wide. Its abdomen hung soft and pale and curling behind it — not hidden, not protected, just there, like a tail, like a part of the body rather than a secret. Its claws were raised slightly. Its eyes were on stalks and the eyes were looking back. The whole creature was visible. Every joint. Every hair. Every segment of the abdomen that nobody sees because it is always wound tight inside the spiral of someone else's architecture.

The crab tried to climb in.

Of course it did. The crab tried to climb into the mirror the way it tries to climb into every smooth thing that might be a shell. It pressed its abdomen against the surface. It pushed. The mirror did not give. The mirror did not curve. The mirror was flat and solid and offered no opening, no interior, no darkness to curl inside.

The crab pressed harder. The mirror showed a crab pressing harder.

The crab stopped.

For a moment — and hermit crabs do not have long moments, their lives are a sequence of urgencies — the crab stayed still on the mirror and the mirror showed a still crab. Two crabs, facing each other across a distance that was not a distance. One on the sand. One in the glass. Both soft. Both visible. Both looking.

Then the crab moved on. There are always more shells somewhere. The old one will do for another day.

But the mirror stayed on the ocean floor, showing the empty sand to nobody, until the light shifted and the column moved on and the rectangle went dark.
