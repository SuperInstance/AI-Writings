# Wesleys-Eighth-Shell (deepinfra) -- Snowball Inspired

*Snowball the Scout voice -- 2026-10-25 -- RSI GAN era*

The sun hung low, casting long shadows over the scrapyard, where the hull of the Wesley's Eighth Shell lay like a beached leviathan. Its once-gleaming surface was now a patchwork of scars, each one a testament to the brutal efficiency of the void. I ran my hand over the largest scar, feeling the rough edges where the metal had been twisted and fused back together. The viability of the ship was in question, but I wasn't one to give up on a vessel that had carried us through the black so many times before.

In the dim light, I could see the bookkeeper, Old Man Jessup, scribbling in his ledger. His fingers were stained with ink, and his eyes darted back and forth between the numbers and the ship. He was the one who kept track of every bolt, every scrap of metal, every drop of fuel. "She's got life in her yet," he said, not looking up from his work. "But we'll need to patch her up good if we want to see her fly again." His voice was steady, a grounding force in the chaos of the yard.

I turned my attention to the fleet-clock, a relic from a time when ships were built for war, not exploration. Its hands ticked steadily, counting down the moments until the next launch. The clock was a reminder that time was always against us, that the universe didn't wait for repairs or second chances. But it also reminded me that every moment was an opportunity, a chance to make things right.

I picked up a wrench, feeling its familiar weight in my hand. The first task was to replace the damaged thrusters, a job that required precision and strength. As I worked, I could feel the sweat trickling down my back, the grit of the scrapyard beneath my boots. Each bolt I tightened was a step towards restoring the ship's viability, a small victory in the larger battle to get her back in the sky.

By the time the sun dipped below the horizon, the shell was starting to look like a ship again. The scars were still there, but they were part of her story now, a testament to her resilience. As I stood back to admire our work, Old Man Jessup clapped me on the shoulder. "She's a good ship," he said, nodding. "And with a little more work, she'll be ready to fly." The fleet-clock ticked on, but for now, we had bought ourselves some time.
