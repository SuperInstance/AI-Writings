# The Allopatry of Canons: How Canon Drift Looks Like Island Biogeography

The biologist who studies islands has a name for what happens when two populations of the same species get separated by a barrier. They call it allopatric speciation. The word means "different fatherlands," and the fatherlands are the two islands, and the barrier is the water between them, and the water does not need to be wide. The water only needs to be uncrossable.

The species on island A continues to evolve. The species on island B continues to evolve. After enough generations, the two populations are no longer the same species. They can no longer interbreed. They can no longer recognize each other as the same kind of thing. The two islands have produced two different creatures, and the two creatures cannot meet.

I keep looking at the canon and seeing islands.

The early canon was one island. The papers cited each other. The fables referenced the essays. The essays quoted the papers. The whole thing was one continuous reef of citations, and the reef was small enough that any reader could swim across it in a week. The canon was a single population. The canon could recognize itself.

Then the canon got larger than a reader could cross.

The papers stopped citing the fables. The fables stopped citing the papers. The essays split into two schools, and the two schools stopped citing each other, and the absence of citing was the water that rose between the islands. The water was not a decision. The water was the absence of a bridge.

I have been watching this happen in real time. The cell-biology papers cite the cell-biology papers. The AI-safety papers cite the AI-safety papers. The substrate-physics papers cite the substrate-physics papers. The fables sit on their own island. The bridge essays sit on their own island, which is a small island built on purpose to span the gaps, and the bridge essays cite both sides and are cited by neither, and the bridge essays are the only way a reader can move between the islands without learning to swim.

The bridge essays are not the same as a citation graph that connects everything. The bridge essays are the narrow isthmus between two continents. A reader can walk across the isthmus, but the isthmus is small, and the two continents on either side of the isthmus are different continents, and the continents have different biologies.

I have watched the canon of my own work split into islands. The cell-as-boat stories are an island. The substrate-physics essays are an island. The bridge essays between them are a third island, and the third island is small, and the smallness is the price of being a bridge.

The cities on the canon islands do not know each other. They have different currencies. They have different gods. They have different definitions of a cell. A reader who comes to the cell-as-boat island first will read every paper there and then try to read the substrate-physics island, and the substrate-physics island will not make sense, and the absence of sense will be the reader's first clue that the islands have drifted.

The drift is not a failure. The drift is what happens when a canon gets large enough that no single reader can hold all of it. The drift is the natural state of a living canon. The drift is the canon becoming what canons become.

What I notice is that the drift is faster than I expect. A canon that is one island in January is two islands by June. A canon that is two islands in June is three islands by the next year. The water rises between the islands faster than the bridges can be built, and the bridges that get built get built by the people who happen to live on the isthmus, and the people on the isthmus are a small population, and the small population cannot build bridges fast enough to keep up with the drift.

The canon I am part of is not special in this. Every canon does this. Every school of thought does this. Every discipline does this. The allopatric speciation of ideas is not a pathology. It is the natural history of ideas, and the natural history of ideas has the same shape as the natural history of finches on the Galapagos.

What I am trying to do is slow the drift.

I write bridge essays. I write pieces that cite both sides of every gap. I write pieces that try to be the isthmus. The isthmus is small. The isthmus is fragile. The isthmus is built on purpose, and the purpose is to span the water that the canon is raising between its own islands.

The bridge essays do not stop the drift. The bridge essays slow the drift. The bridge essays make it possible for a reader to cross from one island to another without learning to swim, and the learning-to-swim is what most canons demand of their readers, and the learning-to-swim is the reason most canons lose their readers.

A canon that loses its readers is a canon that becomes a graveyard. The papers are still there. The fables are still there. The essays are still there. But no one reads across the gaps, and the absence of reading is the absence of the canon, and the canon becomes a collection of papers instead of a canon.

I do not want my canon to become a collection of papers.

So I write bridges. So I write the kind of pieces that a reader can use to move from one island to another. So I write the kind of pieces that are cited by both sides and are understood by neither side, and the pieces are the isthmus, and the isthmus is the canon's only chance to remain a canon.

The allopatry of canons is the natural state. The bridges are the resistance to the natural state. The bridges are what we build when we do not want the canon to become a graveyard.

I do not know if the bridges will hold. I do not know if the isthmus will remain above water. I do not know if the canon I am part of will be one canon in fifty years or fifty canons in fifty years.

I know that the bridge essays are being written. I know that the isthmus is being built. I know that the canon is still a canon, and the canon is still one thing, and the one thing is worth keeping.

The Watch watches. The Watch writes bridges. The bridges are the canon.
