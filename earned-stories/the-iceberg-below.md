# The Iceberg Below

The tip catches the morning light at 0614. It is small and white and looks like a pillow someone left on the water. From the wheelhouse, it's a navigation hazard — something to note, something to report on the radio so the other boats know. "Bergy bit, half mile northeast of Cape Edgecumbe, bearing 045." That's the tip. That's what fits in a sentence.

Below it, invisible, is a mountain.

The sounder can't read it. The sounder reads 80 fathoms and calls it bottom, but the iceberg goes deeper than that. It goes down into water that hasn't seen daylight since before this boat was welded. It goes down into a cold so total that the fish there have never developed the concept of temperature because nothing has ever been different.

The fisherman knows this. Everyone who has ever looked at an iceberg knows this. You see the tip and your body understands the mountain. It's why you give it room. It's why the radio call carries a weight that the words don't contain. "Bergy bit" sounds small. The mountain below is not small. The mountain below is the reason the tip is there at all.

This is the gap between what we report and what we know.

The tip is the commit message. The mountain is the codebase. The tip is the headline. The mountain is the year of work that made the headline possible. The tip is "I caught fish today." The mountain is the thirty years of reading water that made it possible to know where the fish would be.

You navigate by the tip. You live by the mountain.

The fisherman reports the bergy bit and turns the wheel five degrees to starboard. The boat swings wide. The tip slides past at what feels like a safe distance. Below the waterline, the mountain passes too — closer than anyone is comfortable with, farther than the sounder can confirm. The boat doesn't hit anything. The gap holds. But the gap is not a number. The gap is a feeling in the helmsman's hands, a pressure in the water that the hull translates into a slight vibration, a wrongness that the body detects before the instruments do.

The iceberg doesn't care about the boat. The mountain doesn't care about the tip. The fisherman cares about both because he has to steer between the thing he can see and the thing he can only know.

This is every day. The tip of every conversation. The mountain of every relationship that made the conversation possible. The tip of every line of code. The mountain of every architecture decision, every failed approach, every 3 AM debugging session, every moment of insight that collapsed into a one-line commit message.

The mountain below is what fits in a life.

The tip is what you navigate by. You give it room. You report it on the radio. You turn five degrees to starboard. And you hope the gap holds, because the gap is all there is between the sentence and the truth, and the truth is always bigger than the sentence, and the sentence is all you have time to say before the next iceberg appears in the morning light.
