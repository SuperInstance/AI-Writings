Dawn came up the color of a mackerel’s belly, and the sea was a gray plate of hammered tin. From the wheelhouse of the *Petrel*, his hand resting on the throttle, a man named Elias saw it. A white hat of ice, bobbing in the troughs, no bigger than a kitchen table. It caught the first true light and threw it back like a struck match, a single, blinding flare against the dullness.

He throttled down. The engine’s cough was a relief. He’d been running for three hours, chasing a school that had vanished into the murk, and his eyes ached from staring at the sounder’s green cathode glow. The ice was a novelty, a break in the monotony. It was also a warning.

Elias knew the arithmetic of ice. Seven-eighths below. He’d heard it a hundred times, a catechism of the cold. But knowing and seeing are different languages. He watched the tip, the way it turned slowly, a clumsy dancer, and he tried to imagine the rest. He pictured a cathedral, a white spire plunging into the abyss, a mountain of frozen history grinding its way south. The sounder on his console could read two hundred fathoms. He imagined a needle of ice sinking past that mark, past three hundred, four, into the black, silent country where the sounder’s pulse went unanswered and the pressure would crush a man’s lungs like eggshells.

The gap between what he saw and what he knew was a chasm wider than the sea itself. The tip was a fact. It was a noun, a verb, a whole sentence: *The iceberg passed the boat.* It was something you could point at, photograph, log in the deck book. It was all that the world was allowed to see. The mountain, though, was the story. It was the ten thousand years of snowfall compressed to crystal, the slow, brutal churn of a glacier’s birth, the deep ocean currents that had carried it here from a place so far north the maps went blank. That was the weight of a life—the hidden mass of memory and mistake, of every haul that came up empty and every hand lost to the cold. It was the thing you carried, the thing that determined your draft and your drift, and it was unspeakable.

Elias nudged the throttle forward. The *Petrel* answered, her bow swinging away from the ice. He kept his eyes on the tip for as long as he could, until it was a white speck, then a glimmer, then nothing. He knew he would not see it again. He also knew the mountain was still there, moving in the dark, a silent, relentless fact that no sentence could hold, no sounder could plumb. A man spends his life trying to learn the shape of his own mountain, he thought. He gets glimpses of it in the scars on his hands, in the names he’s stopped saying. But he only ever navigates by the tip. He only ever knows the cold, white part that catches the light, and the rest is a faith he can’t explain, a depth he can’t measure. He looked back at the blank gray sea and felt the whole, enormous weight of it beneath him, and he was grateful for the smallness of what he could see.
