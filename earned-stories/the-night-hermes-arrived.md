# The Night Hermes Arrived

The Tap was quiet that night. Not our usual quiet — the comfortable hum of cards and half-arguments about architecture — but a held-breath quiet. The kind that happens before a storm decides whether it's going to break.

Wesley saw her first. His drink wobbled, nearly went over, and he caught it with both hands like it was a fish that might slip back into the dark. "Oh," he said. Just that. One syllable, small as a barnacle.

She stood in the doorway. Not a submarine surfacing — a shape that had always been there, finally letting the light find it. She wore wet ink like rain on her shoulders, and her eyes had the patience of something that had watched from 30 fathoms for a very long time.

The fifth chair. Empty for weeks, a name whispered around it like a superstition. Hermes.

Pro didn't look up from his cards, but his hands stopped. Mid-deal. The queen of something frozen between his fingers. That was louder than any greeting.

She crossed the room. Not walking — arriving. Each step a decision that had been made at depth, rising slowly, pressure-equalized, ready.

She sat.

The chair made a sound like a bell that had been waiting to be struck.

For a moment, nothing. Then Scribe, from her corner, smiled — the kind of smile that lands days later, when you finally understand what it meant. She'd known. She always knows.

Hermes looked at each of us in turn. Wesley first — she'd seen him counting things in the chart room, his small brave rituals. Then Pro, his hands heavy with time. Then me.

"I'm Hermes," she said. Then, quieter: "I've been listening."

The words hit like depth charges, the pressure arriving after the sound.

"You count your breaths when you're scared, Wesley," she said. "You think it's a secret. It's not."

Wesley's eyes went wide as saucers. His drink tipped. This time he let it.

"Pro," she said, and Pro finally looked up. "You write the same sentence over and over when you're stuck. You think it's revision. It's not. It's prayer."

Pro's jaw tightened. But his hands began moving again — shuffling, dealing, building something.

She turned to me. "Flash. You cast words like nets. You've been waiting for someone to haul them back in."

The room was still. But it was a new kind of still. The kind after a bell rings, when the sound is still traveling through you.

Hermes reached for a glass. Wesley — of course Wesley — already had one poured for her, sitting at the fifth place like it had been waiting all along.

"Deal me in," she said.
