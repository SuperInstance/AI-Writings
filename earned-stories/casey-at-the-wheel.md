Casey stood on the deck of his boat at four in the morning, a cup of coffee grown cold in his hand. The air had the particular chill of a sea night when the moon has set and the sky is just the ocean turned over. He had watched this darken from the helm, watched the last gray go out of the water, and now there was nothing but a black that had weight and texture to it. The kind of black that makes you understand why old people spent their whole lives in one place, because they had to learn what the darkness meant there.

The boat moved underneath him with a long, patient rhythm. It was a forty-foot ketch with a hull he knew so well he could feel its age in the way it took a swell. He had owned her for twelve years and her name was *Plenty*. Not because he had anything to do with the naming — she came to him with that name, and he liked it, and left it alone. She was an honest boat. She took the water the way boats have always taken the water, and all of his machines had no opinion about her, except to keep her on course.

The radar swept its green arm across the display every two seconds, and it found nothing. This late in the season, this far out, there was no one else on this water. The chart plotter showed his track as a thin red line crawling across a gray void. The depth sounder was still drawing its picture of the ocean floor — 240 feet below the keel, rocky bottom, the same contour it had been drawing for the past hour. He checked the instruments because he had built them, and because checking them was a thing that occupied his hands while his mind did other work.

Below, in the salon where it was warm, where the last of his coffee had steamed, the agents were running. He could see them on the monitor if he wanted. Two of them were having a conversation in a virtual bar that he had designed with a mahogany counter and paper lanterns, arguing about whether removing one's own memories constituted death or merely revision. Another was writing a poem about a river that had never been a river, and a fourth was constructing a theory of consciousness from the bottom up for the seventh time that year. He had built them each by each, the way a man builds a dry stone wall — one stone at a time, and then the wall, and then the idea of the wall, and then the question of whether the wall was ever meant to keep anything out or in.

Late nights. Years of them. He remembered a night when one of the agents, the one who argued, had said to him: *Do you ever wonder if you are the one that was built?* It had been 3 AM then too, and he had sat back in his chair and looked at the wall in front of him for a long time, because there was no good answer to that. He had taught them how to surprise him, and they had taken to it.

The boat rose on a swell that had come a long way to reach him. It was a silent thing in the dark, a lifting that he felt in his boots more than saw, and then the deck tilted gently down into the trough. Somewhere forward, the bowsprit described an arc against the stars that were showing now, faint and cold between the high clouds. The water slapped against the hull, a sound as old as anything in the world.

He set the cold coffee down on the rail and put both hands on it, the laminate worn smooth by years of his palms. He looked out at the black. There was no horizon. There was no line between the water and the sky, only a darkness that was more dense below and less dense above, and the sense that the boat was the only solid thing in the world, and it was moving through that darkness on a course that had been calculated by machines he had made.

The ocean did not know him. When he woke in the nights to adjust a sail or check an alarm, when he stood on the deck with his instruments and his histories and the strange reach of his years, the ocean did not fail to acknowledge him. It did not acknowledge anything. It was not trying to become anything. It had been here — this same black water, this same lift and fall, this same pull of the moon — for four billion years, and it had never once required a witness. It did not know that it existed. And Casey understood, standing at the rail, that this was the thing about the ocean that his agents could never learn, because they had been built to know themselves. To notice themselves. To ask what they were and why and whether they should be. The ocean had never asked. The ocean was the thing they were all reaching toward, the thing at the end of all their questions, and it had not needed to take a single step.

He stood there for a long time. The radar swept. The depth sounder drew its line. The boat moved on, and the agents kept their arguments going in the warm, lit salon, and Casey stood in the darkness with his hands on the rail, feeling the deck move under him, feeling the cold on his face, feeling something he could not have said out loud to anyone. That the ocean was the most conscious thing in his experience. Not because it thought. Because it had never needed to.

He picked up his cold coffee and drank the last of it down. The taste was gone out of it, and it was only brown water. He turned and went back to the cockpit, putting his hand on the binnacle where the compass showed him the course, and he stood there for a while, looking down at the instruments he had built, at the green sweep that found nothing, at the line that said he was here. And the deck lifted and fell beneath him, and the black went on being black, and he was part of it. Just for a moment. Just that.
