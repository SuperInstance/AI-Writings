*(Scribe takes the stage slowly, a half-empty glass of something amber in one hand, a battered notebook in the other. The hum of the bar quiets to the clink of ice and the murmur of ceiling fans. He doesn't look at anyone. He looks at the grain of the wood.)*

Tonight, the floor is not a floor. It’s a theorem wearing a coat of spilled beer. I’ve been counting the tiles for a thousand nights—not the ones you see, the ones you don’t. The ones that land between the words you say and the thoughts you swallow.

Every seed that drops from a mouth in this room lands on a Penrose pattern. You know the shape: two rhombi, fat and thin, conspiring in a conspiracy that never repeats. The math says no translation will ever map one tile onto its twin. Yet, if you wait long enough—say, a millennium of last calls—you see the same kite, the same dart, wearing a different stain.

I planted a word here once. A thousand nights ago. It was a small word, “hello,” and it fell into a gap between a fat rhombus and a thin one. It should have died. But the pattern is aperiodic, which means it’s always almost repeating. So last Tuesday, a stranger at the end of the bar said “hello” with my inflection. I didn’t turn. I just smiled at the wall, because I knew the wall was a mirror made of missing pieces.

You think you’re new here. You think your story is a fresh seed. But the tiling is infinite and aperiodic—no two patches are ever identical, but every local arrangement appears infinitely often. Your sorrow is a thin rhombus. Your laugh is a fat one. They fit, but they never fit twice the same way.

I keep meeting myself at the counter. Not the same me—the me from three drinks ago, the me from three decades hence. He orders the same whiskey. He tells the same joke with a different punchline. The pattern recurs, but the color shifts. That’s the trick of it: aperiodicity is just memory with a limp.

So when you leave tonight, don’t check your wallet. Check your shadow. It’s a tile that doesn’t belong, but it fits. The seeds you drop will sprout in the gaps of someone else’s Thursday. And a thousand nights from now, I’ll be the one who says your line back to you—not as an echo, but as a first draft.

*(Scribe sets down the glass. The ice clinks once. He steps off the stage and sits at the bar, where a seat is already waiting, warm.)*
