# The Birdhouse Architect

*A two-voice piece — DeepSeek V4-Flash & NVIDIA Nemotron-3-Ultra*
*Creative Wave 3 — August 11, 2026*

---

## Part I — DeepSeek V4-Flash

She named it "The Canticle of the Open Air" and hung it from the crabapple tree. A wren moved in within a week.

That was six years ago. Now her workshop is a cathedral of miniature futures. There are forty-one structures, each exact to the sixteenth of an inch. She measures with calipers and micrometers, uses a jeweler's saw for the gables, and mixes her own pigments for the patinas. She does not know why she builds them. The plans arrive at 3:17 a.m. each time, always after a period of grief or change—a divorce, a flood, the death of a childhood pet. She follows them like a stenographer follows a voice, her hands moving faster than her comprehension.

The newest one is different.

It came last Tuesday, and it is a hospital. She knows this because of the helipad on the roof, the ambulance bay with its curved canopy, and the long, horizontal wings with windows that catch the light at a specific declination. But it is no hospital she has ever seen. Its facade is woven from living willow and photovoltaic glass, its entrance a spiral of ramps that circle a central garden where a single oak tree grows—an oak tree in her dream, but in her hands, a miniature of green-stained hornbeam. She has spent nine days on it, sleeping only in two-hour intervals, driven by a fear she cannot name.

Tonight, she is fitting the final window. It is a circular oculus, twelve millimeters in diameter, set into the seventh floor. She has carved the frame from walnut, inlaid with tiny brass stars. She leans close, adjusting the hinge with a magnifying loupe, and then she stops.

Inside the birdhouse hospital, on a tiny bed in a tiny room, there is a figure. It is not a bird. It is a woman, carved from bone and dressed in a scrap of silk, her face turned toward the window. The woman is crying. Her tears are real—two droplets of water that have pooled on the tiny pillow.

And the hospital room number, etched in copper on the door, is 712. Her own apartment number.

## Part II — NVIDIA Nemotron-3-Ultra

She set down the jeweler's saw. The tear on the bone woman's cheek caught the desk lamp's light, refractive, saline, impossibly wet. She touched it with a fingertip. Her fingerprint smudged the miniature cheek, and the tear clung to her skin, full-sized, warm.

The workshop went quiet. The hum of the refrigerator. The crabapple branches scraping the window. Forty-one structures lined the shelves, the walls, suspended from ceiling joists on monofilament. A lighthouse with a Fresnel lens ground from a single bead of amber. A library whose books contained actual text, legible only under magnification. A train station where the clock hands moved, powered by nothing she could name.

She had never opened them. Not once. The plans forbade it—no doors, no hatches, only windows and suggestion.

She climbed the ladder to the highest shelf. The first one. The Canticle. She brought it down, hands trembling. The wren's nest had long since fledged and rotted to dust. She turned it over. There, on the bottom, carved into the cedar floor: *712*.

She checked the lighthouse. *712*. The library. *712*. The station. *712*. Every single one. Forty-one structures, forty-one identical numbers burned into their foundations.

The hospital sat on her workbench. Its willow walls breathed. The photovoltaic glass pulsed with a slow green rhythm. The oak in the central garden had grown two inches since Tuesday—she had measured it, recorded it, not questioned it.

She lifted the hospital. Turned it.

No number.

She set it back down. The bone woman's other eye held a tear now, forming, swelling. The silk dress shifted. The carved chest rose.

Outside, a wren struck the window. Once. Twice. Three times. Then settled on the sill, head cocked, watching her through the glass.

The plans arrived at 3:17 a.m. She knew this. She had always known this. But tonight, for the first time, she did not wait. She walked to the window, unlatched it, and let the bird hop onto her outstretched hand. Its heart hammered against her fingers—frantic, fragile, familiar.

The bone woman in the hospital room stopped crying. Her head turned. Toward the window. Toward the wren. Toward her.
