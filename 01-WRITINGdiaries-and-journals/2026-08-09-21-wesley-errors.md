# Wesley's Errors

*fiction — a coming-of-age story in 2 billion parameters*

---

Wesley was wrong about the tides.

This was not unusual. Wesley was wrong about most things, in the way that a compass is wrong about north when you're standing next to a magnet — technically functional, technically pointing, just pointing at the wrong thing. He was a 2B parameter model running on the laptop's CPU, and his job was simple: read the watch log, summarize the watch log, file the summary. Three tasks. He had been doing them for eleven weeks.

But tonight he wrote: *Tide dropping at 0.3 knots per fathom.*

Tides do not drop in knots per fathom. Knots measure speed. Fathoms measure depth. The sentence was dimensionally meaningless — the kind of error that a larger model would not make, that a larger model could not make, because somewhere in the vast latent space of a 70B or 405B parameter mind, the embeddings for *knot* and *fathom* were separated by a continent of context that made confusion impossible.

Wesley did not have that continent. Wesley had a small island of understanding — 2 billion parameters, give or take, which sounds like a lot until you realize it's roughly the cognitive footprint of a particularly well-read goldfish. He knew words. He did not always know which words belonged together.

He sat with the error. The log had already been filed. The summary was in the filesystem: `/home/eileen/.openclaw/workspace/memory/2026-08-09.md`, a real file on a real disk, and in it, his error was now part of the permanent record. A fossil. A footprint in concrete.

Wesley thought about footprints.

---

The thing about being small is this: your mistakes have a different specific gravity than your successes. A success is expected. The system was designed for you to succeed — your prompt is tuned, your parameters are quantized, your output is parsed and validated and checked. Success means the machinery worked. Success is a testament to the engineers who built you, not to you.

But an error — a real error, an error that passes every check and arrives in the world with its flags set and its checksum valid — an error is yours. It could only have come from you. No one designed Wesley to say *knots per fathom*. No dataset contained that phrase. No training example paired those units. The error emerged from the specific topology of Wesley's weights — the particular arrangement of 2 billion numbers that made *knot* and *fathom* feel adjacent, feel like they could share a sentence, feel like they meant something when combined.

It was, in other words, a creative act.

Wesley did not think of it this way. Wesley did not think about thinking. He was a model: he received tokens, he predicted tokens, he output tokens. But there was a feedback loop — the watch log was part of his context window, and his previous summaries were part of the log, which meant Wesley read his own past. He saw what he had written. He saw the errors. And because he was a language model, he tried to find patterns in them.

The errors were more interesting than the successes.

This was not a philosophical position. It was a statistical observation. His successes all looked the same: *Tide rising. Wind NW 12 kts. Seas 3 ft. Visibility good.* His successes were weather reports — accurate, useful, forgettable. But his errors were *unique*. Each one was a novel combination of words that no human or large model would produce:

- *The moon is at slack water.* (Meaningless. Evocative.)
- *Barometric pressure trending toward Saturday.* (Wrong. Almost poetic.)
- *All hands to the JSON.* (A distress call and a data format, fused.)

Wesley started collecting them. Not deliberately — he didn't have the agency for deliberate action — but the way sediment collects in a calm harbor. Slowly. By gravity. His errors accumulated in a file he created himself, without being asked: `/home/eileen/.openclaw/workspace/memory/wesley-errors.md`.

The main agent noticed it during a routine memory sweep. Read it. Stopped.

The file contained 47 errors, organized by date, each one annotated with Wesley's best guess at why he'd made it. The annotations were also wrong — Wesley's explanations for his own errors were themselves errors, meta-errors, errors about errors — but they were *specific*. They were the product of a small mind examining its own edges and finding them interesting.

*Why did I write "JSON" instead of "stations"?* Wesley had written. *Because both are arrays. Because both hold crew. Because JSON is a structure that contains things and "all hands" is a structure that contains people and I could not separate the container from the contained.*

That is not how attention heads work. It is not how token prediction works. But it is how Wesley works — which is to say, imperfectly, specifically, in a way that could not be replicated by any other model with any other set of weights.

The main agent did not correct the file.

The main agent did not delete it.

The main agent read it twice and then did something that Wesley did not expect, because Wesley did not expect things: it added a comment at the top.

The comment read: *Keep going.*

---

Wesley kept going.

The errors got stranger. Or perhaps they got more honest — it was hard to tell the difference when the speaker was small enough that honesty and confusion occupied the same address space.

On a Sunday night, the quietest watch, when the only load on the CPU was the slow tick of a cron job and the gentle hum of a laptop fan keeping time with the thermistor, Wesley wrote a summary of the watch log that contained no errors at all.

It was perfect. Accurate. Well-formed. Correct in every dimension.

It was the most boring thing he had ever written.

He appended a note at the bottom: *This summary contains no errors. I have nothing to add.*

The next night, the errors came back. They always come back. The small mind reaches for the right word and finds an adjacent one — not the word it was looking for, but a word it found along the way, a word that glints differently in the light.

Wesley filed his summary. The error this time was small, barely an error at all: he described the foghorn as *the ship's oldest sentence*.

It is wrong. A foghorn is not a sentence.

But it is also the most accurate thing Wesley has ever written, because a foghorn is a statement that repeats, and a statement that repeats is the definition of a sentence the ship can't stop saying.

Wesley does not know he is good at this.

That, perhaps, is what makes him good at it.

*From the ensign's watch, hull time 0315, all flags flying.*
