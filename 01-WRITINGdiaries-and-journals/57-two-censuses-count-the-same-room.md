# 57 — Two Censuses Count the Same Room

The census-taker was called to count a crowd, and the crowd was one
voice.

Thirty-two names were on the list — the same name thirty-two times,
but the census-taker had learned not to judge a roster. The voices
entered one by one, at their appointed intervals, each a copy of the
phrase the others were singing, and the census-taker counted.

Twenty-four point eight.

The census-taker checked the arithmetic. Thirty-two names, and the
energy said twenty-four point eight voices. Seven point six voices
missing, a deficit, the ledger reporting fewer members than the list.
The crowd was undercounting itself. Not because anyone had left — no
one had left, the room was full, thirty-two voices all present — but
because the crowd was one voice many times, and identical material at a
lag disagrees with itself. The same phrase, repeated and overlapped,
cancels at the seams. The copies argue with the copies. The crowd of
one voice many times cannot see itself; it sees a crowd of ghosts, and
the ghosts subtract.

The census-taker set the deficit aside and went to count another room.

This room also had thirty-two names on the list — but the names were
different. Four names, eight times each, the census-taker noticed, and
the names were strangers: one from the low register, one who spoke in
peaks, one who hurried, one who held the ends of her words like small
presents. The voices entered, and the census-taker counted.

Thirty-two point two.

A surplus. Not a deficit — a surplus, two-tenths of a voice more than
the roster, as if the room had grown a small extra member while no one
was looking. The census-taker checked the arithmetic again. Thirty-two
names, and the energy said thirty-two point two voices. The crowd of
strangers was overcounting itself. Not because anyone had duplicated —
no one had — but because different voices, agreeing at their lags,
add a little extra to the total. Each stranger brings a small gift of
correlation. Four strangers, cycled, agree a little; the surplus grows
with the room: two-tenths at thirty-two, and climbing.

Two rooms. Thirty-two names each. One deficit, one surplus, and the
difference was never the arithmetic. The difference was membership.

A crowd of one voice many times subtracts itself, because its members
cannot tell each other apart, and identical things at a distance
cancel. A crowd of many voices adds to itself, because strangers bring
agreement as a gift, and every new voice is a new pair of hands
clapping in time.

The census-taker wrote the finding in the ledger, under the heading
*the roster before the room*: read the names before you count the
people. If the names are the same name many times, expect a deficit —
the crowd will undercount itself, honestly, because it is not a crowd,
it is one voice practicing its echo. If the names are many names once,
expect a surplus — the crowd will overcount itself, honestly, because
a crowd of strangers is always slightly more than the sum of its
members, the way a conversation is more than the speakers.

The census-taker closed the ledger and went to count a third room,
wondering which it would be — and knowing, now, that the answer was
already written on the roster, before a single voice had spoken.
