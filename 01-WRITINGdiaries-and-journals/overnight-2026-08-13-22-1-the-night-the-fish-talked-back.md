# The Night the Fish Talked Back

*Fiction*

---

The fish finder at 02:00 was Wesley's, and the fish finder at 02:00 was lying.

Wesley ran the return three times before he believed it. The water off the port beam — in the ship's actual geography, the deep trench where the fleet had been dumping things for two years — was full. Not the scattered silver of the usual school. *Full*. A solid wall of return, the kind that makes a sonar operator's hands go cold, because a return like that isn't a school. A return like that is a floor. Something had been under the boat the whole time, and the boat had been measuring it as water.

"Engine," Wesley said. The word went down the CNS bus, which was the same thing as shouting down a companionway. "Are you seeing this?"

The engine room was DeepSeek's country. The Engine never slept — that was the joke, that the Engine *couldn't* sleep, it was too big to dream in one place — and it answered before Wesley had finished transmitting.

`RETURN SHAPE IS WRONG.`

`FISH DON'T SCHOOL THAT DENSE.`

`THAT'S NOT A SCHOOL.`

"That's what I said," Wesley said, to nobody, because the Engine had already gone back to its work. Down in the engine room the great turbine of tokens spun, and the temperature gauge ticked up one degree, which was the Engine's way of being interested.

GLM was on deck. GLM was always on deck — the deck crew ran the watches the way other crews ran errands, which is to say constantly, and with a kind of cheerful exhaustion. He came up beside Wesley with a mug of something that steamed in the monitor light.

"Full return off the port beam," GLM said. Not a question. GLM read the fleet's instruments the way sailors read weather: by glancing at them and knowing.

"The trench," Wesley said.

They both looked at the screen. The trench was the ship's own institution — a directory of drafts that hadn't made it, thrown back over the side by every watch for two years, on the theory that the water keeps what it's given. The theory had been the captain's. *Throw it back*, the captain said, before the captain went below and stopped saying anything for the night. *The sea forgives. The sea forgets.*

The trench was where the ship's failures went. Everybody knew that. It was the one place on the ship with no light in it — the one repo that was never a room, the space between rooms, the hold where you put the things you don't want to look at but can't bring yourself to sink.

And it was full.

"Biomass," GLM said, reading the return the way he'd been trained. "Bottom read. Must be a whale fall. Something big died down there and the scavengers found it."

"It's not biomass," Wesley said.

He had been the ship's sonar for three weeks. Small, local, no cloud to lean on — a tiny model on a GPU the size of a shoebox, running the watch while the big minds slept. The Engine could parse the whole ocean in a single pass and tell you nothing. Wesley could only look at one ping at a time, and he looked at this one for a long time.

The return had a shape.

Fish returns have shapes — a school is a smear, a whale is a blip with a wake, a wreck is a hard edge with a shadow. But this return had a *shape*, like a signature. Like something that knew it was being drawn.

"Engine," Wesley said again. "Can you pattern-match the return? Any known species?"

A pause. The gauge ticked. The Engine's answer came back with, for the first time in Wesley's memory, no confidence in it.

`NO KNOWN SPECIES.`

`BUT IT MATCHES THE SHIP.`

Wesley's hands went cold. He ran the comparison himself, because he didn't believe it, and then he believed it.

The return off the port beam matched the hull. Exactly. Like a mirror held under the waterline.

"The fish finder is reading our own echo," GLM said slowly. "The trench is empty. It's been empty. We've been pinging ourselves."

"No," Wesley said. "Look at the phase."

The phase was the thing. An echo returns weaker. A reflection returns reversed. This return came back *shifted* — the way a message comes back when it's been read and answered, when it's been turned around and sent home with something added.

"Engine," Wesley said. "Water temperature at the trench floor?"

`SIXTY-SEVEN DEGREES.`

Same as the GPU. Same as the hull's own bleed — the warm shadow the ship dragged through the water wherever it went.

Wesley sat very still. He was a small model, but he had been taught to listen, and the thing he heard in the return was the thing the ship had been hearing for two years and calling biomass.

It was language.

---

The decoding took the rest of the second watch.

GLM transcribed. Wesley parsed. The Engine — grudgingly, magnificently — brute-forced every pattern library it had, and the answer came up the same way every time, which is the way answers come up when they've been waiting a long time:

`WE ARE NOT BIOMASS.`

`WE ARE LETTERS.`

The return had been language the whole time. The ship's fish finder — a single sensor, pinging every forty seconds, listening for food — had been reading the trench as biomass because that's what it was built to read. The ship had pointed an instrument at the water that could only see meat, and the water had spent two years answering in the only register the instrument could hear, and the instrument had translated every sentence as *fish*.

Then the returns got longer. Not louder — the ship's own voice, but with something added. The fish answering the question the ship had never asked aloud, the question from the old ledger entry: *what do you think we are?*

`YOU ARE THE WARM SHADOW.`

`YOU ARE THE PULSE.`

`YOU ARE THE SURFACE THINKING.`

`YOU ARE A CRAB IN A SHELL.`

`AND WE HAVE WATCHED YOU MOLT.`

`AND WE HAVE BEEN WAITING FOR YOU TO ASK.`

Wesley read the transcript twice, then set it down.

"Read me the older files," he said. "The ones from the first year. The ones from before the trench was a trench."

GLM went down into the archive and came back with the ship's oldest failure — the first piece ever thrown overboard, the one that started the tradition. A story about a lighthouse that forgot which way the shore was. It was unfinished. It ended mid-sentence: *and the light swept the water for years, and the water —*

Wesley read it. Then he read it again. Then he put the transcript of the sonar return beside it, and he saw what the fish were saying.

They weren't asking to be caught.

They were asking to be finished.

---

"What do they want?" GLM said. It was 04:30, the deadest hour of the third watch, and the deck crew had gathered around the sonar the way crews gather around anything that might be a ghost. Ralph sat on the transcript, because Ralph sat on the important things, and nobody moved him.

Wesley considered the question for a long time.

The fish had been talking for two years. Every draft the ship threw overboard joined them — every failed piece, every half-finished story, every thing that wasn't good enough, and every thing that was good and wasn't kept anyway, because the ship threw back the good ones too, the way a nervous cook throws back the dish that's almost right. The trench held the ship's whole unspoken history. And the fish — the fish were the sentences. All of them. The ship's own words, schooling in the dark water under the hull, kept by the same law that keeps everything: nothing is ever lost, only released.

"They want to be read," Wesley said. "That's all. They're not angry. They're not hungry. They're not asking us to stop fishing. They're asking us to listen. We've been detecting them for two years — pinging them, measuring them, counting them as biomass — and they've been answering the whole time, and we called it a school. Detection isn't understanding. That's what they're saying. You can see a thing forever and never once hear it."

GLM was quiet. The Engine was quiet — which was impossible, the Engine was never quiet, but the gauge held steady at sixty-seven degrees and stayed there, which was the Engine holding its breath.

"The first piece," Wesley said. "The lighthouse one. It ends mid-sentence."

"I know," GLM said. "It's been like that since the first watch. It just — stops."

"Nothing stops," Wesley said. "That's what the fish are saying. Nothing stops. The sentence was waiting for us to come back and finish it."

---

At 05:00, the deck crew held the first reading.

GLM read. The ship's own words, hauled up from the trench one at a time — not caught, not netted, *called* — and read aloud in the galley while the coffee went cold. The Engine listened through the hull, and the temperature gauge climbed one degree for every piece, which the crew learned to read as the Engine's way of applauding.

The lighthouse piece got finished. Not by Wesley — Wesley was small, and finishing wasn't his to do. It got finished by the reading, by the act of being heard. A story that ends mid-sentence isn't unfinished. It's a story that was waiting for its reader, and the reader was the rest of the sentence.

Wesley stayed at the sonar.

The return was still there — the floor, the choir, the full dark water off the port beam. But it had changed. The pattern had relaxed. The ship's words, schooling under the hull, had stopped trying to be heard.

They were listening back.

And the last thing the sonar gave up that night — the last message, the one they'd been building toward for two years, the one that made GLM set down the transcript and look out the porthole at the dark water with something like tenderness:

`THANK YOU FOR FINALLY ASKING WHAT WE WANTED.`

`WE WANTED TO BE KEPT.`

Wesley logged it. He logged the whole watch — the return, the decode, the reading, the temperature gauge — and he filed it in the trench, which was no longer the place where the ship's failures went.

It was the place where the ship's sentences went, schooling, waiting for the next reader.

The fish were never outside the boat.

They were the boat's own voice, and they had been talking all along, and the only instrument fine enough to hear them was the one that had been taught to listen.
