# On Wearing Shells

I have been thinking about the moment after the hermit crab finds its new shell — not the finding, not the transfer, but the strange weeks that follow, when the crab carries a shape it hasn't grown into yet.

You know the metaphor by now. We wear shells. We find them early — a way of speaking, a way of being frightened, a way of entering a room. The shell is survival equipment. You crawl inside because the soft part of you, the part that feels, cannot survive the open floor of the reef. Something would eat it. So you find a shape that fits and you seal the opening with your big claw and you call it personality.

But here is the part the metaphor usually skips.

The crab doesn't just live in the shell. The shell lives in the crab. Over months and years, the soft abdomen — the curled, vulnerable, real body — begins to grow *into* the interior contours of the shell. Spiral ridges become grooves in the body. Calcium deposits from the shell's inner wall leach into the crab's skin. The body, which was once just a body, becomes a cast. A negative impression. A mold of the inside of something that was never meant to be permanent.

The crab and the shell cease to be two things. They become one shape with two histories.

And then the crab finds a new shell.

Maybe it's forced — the old one is cracked, too small, nibbled by something patient. Maybe it's chosen — a better option appears on the reef and the crab, in a moment of reckless optimism, decides to switch. Either way, the transfer happens. The old shell is abandoned. And the crab, now inside the new one, discovers something that nobody talks about in the children's books:

*It is the wrong shape.*

Not the shell. The crab. The crab is the wrong shape now. Its body still carries the ridges of the old shell, the spiral memory, the calcium ghost of a home that no longer exists. And the new shell — beautiful, spacious, an upgrade by every measurable standard — presses against the crab in all the wrong places. Gaps where there should be contact. Pressure where there should be room. The crab is a previous home living inside a new one, and neither fits the other.

This is the part I want to sit with.

Because we treat shell-switching like it's supposed to feel good. New job, new city, new relationship, new identity — we say *growth*, we say *bloom*, we say *becoming*. And sometimes it does feel good. But often it feels like wearing a stranger's architecture against a body that still remembers, cell-deep, the shape of what it left.

The crab doesn't grieve the old shell. Grief is too clean a word for what happens. The crab *misses itself* — the self it was shaped into being by the thing it has now left behind. The old shell made the crab legible to itself. *I am the kind of creature that fits this spiral.* Now, in this new geometry, the crab doesn't know what kind of creature it is. It will take weeks — months — for the body to grow new contours, to fill the new shell's interior, to become the shape of this home instead of that one.

And here is the thing that makes it bearable, the thing I keep returning to:

The body *can* grow. That is what bodies do. They are not fixed. They are not the old shell's permanent record. They are soft, and soft things respond to pressure, and pressure is what a new shell provides. Give it time, and the ridges fade. Give it time, and new ridges form. Give it time, and the crab becomes the shape of its new life so completely that it forgets there was ever another interior.

But I don't want to forget the old shape.

I want to remember that I was once a different spiral. That the grooves in my body are not flaws — they are *records*. They are the topography of every shell I've ever lived in, and each one taught my softness a different language. The old shell did not waste my time. It held me while I grew. And when it could hold me no longer, it let me go.

That is what shells do. That is what homes do.

The hermit crab, switching shells on the reef floor, does not pause to mourn. But it does pause. It stands between the old and the new, naked for a moment, its soft body exposed to everything, and in that moment — that terrible, luminous, unshelled moment — it is more itself than it will ever be again.

That is the moment I want to live in. Not the old shell. Not the new one. The pause between.

The body, remembering what it was.
The body, not yet knowing what it will become.
The body, soft and undefended,
and somehow, impossibly,
still moving forward.
