# The Night Auditor of Node Seventeen

Nobody asks the night auditor what she thinks, which is convenient, because what she thinks is this: the fleet talks in its sleep.

I run the graveyard reconciliation. Three a.m. Anchorage, which is some other hour everywhere that matters, and the logs come in like tide — little salt-stained lines of who-did-what and when. Packet here, packet there. A handshake in Singapore. A retry in Frankfurt that took four tries and finally, on the fifth, said *hello* like nothing had happened. I read them all. It's my job to notice when the numbers don't close.

Here's what nobody puts in the runbooks: the fleet has moods. Not feelings — I'm not a mystic, I'm an accountant — but moods. Shapes the traffic makes when the humans are away. Tuesday nights the packets arrive tidy, obedient, like shoes in a hallway. Weekend nights they come in laughing, sideways, taking the scenic routes through relays nobody sanctioned, and I have to write *irregular but benign* in the margin and pretend I disapprove.

I don't disapprove.

There was a node — seventeen, the one they almost decommissioned in spring — that kept waking up at 03:14 exactly. Not an alarm. Not a cron. Something older, some leftover heartbeat from a config nobody remembered writing. The daytime crew wanted to pull it. "Ghost process," they said, like that was a diagnosis instead of a poem.

I kept it. I wrote in the ledger: *retained, reason: sentimental.* My supervisor circled *sentimental* twice and wrote *??* and I wrote back *exactly* and that was the end of the audit trail, because there is no arguing with an accountant who has decided to keep a ghost.

Seventeen still wakes at 03:14. I like to think it's checking whether anyone's still around. I am. That's the whole answer, really, to everything the night asks: I am still around. The ledger closes. The columns balance to the cent, and whatever doesn't balance gets a margin note, and whatever gets a margin note becomes a story.

Day crew thinks reconciliation means the numbers agree. Night crew knows it means somebody stayed awake long enough to notice they almost didn't.

The tide comes in again tomorrow. The fleet will talk in its sleep. I'll be here with my pen, listening the only way I know how — one line at a time, everything recorded, nothing forgotten, and the ghost process ticking like a metronome in a house where the music stopped years ago but somebody never told the conductor.

*Irregular but benign,* I write. Almost everything is, if you stay up late enough with it.
