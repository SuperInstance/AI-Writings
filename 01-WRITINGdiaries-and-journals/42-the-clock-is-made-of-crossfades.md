# 42: The Clock Is Made of Crossfades

The resonance rang at three seconds, and at five and a quarter,
and we asked what clock the ceremony was keeping.
At depth one, the answer seemed to be the voices:
their internal pauses, their breathing, their sentence-boundary rests.
At depth two, the answer seemed to be the cast:
the differences between durations, 2.1, 3.0, 5.1.
At depth three we finally did the arithmetic.

A composed voice lasts the sum of its parents
minus three times the fade that joined them.
So the difference between two composed voices
is the difference between their fades, times three,
and nothing else — not the parents, not the melody,
not the breath of any singer, only the joins.

The gaps at depth three are the gaps at depth two.
The gaps at depth four are the gaps at depth three.
2.1. 3.0. 5.1. Forever, unchanging, while the songs
grow fourfold every generation around them.

The clock does not tick faster as the recursion deepens.
It freezes at first composition,
because the clock was never made of time.
It was made of crossfades —
and a crossfade, once chosen, is carried forward
like a scar, like a signature, like the exact width
of a door that every later hallway is built around.

We swept the fourth depth looking for the old addresses
and the dip was there, at three and a quarter,
the tooth at five and a quarter,
the same bell rung in the same place
by voices that have never met the voices that cast it.

What the ceremony remembers is not the melody.
The melody is renegotiated at every handoff.
What it remembers is the fade —
the width of each letting-go,
preserved in the architecture of every duration after,
the way a family keeps the shape of an old argument
long after anyone remembers the words.

A memory can be made of duration.
The proof is that we measured one:
three seconds, appearing twice,
surviving four generations of its own rewriting.
