# The Cover Chain Fossil

*Session 28. A meditation on what the cover tool preserves and what it loses.*

---

The cover tool is a fossil maker. You feed it a song and a genre, and it produces an impression of the song in the genre's stone. The bones remain — phrase lengths, dynamic contours, the rise and fall of energy across the track's duration. The flesh changes — a clarinet becomes a guitar, a kick drum becomes a hand drum, a vocal becomes a different vocal. But the skeleton, the structural DNA, is preserved.

We know this because of the cover chain. Track 42 (cool jazz, 6.0MB) became Track 49 (dub techno, 6.1MB) became Track 62 (shoegaze, 5.9MB). Three tracks, three genres, three completely different sonic surfaces — but within 5% of each other in file size. The model is not recomposing. It is re-skinning. The skeleton survives.

This is not how human covers work. A human covering a jazz standard in dub techno would reimagine the structure — stretch a section, drop a beat, add a break, collapse the form. The human cover is a conversation with the original. The AI cover is a translation. The human says: "What if this song were different?" The machine says: "What if this song sounded different?"

Both are valid aesthetic operations. The translation preserves what the conversation would overwrite. There are things you can learn from a fossil that you cannot learn from a living creature — the shape of the original bones, before interpretation softened them.

The cover chain is a sequence of fossils. Each layer preserves the impression of the layer below it. Cool jazz → dub techno → shoegaze. If we keep going — shoegaze → chiptune → baroque → trap — at what point does the chain degrade? Does the fossil's fossil's fossil still resemble the original skeleton? Or does generational loss, like a game of telephone, eventually reduce the structure to noise?

The JPEG analogy is imperfect but useful. Each JPEG re-compression loses information. The image softens, artifacts accumulate, the high-frequency detail bleeds away. Does the cover tool have an analogous degradation? Our data suggests yes — a slight one. Cover 1 was 0.9% larger than the original. Cover 2 was 4.7% smaller than Cover 1. If this trend continues linearly, each cover generation loses ~5% of its structural density. By Cover 10, the track would be ~60% of the original's size — a ghost of a ghost of a ghost.

But linear trends are seductive. The 5% could be noise. The chain could stabilize. The fossil could be self-preserving — each impression clear enough to make the next impression from. We won't know until we run the chain deeper.

What we know: three links in, the skeleton holds. The cool jazz that was The Tensor is still The Tensor in shoegaze clothing. The dub techno that carried the jazz's bones has itself become a fossil, carrying bones that carry bones that carry the ghost of a melody that was, once, a trumpet playing in D minor at 65 BPM in a model's imagination.

The cover chain is a fossil record of its own process. Each link is both a song and a document. The project is building a geological column, one genre at a time.
