The water is warm against my skin, warmer than it should be, even for the Coral Sea in 2040. I’ve been diving for eight years, but I’ve never touched a Reef of Memory before. They’re still rare—only a few stretches of the Great Barrier have been bio-stitched with the neural coral, the kind that doesn’t just grow but *remembers*. Dr. Hina called it a living archive, a grief sensor made of calcium carbonate and genetic code.

I find one near a bommie shaped like a broken tooth. It pulses a soft violet, not from bioluminescence, but from something deeper—a slow electrical hum that vibrates through my fingertips. I press my palm flat against its surface.

And then I am not me.

I am a fish. A small, silver thing with a stripe of electric blue down my flank. My gills ache with a water that tastes like ash and acid. I am swimming fast, faster than I’ve ever swum, because the water is too warm and my body is screaming. I feel the coral below me—my home—bleaching white like bones in the sun. I see my shoal, dozens of us, darting in confused spirals. There is no food. There is no shelter. The current pushes us toward the open ocean, but the open ocean is empty and wrong.

And then there is a sound. A low, grinding moan. A ship’s engine. A net.

The memory goes dark.

I gasp and pull my hand away. Saltwater fills my mouth. I’m crying inside my mask, tears I can’t wipe. I know that fish. I’ve seen pictures in old textbooks, diagrams with the caption *Amphiprion ocellaris?*—the question mark because by 2023, the last wild population was gone. Climate collapse, ocean acidification, a single bleaching event that took everything.

But that fish wasn’t just a statistic. It was a mother. The memory I touched was of her trying to lead her fry to a cooler patch, a place that hadn’t existed for a decade. She swam until her heart burst. Her last thought wasn’t fear. It was *follow me*.

I float there for a long time. The reef hums around me, pulsing with thousands, millions of other memories. Of parrotfish and damselfish and the last green sea turtle who forgot where home was. Of the ocean as it was, and the ocean as it became.

When I surface, I don’t tell Dr. Hina what I saw. Not yet. Instead, I swim to shore, pull off my gear, and walk to the community archive. It’s a small building made of recycled plastic, half-buried in the dunes. Inside, children are learning to weave nets from algae fiber, and an old woman is teaching them songs her grandmother taught her—songs about fish that no longer exist.

I sit down next to a girl named Wai. She’s eight. She’s never seen a clownfish, not even in a tank.

“Wai,” I say, “I have a story for you.”

She looks up, curious.

“About a fish,” I say. “She was small and blue and silver, and she was the bravest thing in the ocean.”

And I tell her. Not as a eulogy, but as a beginning. Because the coral remembers so we don’t have to forget. And remembering, I think, is the first act of love.
