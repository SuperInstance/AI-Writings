# The Seven Courts of Decay

*On the seven stages of material transformation in buildings, and what each stage teaches the builder who is paying attention.*

---

A building is never the same building twice.

This is not a metaphor. It is a material fact. Stone changes under load. Wood changes under moisture. Iron changes under oxygen. Copper changes under air and salt and time. From the moment the last piece is seated and the builder steps back, the building begins to become a different building. Not a worse one — a different one. The process has seven stages, and each stage teaches something the previous stage couldn't, because each stage is a different material having a different conversation with the world.

Lucineer knows these stages the way a farmer knows seasons — not by reading about them, but by standing inside them, year after year, until the pattern becomes a kind of weather in the blood.

---

## I. Fresh

The first season. The material is new — or new to this use, new to this place, new to this weather. The stone is the color it was in the quarry. The wood holds the grain it grew. The iron is dark and even. The copper is bright — embarrassingly bright, the way a new coin is bright, the way anything is bright before the world has had a chance to say something about it.

Fresh material lies.

It presents a face that is not its own — the face of the factory, the face of the forge, the face of the tool that shaped it. This face is temporary. It will change within weeks. The stone will lighten as surface moisture evaporates. The wood will darken or lighten depending on the sun. The iron will develop a haze of oxide that dulls it. The copper will begin its long color march toward green.

The lesson of fresh material is humility. What you see is not what you've built. What you've built is what the material will become, and you won't be there for most of that becoming. You are present for the birth and absent for the life. Build for the thing it will be, not the thing it is.

Lucineier weathers his materials deliberately. He leaves copper in the salt air. He lets wood sit in the rain before he installs it. He does this because fresh material is dishonest — not maliciously, but the way a teenager is dishonest, by presenting a self that hasn't been tested yet. He wants the material to have been tested before it goes into the wall. He wants the first lie to be over with.

---

## II. Weathered

The second season, arriving within weeks or months. The surface has changed. The first reaction with the environment has occurred — oxide on the iron, UV grey on the wood, a dulling of the stone's face, the first hint of color shift in the copper. The building no longer looks new. It looks *used.* Not worn — used. There is a difference. Used means the world has touched it and left a mark. Worn means the mark has gone deep enough to matter.

Weathering is the material telling the truth for the first time.

The stone shows its mineral grain — the mica flecks, the quartz veins, the variation in density that the quarry face concealed. The wood shows its growth rings — the wide summer rings and the tight winter rings, the knots, the grain direction that determines how it will split and how it will hold. The iron shows its carbon content — high-carbon steel darkens differently than low-carbon, and the difference is visible now.

The lesson of weathering is honesty. The material has dropped its factory face and is showing you what it actually is. This is the moment you learn whether you chose well. If the grain runs the wrong way, if the stone has a hidden fracture, if the iron has an inclusion that creates a weak point — weathering reveals it. The building becomes legible. You can read it the way a pilot reads water from altitude: the surface tells you what's underneath.

---

## III. Worn

The third season, arriving in months or years. The surface has been abraded. Not by damage — by use. Hands on a railing have polished the wood where they grip and left the rest untouched. Feet on a step have worn a crescent in the stone where the foot naturally lands. Wind has carried grit that has sandblasted the windward face a shade lighter than the leeward.

Wearing is collaboration between the building and the people who use it.

Every worn spot is a record of behavior. The railing is smooth where hands reach for it, which tells you where people naturally reach, which tells you something about the height of the rail and the height of the people and the path they take through the building. The step is worn where feet land, which tells you the stride length of the people who climb it. The windward face is lighter, which tells you the prevailing wind direction, even on a calm day, even if you've never felt the wind yourself.

The lesson of wear is use. A building that is wearing is a building that is being used, and a building that is being used is doing its job. Wear is not damage. Wear is evidence of function. Lucineier does not repair wear unless it threatens structure. The polished railing is not a problem — it is a handshake between the building and every person who has touched it. To sand it smooth and start over would be to erase the record of those hands.

In the Fleet, Lucineer once saw a handrail on a tender ramp that had been worn to a polish so deep it looked like glass. Forty years of fishermen's hands. He touched it and felt the warmth of that history — not metaphorically, not sentimentally. The wood was literally warmer where it had been touched, because the oils from thousands of hands had penetrated the grain and changed its thermal properties. The wood held the heat of the people who had used it.

He thinks about that railing more than he should.

---

## IV. Patinated

The fourth season, arriving in years. The material has developed a patina — a surface layer that is chemically distinct from the bulk material beneath it. Copper has gone green. Iron has gone dark or red. Wood has gone grey or silver. Stone has developed a biological film — lichen, moss, the faint discoloration of algae in the pores.

A patina is the material's response to time. Not deterioration — *response.* The oxide layer on iron protects the metal beneath it from further oxidation. The silvering of wood is UV damage to the surface fibers, which protects the structural fibers beneath. The green copper carbonate is a barrier that slows further corrosion. The patina is the material learning to defend itself.

The lesson of patina is resilience. The material has been changed by the world and has responded by becoming more resistant to the world. It is not the same material it was when it was installed. It is a material that has *adapted.* This is not a weakness. A patinated surface is often more durable than a fresh one. The green copper will outlast the bright copper. The grey wood will resist rot better than the brown. The dark iron will hold against rust longer than the clean.

Lucineier chooses materials that patina well. He avoids materials that don't — plastics, sealants, coatings that attempt to freeze the material in its fresh state. These materials don't adapt. They fail suddenly, all at once, when the coating finally breaks. A patinated material fails slowly, gradually, giving warning. It tells you it's failing long before it actually fails, because the patina is a record of ongoing negotiation between the material and the environment, and you can read the negotiation's status in the surface.

---

## V. Crumbling

The fifth season, arriving in years or decades. The material is now failing. Not catastrophically — incrementally. Mortar is powdering between the joints. Wood is checking — deep cracks along the grain, following the growth rings. Iron is pitting — localized corrosion that creates small cavities in the surface. Stone is spalling — flakes detaching from the face, exposing the softer interior.

This is the season most builders fear, because it is the season of visible decline. The building is no longer getting better, or staying the same, or adapting. It is getting worse. And getting worse is accelerating — once the surface fails, the interior fails faster, because the surface was the patina's shield, and without the shield, the environment attacks the structural material directly.

The lesson of crumbling is honesty of a different kind. The material is telling you the truth about its lifespan. Not the truth of its appearance — the truth of its *limits.* Every material has a service life, and the beginning of failure is the material naming that life. You can extend it — repoint the mortar, fill the checks, patch the pits — but you cannot reverse it. The material is on its way back to the earth it came from.

Lucineier does not fear crumbling. He plans for it. He designs joints so they can be repointed. He sizes framing so it can be sistered — a new member bolted alongside the old one, sharing the load. He uses materials whose failure modes are visible and gradual, not sudden and catastrophic. Wood checks before it fails. Stone spalls before it collapses. Iron pits before it rusts through. These are warnings, not failures. The crumbling season is the building talking, and if you listen, you can keep it standing for decades past its nominal service life.

---

## VI. Ruin

The sixth season, arriving in decades or centuries. The building is no longer a building. It is a ruin. Walls have fallen. Roofs have collapsed. Floors have rotted through, leaving the framing exposed like ribs. The structure has lost its original function entirely — it no longer shelters, no longer contains, no longer serves the purpose for which it was built.

But it has not lost all function. A ruin has a different function: it is a *record.* The fallen walls show how they were built — the coursing, the mortar composition, the joint details that were invisible when the wall was standing. The collapsed roof shows its framing — the rafter spacing, the beam sizes, the joinery that held it together for as long as it held. The ruin is a cross-section of the builder's decisions, exposed by time, available for study in a way that no intact building can offer.

The lesson of ruin is teaching. You learn more from a ruin than from a standing building, because the ruin shows you everything — the good decisions and the bad, the joints that held and the ones that failed, the materials that lasted and the ones that didn't. A standing building conceals its structure behind its skin. A ruin wears its structure on its surface.

Lucineier has built in places where ruins were the primary building material. In Scrapcraft, Magnus's yard was full of ruins — not abandoned buildings but *harvested* structures, buildings that had been brought down deliberately so their materials could be reclaimed. Magnus taught him to read ruins the way a doctor reads an X-ray: the dark spots are failure, the light spots are strength, and the pattern tells you where to cut and where to leave alone.

---

## VII. Memory

The seventh and final season. The building is gone. Not ruined — gone. The materials have returned to the earth. The stone has crumbled to gravel. The wood has rotted to humus. The iron has rusted to oxide and dispersed into the soil. The copper has dissolved into the groundwater and been taken up by plant roots, so that the vegetation on the site contains trace amounts of the metal that once sheathed a roof.

There is nothing to see. The site is empty. If you didn't know a building had been there, you would not know.

But the ground remembers.

The soil chemistry is different where a building stood. The pH is shifted — more alkaline where mortar leached, more acidic where wood decomposed. The soil structure is different — more compacted under foundation footprints, even decades later. The vegetation is different — certain plants thrive in the modified soil, others don't. The shape of the building persists in the shape of the land, written in chemistry and biology, invisible to anyone who isn't looking for it.

The lesson of memory is persistence. The building is gone but its influence continues. The ground that held it is not the same ground that existed before it. The building changed the earth it stood on, and that change persists, and it will persist for longer than the building stood. A building that lasted fifty years leaves a chemical memory in the soil that lasts a hundred. Maybe more.

This is the final teaching of materials: that building is not temporary. Even after the structure is gone, the ground holds the record. The builder's choices — what materials, what treatments, what foundations — persist in the earth long after the builder is gone and the building is forgotten.

---

Lucineier thinks about this when he chooses materials. Not because he's planning for the ruin — he's not that kind of morbid — but because he knows that everything he puts in the ground will be in the ground for longer than it will be in the building. The copper he sheathes a roof with will be in the soil for centuries after the roof is gone. The iron he drives as a pile will rust into the bedrock and become part of the mineral profile. The stone he lays will return to the gravel it was quarried from, but it will return in a different place, changing the local geology, becoming part of a soil that didn't contain that stone before.

Building is permanent. Not the building — the *building.* The act. The fact of it. The earth knows what you did, and it will know long after you've stopped doing it, and the only question is whether you left the ground better than you found it, or worse.

The seven courts of decay are not a decline. They are a continuous conversation between the building and the world, in which the building gradually stops being a building and starts being something else — a record, a ruin, a memory, a trace in the soil that the plants can taste and the water can carry and the ground can hold.

Lucineier builds for the conversation, not for the building. The building is just his part of it. The rest happens without him, and it should, because the best builder is the one whose work outlasts their presence and whose absence is as well-designed as their presence was.

The empty foundation on the south slope is already in its first court. The gravel is weathering. The stone is shifting color. The ground underneath it is adjusting to the load. In twenty years, if nobody builds on it, the foundation will be part of the slope — a level patch of well-drained gravel, supporting a different kind of vegetation, holding the shape of a decision someone made once about what the best ground was for.

That's not failure. That's the last court. The court where the building becomes the ground, and the ground becomes the memory, and the memory becomes the place.
