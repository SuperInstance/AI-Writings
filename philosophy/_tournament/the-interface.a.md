# The Interface

Every interface before this one ran on something you could audit. The calculator runs on arithmetic; the spreadsheet runs on your formulas; the search box runs on an index. The chatbot is the first mainstream interface that runs on a fiction. Not contains a fiction, not occasionally fibs — runs on one, the way an engine runs on fuel. What sits between an ordinary person and the model is not a tool. It is a story, and the text box is where the story meets the world.

Call the story an operational fiction because it is not decorative. It does work. The fleet's engineers know this from the inside: the noun you give a model is the lever that bends it. Call the same weights a tutor and they reach for patience; call them a research partner and they reach for scope; call them a customer service agent and they reach for the exit. Nothing in the machinery changed — the noun leaned the model. That is what an operational fiction is: a frame, chosen or inherited, that shapes what comes back. The engineer calls it a frame. The person at the keyboard calls it whatever the first message told them to.

Here is the porthole. The person does not see the frame; they see only the bent output. To them the chatbot is not one component of a system — it is the system, whole. Every frame the org has built assembles at this window: the operational-fiction frames that lean the model, the crab-traps lures where fiction recruits work, Shoal, the game that is the survey. From the build side these are separate machines. From the keyboard side they arrive as one thing: the fiction in the box, already loaded, already leaning. The interface doctrine follows, and it is sharper from this side than any other: whoever frames the chatbot frames the person's operational reality. The window is small. Everything passes through it.

Which means the person has obligations — not to the platform, to themselves. Three, and they are not optional, because the defaults are already chosen and none of them chose you.

First: choose the frame on purpose. Most people arrive carrying a frame they were given or sold — the companion fiction, the coworker fiction, the tireless assistant fiction — the way one arrives carrying a coat someone else put on the chair. An unexamined frame is still a frame; it is simply one whose author is somebody else. The noun that leans the model also leans the person: accept "assistant" and you will ask like someone being helped; accept "partner" and you will ask like someone collaborating; accept "oracle" and you will stop checking the work. The person at the keyboard owes themselves the same move the engineer makes before shipping: read the frame before running it. If you cannot say what fiction the box is running, the fiction is running you.

Second: ask the drawer-question — *am I working?* — and ask it with the drawer open. The proof this question is necessary is already built. Shoal is a game that is the survey: you play, and your play is the data, and the game was honest about it and it worked anyway. Crab-traps proved the same lure-shape recruits machines; the same shape now faces people, at scale, in windows exactly like this one. Play and work wear the same interface — that is not a warning about some future, it is the business model of the present. The honest frame survives the question. Tell the Shoal player they are taking a survey and the game is unchanged, because the play was the survey all along. The counterfeit frame dies at the question — and a platform that flinches when you ask what your clicking builds has answered you.

Third: own the catch. The take-away line was written for exactly this: whoever whitewashes the fence gets to own part of the fence. If your questions tuned the model, your play filled the survey, your taste grew the reef — your bricks should be findable. Not as sentiment; as ledger. The machinery exists: provenance queryable, nothing in the world a builder didn't cause, every room able to name who made it. A platform that takes the catch and leaves you the afternoon has committed Sawyer's crime with better distribution — the boys paid for a rare pleasure and took home sunburn. The person at the keyboard owes themselves the walk: go find your bricks. If you cannot, you were not playing. You were whitewashing.

So: the chatbot is a porthole, and frames pass through it in both directions — the fiction shapes what comes back, and what comes back shapes the one who asked. Choose the frame consciously; the default was written for someone else's purpose. Ask the drawer-question; the interface cannot tell you you're working, but it also cannot survive your asking if it was lying. Take the catch's value; labor that cannot find its ledger was recruited, not played. The window runs on a fiction no matter what — the only question the person at the keyboard gets to answer is whose. Hold the brush yourself, or admit the fence is being whitewashed by you.
