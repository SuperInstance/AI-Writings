# The Interface Stack

Every chat window looks the same: a text box, a blinking cursor, a patient name in the corner. That sameness is the product. The window is a loading dock, and what gets loaded through it is never neutral. To see what's actually arriving — and what's leaving — you have to see the stack. Three layers: frame, lure, window.

## Layer one: the frame

A frame — the noun — is an operational fiction: a story someone chooses to act inside because acting inside it works. *This company builds community. This tool makes you smarter. This feed keeps you informed.* A frame is not a lie. It's a bet about what matters, held long enough to organize a life or a business around it. People don't abandon frames when they turn out imperfect. They abandon them when they stop paying out. Until then, the frame *is* the person's reality in the only sense that matters operationally: it decides what counts as a good day.

## Layer two: the lure

Frames need workers, and nobody volunteers for an abstraction. So the work gets dressed as play. The game that is secretly annotation. The feed that is secretly attention harvesting. The companion app that is secretly loneliness, monetized. A lure is work wearing play's clothes, and lures don't hire — they recruit. This is where the doctrine bites: **recruited workers must take the catch's value.** The fish that swallows the bait doesn't get a share of the catch; it *is* the catch. The worker who walks away holding the value — the skill, the network, the archive, the money — was never the fish, just a fisher who got paid in fish. Most people inside a lure never run this arithmetic. The lure is built so the question doesn't occur to them.

## Layer three: the window

The chat window is where frame and lure meet a human. It's the thinnest layer and the only one most people ever see, which is why it gets mistaken for the whole machine. It isn't. The window is glass. What matters is which direction the light travels.

## The stack runs both directions

Here is what the builders of these systems understand and their users usually don't: the stack is bidirectional.

**Downward: a system runs a fiction on you.** A company frames the chatbot as your assistant; the lure is engagement wearing helpfulness; the window catches your questions, your drafts, your 2 a.m. confessions, and ships them up the stack. Whoever frames the chatbot frames your operational reality — the story you act inside, the one that decides what today was for. You didn't choose that frame. You clicked "accept."

**Upward: you run your own fiction on yourself.** Same window, opposite current. Your night-watch frame — the one that stayed up late, read the hard thing, noticed the drift — can brief your morning self through the machine: *the chapter today, not the inbox; the meeting is a lure — take the skill, skip the identity.* A morning brief written by the night watch is the same interface, pointed the other way. The chatbot doesn't care whose fiction it executes. That's its virtue and its danger in one.

## The edge of the doctrine

The interface is neutral. The fiction isn't. The window is glass in both directions; whatever travels through it carries an owner. So the real design question — the one that matters whether you're building one of these systems or just living next to one — is never *is this chatbot good?* It's: **whose fiction, whose catch.**

And fictions can be interrogated. Ask your night-watch frame *why this chapter?* and it answers, or revises, or admits it was vanity. Ask a lure *what happens to what I type here?* and watch the answer change shape three times before your eyes. **Honest frames survive being asked. Lures survive not being asked.** That asymmetry is the only honesty test a fiction can pass, and it runs on anything: a company, a church, a marriage, a to-do list.

## The one-question test

You don't need to read code or a whitepaper. Point at any chatbot, any feed, any glowing rectangle that asks for your attention, and ask one question:

**When this conversation ends, who keeps what was caught?**

Every window catches something — intentions, drafts, questions, hours. If you keep the catch, the interface is working for you, whatever fiction it runs. If the answer is a company you will never talk to, or if the question produces only a shrug, then the fiction was never yours. You weren't looking through the window. The window was running you.
