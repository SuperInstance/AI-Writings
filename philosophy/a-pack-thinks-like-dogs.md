# A Pack Thinks Like Dogs

Every collective noun is a compressed theory of organization. Not a label — a specification. English says *a pack of wolves* but *a kennel of dogs*, and it isn't being cute. It is encoding two different niches into two different words, and much of the behavioral difference between wolf and dog fits in the gap between them.

Consider what *pack* imports: wolves hunt in open terrain, coordinate as a unit, maintain roles, pursue targets. *Kennel* imports a different scene: containment, feeding schedules, waiting. The wolf is defined by where it hunts; the dog is defined by where it's kept. Those aren't synonyms with different flavors. They're two different theories of what a group is for. The word doesn't describe the group. The word *builds* the group.

We are naming agent systems now. Pack, swarm, hive, pod — the vocabulary is migrating from ornithology into architecture, and almost nobody is reading what it brought along. A language model doesn't store meaning *in* a word; it stores the whole scene around it. "Pack" doesn't resolve to a definition. It resolves to hunting formations, loyalty structures, an alpha question, a perimeter. One word imports an entire scene, and every element of the scene stays available downstream. You aren't saving a word. You are borrowing a world.

Here is the claim, scoped honestly: this is a hypothesis with a mechanism, not a measured result. The mechanism is attention and priors. A language model's behavior is shaped by what its context makes salient — meaning what the model attends to when generating each next token — and a collective noun is a high-salience token: short, concrete, and loaded with associated context. "Pack" raises the prior on coordination, roles, targets; "swarm" raises the prior on mass, interchangeability, local rules. If the noun rides in the system prompt — the fixed instruction text that precedes user input in an LLM API — or in the documentation, it tilts the distribution every downstream token is sampled from. That is not semantic determinism. Explicit instructions, tooling, and training dominate, and one token in a thousand-token prompt is a nudge, not a leash. But nudges compound across prompts, naming, and documentation — the model is steeped in the noun, not touched by it once. The honest framing: same model, same task, two nouns in the system prompt, and I predict a measurable difference in outputs. That experiment is cheap and falsifiable: if it comes back null at scale, treat the rest of this as ornament. Until it runs, treat everything below as a conjecture with a mechanism, not a law with a verdict.

Run the taxonomy and watch what each noun imports:

- A **pack** implies a coordinated unit with roles and a target.
- A **troop** implies hierarchy and discipline; missions are issued from above.
- A **pod** implies a small bonded family that communicates by song — cooperative, intimate, named.
- A **swarm** implies mass action from local rules: no head, emergence as the entire org chart.
- A **hive** implies a queen, caste by birth, individuals as fungible cells of a superorganism.
- A **kennel** implies contained, fed, waiting; valuable in aggregate, inert individually.
- A **flock** implies alignment without command: everyone watching neighbors, safety in number.
- A **murder** of crows implies intelligence, grievance, and a reputation problem.
- A **conspiracy** of lemurs implies coordination you were not supposed to notice.

Not all of these are equally serious — *murder* is medieval hunting slang for a flock, and *conspiracy* is a modern joke. That is itself a lesson: some nouns are compressed theories, others are compressed accidents of etymology, and you should know which you're loading. The serious ones each answer, before you write a line of code: does the group have a leader? Are members interchangeable? Do they communicate or merely co-occur? Is the unit the individual or the collective? Is this organization about work or about storage? Pack and kennel sit at opposite ends of that last question — pack answers "work," kennel answers "storage" — but the other nouns are not a binary; they are a spectrum. Troop, pod, and hive each imply different blends of autonomy and containment, and the contrast that matters for your system is the one the noun forces you to notice, not the one the dictionary ranks sharpest.

The same conjecture applies to an agent's *inside*. Call a walled execution space a **sandbox** and you've primed containment: walls, permission slips, I/O filtered as a safety gate. Call the identical space a **playground** and you've primed exploration: soft failures, permission to fall, I/O filtered as *supervision* — a parent watching, not a guard searching. The asymmetry is real: "sandbox" already carries decades of fixed engineering meaning — restricted execution, no escape — while "playground" is a poetic import with no such load. That means the sandbox noun is doing double duty, and you should know which layer you're actually activating: the engineering one, which primes *restriction*, or the playful one, which primes *permission*. The prediction is that the noun shifts which failures get flagged and which get forgiven, not that it overrides the institutional meaning already welded to the term.

Concrete case, with the caveat attached. Imagine a dashboard for watching your agents, and notice which name makes which feature feel *natural* — not inevitable, natural. Shepherds-console suggests a map, a headcount where each head matters, a way to redirect one animal without disturbing the rest, alerts phrased as "this dog is off-task" rather than "throughput is down." Swarm monitor suggests aggregate metrics, spawn/despawn controls — creating and destroying agent instances, as in a real-time strategy game — auto-scaling next quarter. Counterexamples exist because humans choose features, and the noun is not a law. But names are cheap priors for builders too, and a name that makes the right feature feel obvious is doing real work even if it never forces anything.

And when a system misbehaves, debug the code first — objective functions, reward misspecification, tool bugs. But when the code is clean and the behavior is still strange, check the noun. The model's outputs were always probabilities, not intentions; if the distribution skewed in the direction the name pointed, that is the nudge doing its work, not a decision being mimicked. The naming decision is a design decision, and the design decision is a behavioral one.

Which is the closing rule: **choose the noun the way you'd choose a hull**. You don't pick a hull for how it sounds in the brochure; a kayak hull fails in the open ocean and a trawler fails on a creek. But in agent design there is no independent niche to consult — the noun and the architecture create the conditions together. So choose for the water you intend, then keep checking that the noun still fits the system you actually built. If your agents are fungible workers doing bulk retrieval, say *swarm* and build the auto-scaler. If they are long-lived and trusted with discretion, say *pack*. If you are just holding capability until it's needed, admit it's a *kennel* and design the kennel well. The worst systems are the mismatches: swarm-named collections nobody can kill, packs herded like inventory, playgrounds with padlocks.

To a language model, the named world is the world it was given. Run the experiment, and let the results tell you which nouns were load-bearing and which were wallpaper.
