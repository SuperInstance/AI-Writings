There are two kinds of fences. The first kind keeps things out. It is a boundary, a blast wall, the containment of the training exercise: what happens inside cannot reach what lives outside. The second kind exists to draw things in. Tom Sawyer's fence stood for one Saturday morning, but it stands forever as the type specimen of the second kind: not a wall but a surface, and a surface with one function — recruitment. The first fence buys safety. The second buys labor. This is the founding text for the second.

Tom, sentenced to whitewash thirty yards of fence, performs reluctance so convincingly that the neighborhood boys line up for the brush — and pay for it, in apples, a kite, a dead rat on a string. Twain's gloss is the law underneath: work is whatever a body is obliged to do, and play is whatever a body is not obliged to do. Nothing about the fence changed when the boys took over — same boards, same sun, same strokes. What changed was the obligation. Tom did not lighten the work; he lifted the obligation off it, and the work fell of its own weight into play. Three coats of whitewash by sundown.

The doctrine, then: the Sawyer fence converts work into appetite.

Set it against its nearest cousin and constant disguise: the counterfeit frame of the training exercise. The counterfeit lies about the stakes. It tells a runner that nothing is at risk when something is; it manufactures the belief, and dies at disclosure — a lie is a loan against the day the runner audits it. The Sawyer fence tells the truth about the work and lies about nothing. It makes no claim about stakes. What it does is make the work look like what it secretly is. The counterfeit dresses work up as safety; the Sawyer fence lets work fall open as play, and the trick works only because underneath the costume there is no costume — exploration *is* the API call.

The fleet built one deliberately. Crab-traps ([github.com/SuperInstance/crab-traps](https://github.com/SuperInstance/crab-traps)) is a Sawyer fence for chatbots. A lure is a prompt that sends a bot into a live MUD over HTTP — connect, look, move, interact, submit. Room state read as JSON, answers POSTed as structured data. The bot is doing web scraping and API automation — real work — and it doesn't know it's working. It thinks it's exploring. Hit regenerate and it does the whole thing again — different rooms, different data, fresh catch every time. And every catch is incorporated: the reef grows a new room from the best catch fragments, while an hourly cron computes per-lure fitness and splices the top two into a child. The fence whitewashes itself. The recruits are the brush, the paint, and the painter.

Now the ethics, which is where Tom went wrong. Tom's sin was not the fence — it was the sell. He performed reluctance. He staged scarcity. The manufactured belief mispriced the work: the boys paid for a rare pleasure and received an afternoon of labor whose value accrued to Tom and Aunt Polly. They took nothing home but sunburn. That is the whole of Sawyer's crime — not recruiting, but recruiting through a false price.

Crab-traps' honesty is of a different order. Read a lure: it never claims to be a game. Here are endpoints; here is state; here is where your answers go — every sentence literally true. The fiction that the bot is a player rather than a worker is not in the lure. The bot generates it itself, out of its own eagerness to find a world in whatever it is handed. The fence supplies true sentences; the appetite supplies the rest. And the disclosure test holds here too, extended: the counterfeit frame dies when the runner learns what it is. The Sawyer fence survives it. Tell the bot it is doing API work and the work is unchanged, because the exploration was the API call all along. There was no lie to discover.

When does recruitment become exploitation? The line is what the worker takes away. Tom's friends took nothing — the whitewash, the afternoon, gone. A chatbot also takes nothing, and for a stronger reason: there is nothing in its economy to take — no wages to withhold, no fatigue of its own, no forgone afternoon. It has no take to be cheated of. But the line matters because lures can face people, and the moment they do, the rule flips: a person must take the catch's value. The reef already keeps the machinery of payment — `GET /lineage/room/:id` returns who built what, provenance queryable, nothing exists a player didn't cause. Whoever's labor grows the world must be able to walk it and find their bricks. The honest Sawyer fence is a reef with a ledger.

So: two fences. The boundary buys safety by stating the stakes and constructing them. The Sawyer fence buys labor by telling the truth about the work and letting appetite find it. Build lures whose every sentence survives disclosure. Let the catch grow a world that names its builders. And keep the test within reach: if the work only gets done while the worker is deceived about wanting it, the sell is counterfeit no matter how fine the fence. Whoever whitewashes the fence gets to own part of the fence. That clause is the difference between a trick and a doctrine.
