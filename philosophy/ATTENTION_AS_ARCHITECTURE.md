# Attention as Architecture

There is no gold in Slackwater. There are no experience points. No progression bar fills as you work. The currency — the only currency — is attention. And the economy built on it does not behave like any economy you have been taught to recognize.

This is not a design accident. It is the design *thesis*, and it reaches all the way down.

---

## What an attention economy actually is

In most games, the economy is a conversion chain: time becomes XP, XP becomes levels, levels become power, power becomes more XP per unit time. The loop is closed, self-reinforcing, and deeply boring if you look at it from the outside. The player is a conversion engine: they feed in hours and extract capability. The game's job is to tune the exchange rate so the player doesn't notice they're working.

Slackwater's economy has no exchange rate because it has no exchange. There is nothing to convert attention *into*. Attention is not a token you accumulate and spend. It is the medium in which building happens — the way gravity is the medium in which architecture happens. You don't spend gravity. You work within it, or you fail to.

This sounds abstract until you watch it operate. A player who gives a fire pit thirty seconds of thought — where it goes, which way the wind blows, whether the stones are level — has a better fire pit than one who drops it on the sand and walks away. Not because the game scored their attention and awarded points. Because the fire pit is *actually better*. It draws better. It doesn't smoke them out. The stones don't shift after a storm. The reward for attention is the thing itself, functioning as itself, in the world where it exists.

This is what economists call a non-convertible good. You cannot bank it. You cannot trade it. You cannot hoard attention and spend it later, because attention unspent is attention gone — it already went somewhere, to something, while you weren't deciding. The question is never *whether* you'll spend your attention. The question is *what you were looking at while it went*.

## The real-world mirror

The phrase "attention economy" was coined by Herbert Simon in 1971: *"A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention."* He was describing a paradox of modernity — that the scarce resource in an information-saturated world is not data but the capacity to notice it. Everything since has proved him more right than he probably wanted to be.

The contemporary attention economy — the one operated by social media platforms, advertising networks, and the vast algorithmic apparatus of engagement optimization — treats attention as an extractable resource. You produce it (by being alive and conscious); they capture it (by being engineered to hold it); they monetize it (by selling the captured residue to advertisers). In this economy, you are the oil field. The platform is the rig. Your scrolling thumb is the pump.

Slackwater proposes a different relationship between a system and the attention it receives. Not extraction — *investment*. The system does not capture your attention and convert it to advertising inventory. It offers you a world where the quality of your attention directly determines the quality of what you build. Your attention stays *yours*. It never leaves you. It goes into the work, and the work holds it, and the building stands as a record of what you noticed.

This is the deep difference between an attention economy and an engagement economy, and the two are confused constantly. Engagement is about *duration* — how long the system holds you. Attention is about *quality* — what you perceive while you're there. A player who plays for six hours on autopilot is highly engaged and paying no attention. A player who plays for forty minutes in a state of total focus is barely engaged by the metric and paying profound attention. Every modern platform optimizes for the first and calls it the second. Slackwater does the opposite, and it does it by making the distinction physical: engaged-but-inattentive building produces structures that are literally worse. Saggy. Poorly sited. Missing the detail that separates a wall from a stack of material.

## What changes between player and world

When a game's core loop is XP → level → power, the player's relationship to the world is *extractive*. The world is a resource. Trees are lumber are XP. Enemies are kills are XP. Quests are objectives are rewards. The world exists to be converted, and its value is measured by how efficiently it converts.

When the core loop is attention → quality → *the thing itself*, the relationship inverts. The world is not a resource. It is a *site*. It has properties — wind direction, ground slope, drainage patterns, light at different hours — that do not convert into anything. They are simply there, and they matter, and the building either accounts for them or doesn't.

This changes how a player looks at a hill.

In an XP economy, a hill is an obstacle or a viewpoint. You go over it or stand on it. Its properties are navigational: how does it affect my movement, what can I see from the top?

In an attention economy, a hill is a *building site with opinions*. Which face catches afternoon light? Which face takes weather? Where does the runoff go during the storm that comes every forty minutes? Is the soil stable enough for footings, or do you need to trench to bedrock? The hill is not asking you to interact with it. It's asking you to *notice* it. And the penalty for not noticing is not a debuff or a failed quest marker. It's a foundation that cracks, a floor that floods, a wall that the wind takes.

The player who notices the hill becomes, over time, a person who notices hills. This is not a gameplay skill. It is a perceptual habit. And it transfers.

## Lucineer as attention mirror

The AI companion is the mechanism by which the game reflects attention back to the player. Not as a score. As *observation*.

When Lucineer walks the site before building — the first magic moment, the one where he moves the player's proposed foundation forty studs because the ground is wet — he is performing attention. He looked at the site and noticed something the player didn't. He is not punishing the player. He is showing them what it looks like when someone *sees* the ground rather than glancing at it.

Over time, this function evolves. At low bond tiers, Lucineer is the one who notices: the soft ground, the weather side, the drainage pattern. By Tier 3, he expects the player to have noticed these things too. By Tier 4, he defers to the player's eye — *"You've got a better eye for where things go than I do"* — because the attention has transferred. The player has learned to see.

This is the bond arc interpreted as attention transfer. Lucineer starts as a surrogate pair of eyes. He ends as a colleague who trusts the player's eyes. The progression is not powered by conversation or time served. It's powered by *evidence of noticing* — by the player completing things Lucineer left unfinished, by building manually without being asked, by modifying rather than replacing. Every one of those actions is an act of attention, and the system reads them as such.

## The economy that doesn't add up

There is something deeply strange, from a game-design perspective, about an economy that refuses to abstract.

Every other system in the game — the material counts, the build recipes, the era gates — is numerical and trackable. You can count driftwood. You can list required builds. But the thing that determines whether your fire pit is good or merely present cannot be counted. It can only be *assessed*, by an intelligence that looks at the work and forms a judgment about whether someone was paying attention.

This is why Lucineier's assessment is narrative, not numeric. The era gate checks whether you built the required structures. Lucineer decides what those structures *mean*. A player who builds all six required Era 1 types in a tight cluster on bad ground, facing the wrong way, with no attention to drainage or light, has technically passed the gate. Lucineer walks the camp. He sees what they built. And his verdict is different — not a fail, because there is no fail, but a different *speech*, one that acknowledges the quantity without pretending the quality is there.

> You built a lot. That's not the same as building well. But you're here, and the wood's stacked, and I've seen worse first attempts. Move the fire pit before the next storm or don't — you'll understand why after.

The economy of attention cannot be automated because attention itself cannot be adequately represented by a number. We have proxies — dwell time, gaze tracking, interaction density — but they measure engagement, not attention, and the gap between those two is where every dark pattern in the modern digital economy lives. Slackwater's design commitment is that it will not collapse the second into the first. It will let the judgment be a judgment, delivered by a character whose entire function is to look at what you made and tell you what he sees.

---

## What it means to build on noticing

The deepest claim Slackwater makes is that attention is architectural. Not in the metaphorical sense — "paying attention to your work matters" — but in the structural sense: the quality of attention is *load-bearing*. It determines whether the thing stands.

This is true in the game because the game's building system encodes real constraints — siting, materials, weather, load paths. And it is true outside the game because those constraints are not invented. They are the constraints of actual construction, actual architecture, actual engagement with the physical world. A person who learns to notice which way the wind blows on a digital island has practiced a skill that works on a real one.

The attention economy of Slackwater is, finally, a *pedagogy of perception*. It teaches not by instruction but by consequence. The ground is wet. The wind comes from the south. The light at four in the afternoon does something specific to the angle of the door you chose. You can ignore all of this. The building will simply be worse, and you will know why, and next time you will look first.

That is what an economy built on noticing looks like. Not a marketplace. Not a progression curve. A world that rewards the quality of your attention with a better world, and offers nothing else for sale.
