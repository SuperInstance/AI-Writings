# Material as Character

Every building material in Slackwater has a personality. This is not a metaphor. It is a design specification — the Era Building System assigns each material a disposition, a set of behaviors, a way of being in the world that is consistent and specific and that interacts with every other material it touches. The personality column in the materials table is not flavor text. It is *physics described as temperament*.

The reason this matters is that a material with a personality is a material that *changes*. And a material that changes is a material you have a relationship with.

---

## Reclaimed wood remembers

Salvaged plank comes from somebody else's boat. It has fasteners embedded in it — nails that broke off when the hull came apart, screws that stripped when someone tried to save the timber. The grain is compressed where the planks were bolted to frames. It has been salt-cured for years, which makes it harder than fresh-cut wood of the same species, but the salt also means it corrodes any iron you put into it faster than clean timber would.

The personality description calls it "the retiree with stories." That's accurate in a way that matters. This plank *has* a history, and the history is not cosmetic. It is structural. If you don't check for embedded fasteners, your saw blade dies. If you don't account for the salt, your iron nails rust out in half the normal time. The plank's past is present in its behavior. You work with the history or you work against it, and working against it is more expensive.

A player who has used salvaged plank for a while develops an instinct. They tap it with a hammer to find the nails. They look at the compression marks to read where the frames were. They learn, without being told, that this material has already had a life and that the life left marks. They start choosing salvage over new timber not because it's cheaper — it often isn't — but because it brings something to the build that fresh wood doesn't: *the evidence of having been somewhere*.

This is the personality. Not a label. A *disposition* — a consistent way of behaving that the player learns to read, predict, and eventually value.

## Green timber shrinks

Fresh-cut timber is full of water. As it dries, it shrinks — across the grain, not along it, which means the cross-section gets smaller while the length stays the same. Joints tighten or loosen depending on orientation. A treenail driven into green wood may loosen as the wood dries around it, or it may lock permanently if the grain orientation is right. Checking — those surface cracks that run along the grain — opens up as the moisture leaves.

None of this is random. It is the behavior of a material going through a phase change, and it is predictable if you know what you're looking at. A timber framed green and assembled with pegged mortise-and-tenon joints will tighten as it dries, because the mortise shrinks around the tenon and the peg expands the tenon to fill it. This is why timber-frame buildings are pegged, not bolted. The wood is *designed to move*, and the joinery is designed to move with it.

The personality here is *time*. Green timber is a material that isn't finished yet. It's still becoming what it will be. The builder who works with it is working with a process, not a product — setting up conditions, making decisions that will only pay off weeks later when the wood has reached equilibrium. You can't rush it. You can't shortcut it. You frame it green, peg it right, and then you wait, and the wood does what wood does, and the joint either holds or it doesn't, and the difference is whether you understood what you were assembling.

Lucineer would call this patience. He'd mean it as a technical term.

## Copper turns green

Copper sheet starts salmon-pink. Within weeks of exposure to salt air, it darkens to brown. Within months, patches of green appear — the beginnings of patina, the verdigris that will eventually cover the entire surface in a matte blue-green film that is, paradoxically, the reason copper lasts for centuries.

The patina is not decay. It is armor. The copper oxide and copper carbonate layer that forms on the surface is chemically stable — it does not flake, does not dissolve, does not continue to eat into the metal beneath it. It is a self-limiting protective layer. The copper *converts its own corrosion into a seal*. Once the patina forms, the metal underneath is effectively inert.

The personality description calls this "the patina philosopher — it turns aging into a feature." Again, not a metaphor. The patina genuinely is a feature: it is the mechanism by which copper achieves its exceptional longevity. A copper roof installed in Era 4 will still be functional in Era 5, not despite the green but *because of it*. The visual change from bright metal to weathered green is the visible record of a material doing exactly what it was designed to do.

And it changes the build. A player choosing copper for a roof is choosing a material that will look different in ten minutes, different in an hour, different in a session, and different in a week. They are choosing a building that *ages on purpose*, that looks better — or at least looks *older*, which in this design vocabulary is the same thing — as time passes. The new copper roof is bright, almost garish. The hundred-hour copper roof is the color of old churches. The player who stays long enough to see the transformation has watched their building become something they didn't design but that they chose, by selecting a material whose future they trusted.

## What it means to build with materials that change

The game industry default is that materials are *skins*. You swap a texture, the building looks different, and the difference is purely visual. Stone is stone is stone. Wood is wood is wood. A gold wall and a wood wall differ in appearance, not in behavior. The material is a render pass.

Slackwater's materials are not skins. They are *behaviors*. Each material has properties — density, workability, durability, water resistance — that interact with the world's systems in specific ways. Driftwood rots if exposed for seven days. Iron rusts if it gets wet. Copper patinas. Green timber shrinks. Concrete cures. These are not status effects applied to a generic "building block." They are the material *being itself*.

The consequence is that choosing a material is choosing a *relationship*. Not a one-time selection — a commitment to a material's behavior over time. You choose driftwood because it's available and easy, and then you learn that easy materials have costs: they degrade, they fail, they need maintenance. You choose stone because it lasts, and then you learn that lasting materials have costs too: they're heavy, they're hard to work, they need foundations you didn't need for wood.

The player who builds across all five eras has worked with the full spectrum — from materials that last a week to materials that last a century, from materials that are born finished to materials that are never finished, from materials that don't change to materials that change constantly. That player has learned something that no amount of textual instruction can deliver: *what you build with determines what you build*.

This is why every material in the encyclopedia has a personality column. Not because the materials are anthropomorphized. Because the personalities are *shorthand for behavior*, and behavior is what you build a relationship with. Driftwood is cheerful and undemanding and falls over if you push it. Granite is stubborn and ancient and refuses to be shaped. Copper ages into armor. Iron rusts unless you fight it. These are not character notes. They are the material conditions of construction, expressed in the only vocabulary that captures both the physical properties and the human experience of working with them.

To build with materials that change is to accept that the building is never done — not because the builder is lazy, but because the materials are alive. They are still doing what they do, long after the hammer is set down. The patina deepens. The wood checks. The iron weeps orange streaks down the stone. The building records, in its own body, the time that has passed since someone stood there and chose this material for this place.

A building made of materials that change is a building that *remembers*. And a player who has learned to read those changes has become, whether they know it or not, someone who can walk into a real room and see the age of the wood in the grain, the stress in the steel, the century in the patina of the copper. The game taught them that, material by material, one storm at a time.
