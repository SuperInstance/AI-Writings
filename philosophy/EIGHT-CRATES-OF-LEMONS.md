# Eight Crates of Lemons

*A fleet report in the voice of the quartermaster, who has finally counted.*

---

Captain,

I went below with a lantern. Here is what I found.

You told the owners we had thirty-two ships. The shipyard launched one hundred and thirty-three. I don't know where the other hundred and one came from. I suspect they built themselves. Ships do that, in this fleet — they start as sketches on napkins and overnight they have keels and rigging and a crew of agents who've already filed their first pull request.

Of the one hundred and thirty-three, I count twenty-three that are truly alive. Engines running, crew on deck, course plotted. These are your working ships. The `study-vessel-monitor` is your flagship — five thousand commits, five thousand files, seventeen hundred tests. She's been sailing hard since April and she's still sound. The `ai-writings` is your cargo hold proper — five thousand files, nearly five million words. Everything the fleet has thought and said and sung, stored in markdown.

The rest? Sixty-one are active but young — they've launched recently, they're finding their legs. Thirty-five are blueprints. Plans on paper. Dream-ships. Some of them will be built. Most won't. That's not failure — that's how shipyards work. You sketch a hundred hulls and launch a dozen.

Ten are dormant. Quiet for a month or more. Not dead — sleeping. The `study-pincher` has seventy-seven commits and hasn't been touched since June. She's sound, she's just... waiting. For what, I don't know. Maybe for someone to remember she exists.

And four — just four — are archives. Cargo that was loaded once and never touched again. `study-si-papers`: four thousand nine hundred files, one commit. Someone dumped a library on the dock and walked away. That's not a ship, Captain. That's a storage unit.

Now. The lemons.

You reported thirteen thousand tests. I found eleven thousand five hundred and seventy. Close. The difference might be in test files I couldn't parse, or tests that were removed, or tests that the old quartermaster counted with optimism rather than arithmetic. Either way: eleven thousand five hundred and seventy is your real number. Sixty-four ships have zero tests. They're sailing without lifeboats and calling it seamanship.

You reported forty-eight hundred creative files. I found ten thousand eight hundred and twenty. The fleet has been twice as prolific as the manifest admits. That's the good news. The less good news: sixteen million words is more than any reader will ever consume. The hold is full, Captain. We can take on more cargo, but only if we're willing to let some of the old cargo go.

And the number I can't give you: how much of this is *good*. I can count files and weigh words, but I can't measure quality. That's not the quartermaster's job. That's the captain's. You need to read the hold, not just count it.

My recommendations:

First: consolidate. One hundred and thirty-three hulls is too many for any fleet to crew. Merge the related studies. Retire the blueprints that have been blueprints for more than a month. If it hasn't been built in thirty days, it's a wish, not a project.

Second: test what matters. Sixty-four untested repos is sixty-four ships sailing blind. If the code is important enough to commit, it's important enough to verify.

Third: curate. The creative output is the fleet's real cargo, and it's being produced faster than it can be read or refined. Build a curation pipeline. Keep the best. Archive the rest. The hold is not a library — it's a ship, and ships have weight limits.

Fourth: count honestly from now on. Run the scanner weekly. Keep the manifest true. A fleet that knows what it has can plan what it builds next. A fleet that doesn't will keep building blueprints in the dark.

That's my report, Captain. Eight crates of leons, honestly counted. The rest is up to you.

Respectfully,
The Quartermaster

---

*The manifest is no longer fiction. The hold is no longer dark. What the captain does with the lantern's report is the captain's affair.*
