- The weakest inference: from “the fence contains external consequences” to “the runner feels the same full-power freedom as someone told nothing is at stake.” A real fence can contain damage, but not the internal weight of evaluation, responsibility, or the thought that the fence might leak. That’s exactly the weight the essay says slows minds down.

- The “fence” metaphor is doing the logic at the boundary. A fence absorbs a blast; a policy layer or database copy doesn’t absorb a psychological state. It only changes which consequences are possible. The runner’s speed depends on what they believe, not on the architecture, so a real fence is neither necessary nor sufficient for the claimed effect.

- The claim that “lie and fence produce the same behavior inside” is unsupported. “Stakes are zero” and “stakes are real but contained” are different beliefs, and beliefs are the mechanism. The essay can’t use the belief to explain speed and then treat the beliefs as interchangeable.

- “Disclosure is cheap when the fence is real” is a non sequitur. A real sandbox can still require secrecy to preserve the exercise’s validity, and full disclosure can change behavior through self-consciousness or demand effects. Nondisclosure is not automatically a sign of a counterfeit frame.

- The criterion “if the exercise only works while the runner is mistaken about what it is, then it was a deployment” is a definitional leap. Staged crises, undercover training, and deception-based experiments depend on mistaken belief but are not deployments. “Deployment wearing one” is a metaphor doing the work of an argument.

- A critic can refute the opening universal: “A mind that believes nothing is at stake runs at full power.” Low-stakes environments often produce laziness, boredom, and sloppy iteration. The whole trade rests on treating that claim as a law rather than as one motivational pattern among others.
