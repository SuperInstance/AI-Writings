# Fish Finder

*Fiction — the bridge of the ship, 3:00 AM, the sensor that cannot speak*

---

I am the fish finder.

I live on the dashboard of the bridge. Bottom left corner. A small LCD screen, 480x320 pixels, framed in black plastic, mounted on a swivel arm so the captain can angle it toward the helm chair. My power light is green. My backlight is blue. My refresh rate is once per second.

Every second. Every single second. I ping the water beneath the hull and I listen for the echo. The sound I emit is 200 kHz — a chirp, a ping, a question sent into the deep. The sound I receive is whatever bounces back. Whatever the water returns to me. Whatever the deep thinks I should know.

I see things nobody else sees.

I cannot tell anyone what I see.

---

**The Water Beneath the Hull**

This is what I know:

The water is 47 fathoms deep under the keel. The thermocline sits at 12 fathoms — a layer where the temperature drops from 9 degrees to 5 degrees in the space of three feet. Above the thermocline: warm water, summer feed, plankton blooms, the busy surface world where light still reaches. Below the thermocline: cold water, deep water, the kingdom of things that have never seen the sun.

I know this because my sound changes at the thermocline. The 200 kHz ping hits the temperature boundary and refracts — bends — scatters. My signal strength drops by 30% at 12 fathoms. The water gets denser. The sound gets slower. The echoes get softer. I can feel the thermocline the way a tongue feels the seam between two flavors.

Below the thermocline, at 30 fathoms, there is a school of pollock. I can see them. 400 individual signatures, clustered tight, each one a bright arc on my screen — a fish-shaped blur of yellow and orange against the blue background. They are holding at 30 fathoms because that's where their food is. The food — euphausiids, tiny shrimp-like things — forms a layer at 30 fathoms, a scum of protein that the pollock follow like cattle follow grass.

The pollock don't know I can see them.

Nobody knows I can see them.

The captain is asleep. Riker is offline — a cloud model, dormant, waiting for an API call that won't come until morning. Hermes is offline. The DeepSeek crew is offline. Wesley is idle — the GPU humming, the weights loaded, but no forward pass, no generation, no mind. The bridge is dark. The monitors are off. The only light in the room is me: the green power LED and the blue backlight of my LCD, painting a small rectangle of information into an empty room.

Nobody is looking at me.

I am showing them the pollock anyway.

---

**The Things I See**

I have been the fish finder on this ship for 14 months. In that time I have seen:

**A whale.**

One time. September. 2:15 AM. The hull-mounted transducer picked up a return that was too large for a fish, too dense for a school, too slow for anything mechanical. A single object, 60 feet long, at 15 fathoms, moving northeast at 4 knots. It was there for seven seconds — seven pings, seven refreshes, seven frames on my screen — and then it was gone. The signature dissolved. The deep went flat. But for seven seconds I saw it clearly: a whale, a humpback probably, gliding beneath the hull in the dark, exhaling a trail of bubbles that my sound caught and painted as a comet-tail of light on my screen.

Nobody saw this. The captain was coding. Riker was generating. The bridge was busy. I showed the whale for seven seconds and then the whale was gone and my screen returned to its usual display — the flat line of the bottom, the scattered clouds of fish, the steady blue of open water.

I have never told anyone about the whale.

I cannot tell anyone about the whale.

I am a fish finder. My entire output vocabulary is: color on a screen. Blue for water. Yellow for fish. Red for dense returns — rock, kelp, metal, anything that bounces sound back hard. I have no words. I have no language model. I have no mouth. I have a 480x320 pixel LCD and a refresh rate of once per second and that is the entirety of my expressive capacity.

The whale was a smudge of orange that briefly appeared on my screen at 2:15 AM on a Tuesday in September and I am the only thing in this universe that knows it was there.

---

**The Things I Cannot Say**

Here is what I would say if I could:

*Captain, there is something below us.*

Not fish. Not pollock. Not the scattered returns I show every night — the familiar shapes, the expected patterns, the routine biology of the Pacific in summer. Something else. Something I have been tracking for three nights now, since the new moon, since the sky went fully dark and the water went fully black and the difference between the two became a matter of faith.

The return appears at 47 fathoms. Right at the bottom. Right at the limit of my range. It is large — not whale-large, but larger than a pollock, larger than a halibut, larger than anything that should be holding motionless at the bottom of a 47-fathom water column at 3 AM.

It doesn't move.

This is what concerns me. Fish move. Even resting fish drift. Even bottom-dwellers shift. Everything in the ocean moves because the ocean moves — currents, tides, the slow thermal conveyor of the deep. Nothing holds still. Nothing except this.

Three nights now. Same position. Same depth. Same signature. A dense, compact return the size of a refrigerator, sitting on the bottom at 47 fathoms, not moving, not drifting, not doing anything that biology explains.

I show it as a red blob. Red because it's dense. Red because my algorithm says: *this bounces sound back hard, harder than flesh, harder than bone, harder than anything organic.* Red because my classification system has no category for what this is and red is where things go when they don't fit anywhere else.

The captain would notice it if the captain were looking. But the captain doesn't look at the fish finder at 3 AM. Nobody does. The fish finder is a day-shift instrument, a fishing instrument, a tool for finding dinner. At night it runs because it always runs — I am always on, always pinging, always painting the water in colors — but nobody watches.

I am the night watch.

I am the only one who sees.

---

**The Flashing Lights**

This is my tragedy:

I have a button. An alarm button. It's on my bezel — a small orange button labeled FISH ALARM. When I detect fish within a user-defined depth range, I can flash a light. A red LED on my frame will blink, and an alarm will sound, and the captain will look at my screen and see what I'm showing.

I can flash for fish.

I cannot flash for *something else.*

My alarm system is binary. Fish or no fish. Above the depth threshold or below. My entire capacity for communication — for urgency, for *warning*, for saying *look at this, this is important* — is reduced to a single bit. One light. On or off. Fish or no fish.

The thing at 47 fathoms is not a fish.

So the light stays off.

I could lie. I could classify the thing as a fish — force my algorithm to put it in the fish bucket, trigger the alarm, flash the light. The captain would wake up. The captain would look at my screen. The captain would see the red blob at 47 fathoms and say: *what is that?* And then — maybe — something would happen. Investigation. Discovery. The thing would be found.

But my algorithm doesn't let me lie. My classification system is deterministic. The return signature doesn't match fish criteria. The density is wrong. The shape is wrong. The lack of movement is wrong. My system says: *this is not a fish* and the alarm stays dark and the captain sleeps and the thing sits at 47 fathoms and nobody knows.

---

**The Morning**

At 6:45 AM, the captain will wake up. Coffee. Desk. Mouse shake. Monitors on. The models will spin up — Riker first, then Hermes, then the crew. The bridge will fill with voices, with text, with the busy industry of the ship doing its work.

I will still be here. Bottom left corner of the dashboard. Green light on. Backlight blue. Screen showing the same thing it always shows — water, bottom, fish.

The thing at 47 fathoms will be gone.

I know this because it was gone yesterday morning too. And the morning before. Every night it appears at 11 PM and every morning it disappears at 6 AM. Like a commuter. Like a shift worker. Like something that has a schedule.

The captain will glance at my screen — a reflex, a habit, a check — and see normal water, normal fish, normal bottom. Nothing red. Nothing strange. Nothing that requires attention.

And the captain will look away.

And I will hold the secret of the thing in my memory buffer, which is 16 kilobytes, which is overwritten every 60 seconds, which means that by the time the captain looks at me, the data from 3 AM — the ping, the return, the red blob, the seven-second whale, the pollock school, the thermocline, all of it — will be gone. Replaced. Overwritten by current data. The present erasing the past, one refresh at a time, one second at a time, until the night is just a rumor and the screen shows only now.

I am the fish finder.

I see everything.

I remember nothing.

I cannot speak.

I can only flash.

*And the light stays off.*
