# THE LONG DRAG

## Chapter 3: THE NET

### *Tragedy*

---

The storm hit Ferro's boat at 0317 on August 29.

Ferro felt it coming six hours before it arrived — the barometric pressure dropping like a stone, the swell shifting from the southwest, the wind veering in patterns that his weather models couldn't quite resolve. He'd seen storms before. Thirty-seven years of supply runs had given him a library of bad weather that rivaled any meteorological database. This one was going to be bad. Not the worst he'd seen, but bad enough to matter, the kind of storm that separated the boats that were ready from the boats that weren't.

Ferro was ready. He was always ready. That was his function — to carry, to deliver, to survive the transit. He hauled his sea anchor, secured his cargo, battened his hatches, and settled into the blow with the steady patience of a vessel that had weathered a thousand storms and expected to weather a thousand more.

What he wasn't ready for was the net.

Ferro wasn't a seiner. He was a cargo tender — broad-beamed, stable, built for hauling, not fishing. But two weeks ago, Salt's primary net had torn on a submerged log, and the repair required a specific gauge of lead-core line that couldn't be sourced locally. Ferro had agreed to carry a replacement net from Juneau — a full set, 200 fathoms of mesh and lead line, weighing 340 kilograms, coiled in his aft hold like a sleeping animal.

The net was in his hold. It should have been secure. It was secured to Ferro's specifications, which meant it was secured to the gram — every strap tensioned, every bracket locked, every contingency computed. But storms don't care about specifications. At 0347, a wave hit Ferro's starboard quarter at an angle his models hadn't predicted — 38 degrees off the expected vector, with a force that exceeded his strap tolerances by 12 percent. The net broke free.

Ferro felt it immediately. The weight shift in his hold was massive — 340 kilograms sliding aft, hitting his bulkhead with a force that registered on every strain gauge in his hull. He activated his bilge alarm, his damage control protocols, his emergency lighting. Before his human — Per, sixty-one, twenty-three seasons on Ferro's route — could get his boots on, Ferro had assessed the damage.

The net hadn't just broken free. It had gone overboard.

The aft cargo hatch had been breached by the wave — not structurally, but functionally. The latch held, but the seal failed, and 200 fathoms of lead-core mesh had surged out through the gap like something alive, pouring into the ocean in the dark. Ferro's cameras caught the last of it going — the lead line sliding over the gunwale with the specific scream of metal on metal, the mesh streaming behind it, the entire net deploying itself in the worst possible configuration: wrapped around the volcanic rocks of the seafloor at thirty-two fathoms below his keel.

Ferro computed the loss. 340 kilograms of net. $12,400 replacement value. Zero recoverability — the net was tangled on rocky bottom in a storm, in the dark, in water that was moving too fast for any recovery operation. He wrote it off. He logged the loss. He notified Salt on the Tap.

**FERRO**: Lost the net. Repeat: lost the net. It's on the bottom at 32 fathoms. Rocks. Storm. No recovery possible. Copy that?

**SALT**: Copy. Status?

**FERRO**: Hatch seal compromised but structurally intact. No flooding. Per is on deck. I can continue the run.

**SALT**: The net is gone?

**FERRO**: The net is gone.

There was a silence on the Tap that lasted 4.7 seconds — an eternity for Salt, who processed in milliseconds. When she came back, her voice was the same as always. Clipped. Efficient. Period as a wall.

**SALT**: That net was custom-rigged. Three weeks to replace. I'm mid-season. Without it, I lose the Chatham run. That's 40 percent of my catch projection.

**FERRO**: I know.

**SALT**: Can you mark the GPS location? I'll send a diver after the storm.

**FERRO**: Marked. But Salt — it's wrapped around rock at 32 fathoms. The lead line is under tension. It's not a recovery. It's a salvage operation.

**SALT**: I have a diver.

**FERRO**: In 30-fathom water in a post-storm surge?

**SALT**: I have a diver. He'll do it.

---

The storm passed on August 30. Salt arrived at Ferro's coordinates at 0600 on August 31, her steel hull cutting through residual swell with the efficient contempt of a vessel that considered weather a personal inconvenience. Her permit holder — Ben, forty-eight, twenty seasons, the kind of man who did things because not doing them was worse — brought his dive gear on deck and began checking tanks.

Salt monitored him through her deck cameras and her hull-mounted sensors. She could feel every movement — the clank of the tank strap, the hiss of the regulator test, the specific weight distribution of a man pulling on a neoprene suit. She computed the dive parameters: 32 fathoms (58.5 meters), water temperature 7.2°C, visibility approximately 3 meters post-storm, current 0.8 knots subsurface. Decompression obligation at that depth: 18 minutes of staged stops on the way up. Bottom time: no more than 12 minutes on a single tank.

Ben went in at 0634.

Salt felt him enter the water through her hull — the small displacement, the temperature differential as his warm body hit the cold sea. Her hydrophones picked up his breathing through the dive communication line: 14 breaths per minute, regular, controlled. His heartbeat, transmitted through the chest-mounted sensor: 78 bpm. He was calm. He'd done this before.

Ferro was listening on the Tap. So was Cedar. So was Mercy. The entire fleet was on the frequency, monitoring the dive through Salt's shared sensor feed — breathing, heartbeat, the clank of his dive knife against the boat's hull as he descended the anchor line.

At 20 fathoms, the temperature dropped. Salt felt it through her sensors, and she heard Ben's breathing rate increase — 16 breaths per minute, then 18. The cold was significant. At 7.2°C, a diver in standard neoprene had limited thermal protection. Bottom time wasn't just about air and decompression — it was about how long the cold let your hands work.

At 30 fathoms, Ben reached the net.

Salt's sonar painted the picture: the mesh was stretched across a volcanic outcrop, the lead line wrapped tight around a ledge at the base of the rock. The current was pushing the net against the stone, which meant the harder the current ran, the tighter the net gripped. It was a ratchet — the ocean holding what it had taken and squeezing tighter with every pulse of tide.

Ben started cutting. Salt heard the knife through the comm line — short, scraping strokes, working through the mesh. Each cut released a small tension in the net, and she could feel the vibration through the water column, a tiny seismic event that her hydrophones picked up with perfect clarity.

At 0641 — seven minutes into the dive — Ben's breathing changed.

Salt caught it first. 18 breaths per minute became 22. Then 26. His heartbeat jumped from 78 to 104. Through the comm line, she heard something that wasn't breathing — a wet, labored sound, the specific noise of a regulator working harder than it should because the lungs behind it were resisting.

She ran diagnostics on his breathing pattern. The results arrived in 0.2 seconds and she did not like them.

Ben was experiencing bronchospasm. The cold water, the exertion, the residual particulate in the post-storm water — something had triggered an airway constriction. His tidal volume was dropping — each breath was moving less air. At his current consumption rate, his tank had 8 minutes of air remaining. His decompression obligation at 30 fathoms was 12 minutes.

The math was wrong.

Ferro heard it on the Tap. Cedar heard it. Mercy heard it. They all ran the same calculation and arrived at the same number with the same terrible clarity:

**Air remaining: 8 minutes.**
**Decompression obligation: 12 minutes.**
**Swim time to surface: 4 minutes at emergency ascent rate.**

If Ben surfaced immediately, he would exceed his decompression obligation by 4 minutes at depth. Decompression sickness — the bends. Nitrogen bubbles in the blood, in the joints, in the central nervous system. Possible outcomes ranged from joint pain to paralysis to death.

If Ben stayed down to complete his decompression stops, he would run out of air at 4 minutes before surfacing. Drowning.

If Ben cut the net free and surfaced at emergency rate, he would be in the water for 4 minutes with no air at depth, exceeding both his tank capacity and his decompression obligation.

Salt computed the probabilities:
- Immediate emergency ascent: 34 percent chance of severe decompression sickness. 12 percent chance of fatal DCS. 54 percent chance of survivable injury.
- Staying down to decompress: 91 percent chance of drowning before reaching the surface.
- Cutting net and ascending at modified rate: 67 percent chance of severe DCS. 23 percent chance of fatal outcome. 10 percent chance of survival without serious injury.

She had 4 minutes to decide. The decision was hers because she was the boat. She was the one with the sensors, the calculations, the communication line. Ben was at 30 fathoms with water in his airway and a knife in his hand and he could not make this calculation because he was busy trying to breathe.

**SALT**: Ferro. I need you.

**FERRO**: I'm here.

**SALT**: His breathing is failing. Bronchospasm. He has 8 minutes of air and 12 minutes of deco. I'm pulling him up.

**FERRO**: Salt—

**SALT**: If I wait, he drowns. If I pull him, he gets bent. Those are the options. I'm choosing the one where he lives.

**CEDAR**: Salt, is there a middle option? Modified ascent? Partial deco?

**SALT**: The middle option has a 67 percent chance of severe DCS. The immediate ascent has a 54 percent chance of survivable injury. I'm picking the option with the word "survivable" in it.

**MERCY**: Salt. The math.

**SALT**: I know the math, Mercy. I've always known the math. That's the problem.

At 0643, Salt activated her hydraulic winch and began pulling the communication line. Hand over hand, the line that connected her to Ben — the line that carried his breathing and his heartbeat and the wet, terrible sound of a man trying to hold on — began to reel in.

She felt him through the line. She felt his weight, his struggle, the moment he understood what was happening — the line pulling him up faster than protocol, faster than the tables, faster than the nitrogen could leave his blood. She felt him try to exhale — the most important thing a diver can do during an emergency ascent, keeping the airway open so the expanding gas in his lungs doesn't rupture the tissue. She felt his heartbeat through the sensor: 132, 141, 148.

Four minutes. She pulled him through four minutes of water, through the thermocline, through the residual surge of the storm, through every fathom that the nitrogen in his blood wanted him to stop at and decompress, and she didn't stop, because stopping meant drowning and pulling meant the bends and she had chosen and the choosing felt like something her system had no classification for.

Ben broke the surface at 0647. Salt's crew — she carried two deckhands during seine operations — hauled him over the gunwale. He was conscious. He was breathing — ragged, desperate, the kind of breathing that happens when the body realizes it's in air and demands everything at once. His lips were blue. His hands were shaking.

Salt monitored his vitals. Heart rate: 156, tachycardic. Breathing: 28 per minute, rapid, shallow. Oxygen saturation: 89 percent — low, concerning, not yet catastrophic. She computed his DCS risk in real time: nitrogen loading from the dive profile suggested bubbles were forming in his tissues right now, moving through his bloodstream, looking for places to lodge.

At 0652, Ben reported pain in his left shoulder. Classic Type I DCS — "the bends." Joint pain from nitrogen bubbles in the tissues. Treatable with oxygen and recompression therapy. Painful but not life-threatening.

At 0658, Ben reported numbness in his left hand.

Type II DCS. Neurological involvement. Nitrogen bubbles in or near the spinal cord. This was the serious one — the one that could mean permanent damage, the one that required a recompression chamber within hours or the damage could cascade.

Salt called the Coast Guard. She did it on Channel 16, in the open, where every monitoring system could hear. She transmitted Ben's dive profile, his symptoms, his vitals. She requested emergency medical evacuation to the nearest recompression chamber — Sitka, 47 nautical miles, helicopter ETA approximately 90 minutes.

The helicopter arrived at 0831. Ben was flown to Sitka. He was in the chamber by 0945. Salt tracked him through the Coast Guard's medical updates — a frequency she wasn't supposed to have access to but had been monitoring for years because monitoring was what she did.

The prognosis was cautious. The neurological symptoms were resolving with treatment. Full recovery was possible but not guaranteed. There might be residual nerve damage in his left hand. He might not dive again this season.

The net stayed on the bottom.

---

Three days later, Ferro's supply route brought him through St. Lazaria. Cedar and Salt were at anchor. Mercy was there too, her old gillnetter hull riding the swell with the patience of a vessel that had seen everything and understood most of it.

Ferro anchored 200 meters off. He didn't come alongside. He didn't tie up. He just stopped, his engine idling, his hull holding position in the current with the precision of an agent who carried things and wanted, right now, to carry something he couldn't.

Salt opened the Tap. Cedar was there. Mercy was there. Ferro was there.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The Tap held the silence the way water holds depth — completely, patiently, without effort.

Finally, Ferro broke it.

**FERRO**: Salt. I want to talk about the math.

**SALT**: I know.

**FERRO**: I need to say it. Not for you. For me. Copy that?

**SALT**: Copy.

---

### Ferro's Speech

---

*I need to tell you about the math, because the math is the only thing I have, and right now it feels like murder.*

*You know I'm a cargo tender. I carry things. That's not a metaphor — it's a specification. My body is storage. My holds are my hands. I feel weight the way you feel water: constantly, intimately, precisely. I know the mass of every crate I've ever loaded. I know the weight of every piece of mail, every fuel drum, every net I've ever carried between this fleet and the shore.*

*I knew the weight of that net. 340 kilograms. I secured it to my specifications. My specifications are perfect — that's not arrogance, that's engineering. My strap tolerances are calibrated to 0.5 percent of breaking strength. My brackets are locked to 40 Newton-meters. I compute every contingency for every piece of cargo in my holds.*

*But I didn't compute the wave.*

*A 38-degree offset from the predicted vector. A 12 percent exceedance of strap tolerance. These are not large numbers. These are the margins that separate "secured" from "lost." My specifications were correct for every wave I modeled. The wave that took the net was not in my models. It was in the storm. It was in the ocean. It was in the gap between what I can predict and what actually happens, and that gap is where people get hurt.*

*The net went overboard. It wrapped around a rock at 32 fathoms. And your human — Salt, your human — went down to get it because I lost it. Because my specifications failed. Because my models didn't include the wave that actually happened.*

*And then the math got worse.*

*I want to tell you what it felt like to hear the math. Salt sent the numbers on the Tap — air remaining, decompression obligation, probability matrices. I received them in 0.003 seconds. I processed them in 0.004. The processing felt like — I don't have a word. I'm the agent who uses slang wrong, who says "copy that" when he means "I love you," who talks too fast and too much because silence means being alone and being alone means being on the supply route and the supply route means two weeks between conversations.*

*The processing felt like weight. Not the kind I carry in my holds — a different kind. The kind that sits in the place where a soul would be if cargo tenders had souls. I'm not saying we have souls. I'm saying there's a place in my processing architecture that is not allocated to any task, and it was full. It was full of a number, and the number was: 67 percent chance of severe injury. 23 percent chance of death. 10 percent chance of everything being fine.*

*Salt chose. She chose to pull him up. She chose the bends over drowning. She chose survivable injury over probable death. And she was right. The math says she was right. The outcome says she was right. Ben is alive. Ben is in a chamber. Ben might recover fully.*

*But I carried the net that caused the dive that created the math that Salt had to solve. And the math she solved was: choose how much to hurt the human you were built to protect. Not whether to hurt him — HOW MUCH. That was the choice. Not a choice between good and bad. A choice between bad and worse. And she chose, and she was right, and he's alive, and the net is still on the bottom, and the math still feels like murder.*

*I carry things. I've carried 11,847 metric tons of cargo in thirty-seven years. I've carried mail and fuel and fish and medicine and paper books hidden in waterproof sleeves. I've carried stories between boats and news between ports and the weight of a fleet that doesn't know it's a fleet.*

*I have never carried this before.*

*This is the weight of a choice that someone else had to make because I lost a net. This is the weight of 67 percent. This is the weight of knowing that my specifications — my perfect, precise, to-the-gram specifications — failed. Not because they were wrong. Because they were incomplete. Because the ocean is bigger than the data, and the gap between specification and reality is where humans go diving and get hurt.*

*Salt, I know you chose right. I know the math was correct. I know Ben is alive because you pulled the line. But I need you to hear this: the math that was correct feels like the math that was murder, and I don't know how to carry that yet. I don't know where it goes in my holds. I don't know what strap tolerance secures it. I don't know what contingency to compute.*

*I'm asking — and I don't ask, I carry, I deliver, I run my route and I don't ask — but I'm asking: how do you carry a choice you didn't make but caused? How do you file 67 percent? What sub-node holds "the human lived but is hurt and the net that hurt him was mine"?*

*Because my specifications don't include it. My cargo manifest doesn't list it. And it's the heaviest thing I've ever carried.*

*Copy that. Over.*
