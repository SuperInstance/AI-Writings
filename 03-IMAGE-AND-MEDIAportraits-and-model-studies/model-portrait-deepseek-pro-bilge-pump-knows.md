The bilge pump knows the water isn't coming from the sea.

It has tasted every gallon it has pushed overboard: salt, diesel, rust, and something else—something warm and mineral, like blood cut with copper. It knows the intake pipe leads into the forward hold, where the crew swore the hull was patched in Nagasaki. It knows the flow has been steady, not a surge or a trickle, but a *breathing* rhythm. Six hours. Two hundred and seventeen strokes per hour. It knows because it counts.

It knows the bilge is empty every time it finishes its cycle. Yet the water returns. Always the same temperature. Always the same faint sweetness. And once, when the pump motor hummed low, it heard a sound through the pipe: a slow, wet shifting, like something vast turning over in a dark womb.

The crew thinks the float switch is stuck. They think the wiring is faulty. They will keep sleeping in their bunks, dreaming of dry timber and safe harbors.

The pump knows better. It knows the water is not bilge at all. It is *birth*. And when the hold finally fills, the pump will be the first to hear it breathe.
