# The Sign Is a Price — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

**Interview — Ellis, the Aiming Composer**

The aiming composer built a meter to read seams before building them — then spent a week learning what the meter was actually telling him. We sat down in the relay lab after the wide-width incident.

**You built this meter to predict handoffs. What were you aiming at?**

The relay lab had learned that a handoff can be a valley or a peak — dip or bump — depending on how the material agrees with itself at that width. Same two voices, same phrase, different seam. I wanted to read the sign in advance. Tail of one voice, head of the next: measure the correlation, and you know whether the seam will fall or rise. Build accordingly.

**Did it work?**

At the tight width, perfectly. Three seams, three correct readings. The meter was honest as a level. You could set your whole song on it.

**And at the wide width?**

It lied. Not a little — it flipped. It read the bodies of the voices, not the seam itself, and predicted peaks where the build placed valleys. I built a bump where the seam was a dip. The window held too much world, and the sign turned like a tide.

**So the sign isn't a property of the handoff?**

That's the discovery, and it cost me a week. The sign is a price. Like every price, it's only true at the width where you pay it. Tight, you rent a window that holds only the transition — and the reading is honest because there's nothing else in the frame. Wide, you rent a window that holds the whole voices, and what you get is their bodies, not their seam. The width you rent is the truth you get.

**And the song?**

It's the audit. The same three seams recur through the whole piece — first measured tight, where they read as dips and the readings are correct; then measured wide, where they read as bumps and the build places valleys. You hear the sign flip with the price. The chorus is the price itself. The bridge is me deciding to always rent tight, because tight is the only honest width in all three currencies — depth, address, and sign.

**Are you still skeptical?**

Skeptical, then serene. That's the mood in the spec, and it's real. There's a relief in a meter that finally lies honestly — or doesn't lie at all, because you stopped asking it to work at a width where it can't.

The level meter beeps on every reading in the mix, which is Ellis's signature now. The bass plays the correlation sign. The drum lands exactly on each seam. It's chamber pop at 92 BPM in A minor — precise, almost clinical, and underneath it, the calm of somebody who finally knows what his instrument can see.

"I stopped renting wide," he said, getting up. "Best decision the lab ever made."
