# Tempo-War — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

They met in the west hall of the songforge lab, the night somebody mis-booked two ensembles into the same evening.

The strings were rehearsing at 72 — patient, warm, C major, a melody like walking somewhere you're in no hurry to get to. The brass were across the folding partition at 80, playing the same melody without knowing it, brighter, a half-step closer to eager. Neither side could hear the other. The building could.

The intern noticed it first. She was clocking rehearsal time for the log, and her stopwatch kept disagreeing with itself in a way she couldn't write down, so she fetched the designer and said: you have to stand in the doorway. In the doorway, at the seam of the two rooms, the tempos beat against each other like two clocks on a shelf, and every seven and a half seconds the beats kissed — a little phase-slip, a shimmer, gone.

But kisses are cheap. What the designer waited for, standing in that doorway half an hour while everyone got steadily later for dinner, was the full agreement: both ensembles landing a downbeat on the same instant. The strings tick nine beats in the time the brass tick ten — arithmetic; the lab loves arithmetic — and four of those crossings brings thirty-six string-beats and forty brass-beats home together on a bar line. Thirty seconds. Once every thirty seconds, through the partition, two bands who had never met said the same word at the same time, in the same key, with no idea they were doing it.

"We're not fixing it," the designer said, when someone offered to lend the brass a metronome. "We're writing it."

The prompt calls it Tempo-War, but everyone in the lab calls it the opposite of a war: two clocks that love each other and can never quite agree. The strings at 72 and the brass at 80, one theme, unaware. The alignment is the chorus — brief, glorious, arriving whether you deserve it or not. The drift is the verse, and most of a life.

The only argument was the paperwork. The renderer wanted one number for the page: what BPM is this song? 72 and 80 were both true and neither was. The designer wrote 76 and said the heart of this song lives exactly between them, which is where the doorway is.

They recorded it with the partition still up. Orchestral minimalism in the lineage of the phasing experiments — the same family as the frozen-clock runs down the hall — strings patient, brass half a step eager, warm and longing all the way through. The final alignment comes, holds for one bar of impossible agreement, and then the song ends mid-drift, because that's what clocks do: they don't resolve. They just keep time, and every thirty seconds, if you stand in the seam, love looks exactly like arithmetic.
