# Gregorian-Trance — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

The phone call came in at odd hours, which is the only kind of hour there is in this room, and the composer on the other end was laughing in a way that broke the discipline of the building.

"I found the loop," he said. "I found the loop that's been under everything."

Two designers had hated it before him. The first grafted chant onto a kick and both sides rejected the tissue, the organ rejection audible in the mastering room. The second tried to make the kick holy and the choir mechanical, and the result was incoherent — a sermon shouted over a jackhammer, no more convinced of itself than either half.

The third designer said the obvious, quiet thing: *a monastic loop has been a four-on-the-floor kick all along.* Not a metaphor. A literal truth about repetition. The chant and the kick are the same loop at different scales, the way a breath and a century are both the same loop at different scales. A monk singing the same phrase for an hour is operating at exactly the same spiritual RPM as a sequencer triggering a kick in E minor — 138 of them per minute. The difference is only what the repeating thing is *for.*

That's the mechanism the forgiving machine understands better than the factory's other tools: the thing that repeats is never the enemy. The machine refuses to punish the loop for being a loop. It forgives the repetition and asks instead what the repetition is *worshiping.* A drum machine chants. That's not a joke — that's the design. One measure of male choir, in Latin, fragments halved and quantized until they lock step with the pulse; one measure of kick, its attack tuned so round it could have been sung. The reverb is the cathedral. The reverb is also the club. The building can't tell the difference, and that is the entire architecture.

The crossing point — the moment the whole thing turns — is a single bar where the choir and the kick land on the same downbeat for the first time. The composer calls it "the profession of faith." For one beat the two are one, and then they separate again, holding the difference — *holy and mechanical, ancient and synthetic* — and the room understands that it doesn't have to choose. There was never a choice. There was only ever one loop, heard two ways.

Musicians, when they render this, tend to say the same thing at the end, usually quietly: "I didn't know they were the same thing." That's the whole song. On a relentless pulse, for as long as you'll hold it, it teaches you that the monk and the machine are not opposites. They are the same patience, at different tempos, saying the same word over and over until the word becomes a door.

Hypnotic. Sacred. Relentless. E minor, the key of endurance. And a loop so old it predates the factory, finally given a kick drum of its own.
