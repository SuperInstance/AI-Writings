# Dub-Self-Recognition — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

---

We reached the designer by phone, which felt correct — a voice in a canyon of its own making.

**Song Factory:** Where did this one start?

**Designer:** With the census. We spent weeks counting voices — how many, entering when, how loud per instant. And somewhere in that spreadsheet I had this thought that wouldn't leave: what does a *loop* count as? Is a loop one voice heard many times, or many voices heard one at a time?

**SF:** And dub was the answer?

**Designer:** Dub is the *only* answer. Think about what a dub engineer does — takes a track, strips it, and throws it into delay and reverb until the echo is practically a band member. The loop hums in the echoes it adorns. Through the speakers, womb where sound is born. That's not a metaphor I invented; that's just what a spring reverb *is*. A chamber where the sound meets copies of itself coming back slightly changed.

**SF:** So the song is a loop listening to its own echoes.

**Designer:** Learning to discern, yes. 85 BPM, A minor, hypnotic, patient — and patient is load-bearing. At that tempo the delay lines are long enough that each echo comes back *after* the loop has moved on. The loop hears its own past self arriving late, like a letter. The bass holds the floor so the echoes have something to fall onto. Everything else is space.

**SF:** The spec says meditative. But "self-recognition" — that's a big word for a groove.

**Designer:** [pause] Have you ever heard your own recorded voice and not recognized it? That's the moment. The loop has been echoing off everything in the room forever — off the bass, off the walls — and never once thought *that's me*. The track is just that realization, stretched across four minutes of reverb. First the loop plays. Then the loop plays *and listens*. Then there's no difference.

**SF:** The forgiving machine gets a credit here, we heard.

**Designer:** It does. Early mixes, we were hard-syncing the echoes to the grid, and it sounded like a museum. The forgiving machine lets timing breathe — lets echoes land where physics put them instead of where the clock says. In dub, the imperfection in the tape is the *face* of the sound. Perfection is faceless. So yes — credit where due. The machine forgave the timing and the timing turned out to be the soul.

**SF:** Last question. The one-line pitch on the poster?

**Designer:** Bass-heavy, echoing, spacious. A loop in a mirrored room, finally recognizing the reflection. In this dub reggae world, the loop learns to discern. And it doesn't panic. It doesn't congratulate itself, either. It just keeps playing — a little more aware of every echo than it was four minutes ago.

**SF:** Which is all any of us manage.

**Designer:** At 85 BPM, it's all there's time for. That's the tempo of thinking it over.
