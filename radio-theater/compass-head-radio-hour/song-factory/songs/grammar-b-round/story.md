# Grammar B — Round — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

**Story — Boats on a Widening River**

Odette taught the same round to the same four singers every Tuesday, and on the third Tuesday of the grammar study, she stopped correcting the drift.

The prompt had warned her — grammar B doesn't warn, it describes: "Four voices begin the same line one after another, chasing each other around the melody. The slowest voice trails by a little more each pass. The harmony stretches; the parts separate like boats on a widening river."

She laughed at the simile when she read it. Rivers don't widen in a Tuesday-night rehearsal. Boats don't separate unless somebody rows. Then Ansel, her bass, began to trail — a little more each pass, exactly as described — and she watched the simile become an observation.

It happened the way weather happens: so slowly that you'd miss it if you weren't watching for it. Pass one, the four of them chasing the melody around the circle, F major at 72 BPM, gentle as a hymn. Pass two, Ansel a hair behind, and the harmony stretched a little — not wrong, just wider. Pass three, the stretch was a distance, and Odette could hear the round becoming two songs that happened to be about the same thing. The soprano glanced at her, the way singers do when they want permission to fix something. Odette shook her head. She had stopped correcting the drift, because the drift had stopped being a defect. It was the arrangement.

By the fifth pass, the three fast voices were finishing their phrases in a warm, close cluster — together, the way they'd always been — and Ansel was out on the water, a full bar behind, singing the same line with the same words and somehow meaning them later. The parts had separated. The river had widened. Odette stood at the edge of the room like a person on a bank, watching the last boat.

The prompt had promised the ending too: three voices finished and gone; the slow voice still singing the last phrase alone. Not sadly — inevitably. Odette heard the soprano stop, heard the second voice stop, heard the tenor stop mid-nod, and heard Ansel carry the line alone to its last note, where he held it — the single sustained final note — while the others stood at the edge of the stage like people who have come down to the water.

Afterward, in the log, she wrote: "The drift was always the arrangement."

Down the hall, the other grammar children were learning the same lesson in different dialects. The A round's slow voice had discovered it from the inside, from the boat. Odette's round discovered it from the bank. Same river. Both true. Grammar B had just been the one that said so out loud.
