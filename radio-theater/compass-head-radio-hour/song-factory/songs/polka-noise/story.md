# Polka-Noise — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

It happened on a Tuesday, which is important, because the designer who did it would never have done it on purpose on a Friday.

The lab was running a batch of Oktoberfest prompts — accordion, oompah, tuba, the whole lederhosen catalog, 120 BPM and friendly as a parade. Meanwhile, on the other side of the patch bay, a noise rig was warming up for a separate experiment: feedback loops, distortion pedals chained like guard dogs, found percussion. The two rigs shared a patch cable. One of them — nobody has ever confessed, and the designer maintains a dignified silence on the subject — plugged the accordion's output into the noise chain's input.

The first take lasted eleven seconds before someone ran in.

Here's the thing about the transmission tax: it's usually a small levy, a little bit of signal lost at each handoff, barely noticeable. But the noise chain doesn't levy a tax. It demands everything. It ran the accordion through the distortion and the feedback until the melody couldn't pay, and what came out the other end was a German polka in the middle of being eaten — the oompah rhythm intact and grinning, the tuba still thumping the downbeat like nothing was wrong, and the accordion screaming its melody into a wall of feedback that swallowed it note by note. B-flat major dissolving into atonality in real time, like a tent collapsing in slow motion.

The room was silent. Then somebody laughed. Then everybody laughed, including the designer, who had already started saving the file.

"Merzbow meets Oktoberfest" was written in the notes that night, and it stuck. The dark humor is the point — this is the only song in the catalog that is genuinely funny, and the funniness is structural, not decorative. The accordion keeps trying to be a cheerful polka. The noise keeps eating it. The tuba keeps playing oompah as if the apocalypse were just a scheduling conflict. 120 BPM, but unstable — the tape speed wavers like a man who's had one too many steins, the rhythm teetering between dance and collapse. The found percussion is a row of beer steins, recorded and slammed.

The designer was asked, eventually, whether the accident should have been fixed. She said no. She said the most honest Oktoberfest recording ever made is one where the celebration is real enough to attract feedback — where the joy is loud enough that the universe starts answering it with static. She still won't say who plugged in the cable. The song is the confession.
