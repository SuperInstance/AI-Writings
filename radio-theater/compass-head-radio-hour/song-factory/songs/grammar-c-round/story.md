# Grammar C — Round — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

**Essay — What the Recipe Doesn't Say**

Grammar C renders the round as a flat list, and the list is worth looking at in full, because its silences are the song:

"genre: a cappella vocal round; bpm: 72; key: F major; instruments: four vocal parts, no instruments; mood: gentle, inevitable, solitary at the end; structure: round entries staggered, voices drop out one by one, single sustained final note; avoid: instruments, percussion; extra: one voice noticeably slower, round gradually drifts apart, ends with only that voice."

Notice what the recipe does not say. It does not say which voice is slow. It does not say why the round drifts — only that it does, as if drift were a direction, when drift is a weather. It does not say what the voices are singing about, or to whom, or whether the single sustained final note is a relief or a grief. The ledger's fields end where the song begins.

And yet the song is right. This is the essay's claim, and the study's whole point about grammar C: the missing details are where the music lives. C is the grammar of the ledger, but the ledger's silence is its mercy. Everything it cannot say, the renderer must imagine — and imagination is the only instrument the spec doesn't list, the one instrument in the factory with no key-value pair.

Look at the most important entry: "mood: gentle, inevitable, solitary at the end." Four words that carry the entire human condition of the round — the tenderness of four voices agreeing, the fatality of the drift, the solitude of the last voice. The recipe does not say "not sadly." It doesn't have to. "Inevitable" is a word that already contains the whole lesson the round was built to teach: some endings are not sorrows, they are appointments. The renderer hears "inevitable" and sings it without sorrow, and the song arrives whole.

The family comparison writes itself. A's round was a telegram: "one voice is slower, the round drifts, and only that voice finishes" — a dare, trusting the machine to dream the gaps. B's round was weather: boats on a widening river, a simile that became an observation. C's round is a form. And here is the surprise the study saved for last: the form is the most honest of the three. A pretends it isn't asking for much; B pretends it's describing; C admits it's handing over a recipe and hoping.

The single sustained final note is the only line in the recipe that is literal. Every other line is a hint with a colon. The last line is a fact. The slow voice sings it, and the recipe, for once, has nothing left to add — which is exactly when the song becomes more than the recipe, in all three grammars, every time.
