# The Knife-Edge — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

The aiming composer had spent years placing silences. Dips, valleys, the quiet seams where one voice hands the line to the next — she could hear a seam at twenty paces, and she could aim one the way other people aim a camera. "You find the valley," she liked to say, "and you put the handoff in the valley, and the handoff lands soft. That's the whole craft."

The afternoon it all stopped being simple was a Tuesday, and it started with a meter report.

The same address. The same crossfade — sixty milliseconds of recording, measured two ways. By one measurement, a valley thirty-one decibels deep: the deepest quiet the relay had ever rented, a seam so soft you could sleep in it. By the other measurement, a peak thirty-one decibels high: a summit. The material, asked to hand the line over, had answered with a blade. Dip on one edge, bump on the other. The same sixty milliseconds of real estate, cut into two truths.

The composer did what composers do when the meter lies in both directions at once: she re-ran the measurement. It agreed with itself. Both times.

That afternoon became the song. Two voices in a close canon at 88 BPM, E minor, singing the same phrase twice — once mixed to valley, once mixed to peak — so the listener can hear the address change its mind. The verses alternate the two measurements of the same place: verse, dip, verse, bump. The chorus is the blade itself, the dip and the bump sounding together, a single triangle struck at the knife-edge address so the ear can find the seam by sound alone. And the bridge is the hard part — the bridge is the composer deciding not to choose. Letting the feature be whatever the material meant it to be. Learning to read the sign of the seam before naming it.

The lab asked her, afterwards, whether the material had been broken. "No," she said. "The material was honest. A valley and a peak are the same amount of change — thirty-one decibels from the center, in opposite directions. I'd been naming features before reading them. Some materials stay valleys through every listing because they never agree with themselves at that width. This one agreed. It just agreed in both directions."

The tape loop at the end reverses the seam and plays it again as a peak, so the song's last gesture is the blade turning over in the light. She says she still doesn't know if it's a valley or a summit. "It's a knife-edge," she said. "You don't choose what a knife is for. You learn which edge faces up. And you aim accordingly — a peak is as aimable as a valley, when the material agrees with itself. The awe of a blade is that it was never one thing. It was always the decision, arriving in two copies."
