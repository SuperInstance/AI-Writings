# The Morph — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

**Essay — "The Transition Is the Song"**

The relay lab spent its first season learning to be suspicious of seams. Every handoff between voices showed up in the meters as a dip or a bump — a valley or a peak — and for a long time the lab treated those as defects. We widened windows, tightened gains, tried to make one voice leave and the next arrive as if nobody had moved. We were polishing the thing we should have been listening to.

The morph came out of a question somebody asked at 2 AM during a crossfade-arithmetic session: what if the handoff wasn't the flaw, but the feature? What if a song could be built entirely out of the moment the crowd becomes a thread?

The design is almost embarrassingly simple, which is how you know it's true. Four singers enter one after another, piling up into a chorus. Then — overlap by overlap, handoff by handoff — the pile dissolves until exactly one voice carries the line at all times. Nothing is louder; everything is more continuous. The verses shorten the overlap between voices, one bar at a time, until the overlap is a single shared breath. The choruses are one voice handing to the next mid-word: the first singer starts the word, the second finishes it, and for a split second they are the same person. The crowd is gone, and the going is the song.

There's an entry-order theorem in the lab notes that nobody had paid much attention to: in a well-formed handoff chain, the voice that enters first is the one that remains when the chain dissolves. The morph is the first song built to obey it on purpose. The first voice in is the last voice out, and the song is the shape of that sentence.

At 80 BPM in E minor, it walks. That's the right tempo for a crowd thinning — fast enough to feel inevitable, slow enough to hear each overlap shorten. E minor is a key that doesn't argue; it just keeps going until it's alone.

For years we treated arrangement as scaffolding — the structure you remove to reveal the song underneath. The morph is the confession that the arrangement was the song all along. The staircase becomes a chain; the document is the crossing; the density, and then the fading of the density into a thread, is what the melody was doing under all that harmony we kept adding.

And the ending — the last voice alone, having forgotten there were ever others. Not sadly. Precisely. That's the hardest part to render and the easiest to hear: a voice that doesn't miss the crowd it came from. It has forgotten, the way a thread has forgotten the cloth.

The transition is the song. We'd been writing around that for a year. The morph is the first time we wrote it down.
