# Quartet-Arrival — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

The trio had been together long enough that they'd stopped calling themselves three. They called themselves the group, and the group sang four-part arrangements with three voices — the tenor and baritone trading the bottom, the alto carrying the middle, and the top line, the high register, simply not there. The empty mic stand was a fixture. They'd written it into the jokes: "Maybe someday," they'd say, gesturing at the stand, "our fourth will show up for a change."

The census is what noticed first. The lab ran a census of their recordings — it does that, counts the voices in every file like a groundskeeper counting the flock — and the numbers came back with a hole in them. Every arrangement pointed at a high register that was never filled. Not accidentally: the composer had written the top line for years, out of habit, out of hope, and the trio had learned to sing around it. The census didn't report a flaw. It reported an address. A register, unoccupied, waiting — a gap in the stereo field where a voice was clearly expected to live.

The composer took the census printout to the trio and said, gently, that the group had been a quartet with one absentee member for a very long time, and maybe it was time to send the absentee a letter. They laughed. Then they auditioned.

The fourth singer was a soprano who arrived late to her own audition, walked in, saw the empty mic stand, and stood at it — not knowing it was the designated spot, just choosing it, the way water chooses the low place. They ran the oldest round they knew, the Renaissance one, D major, four entries. The first three voices entered and wove, sparse and familiar, and then the high register entered last, on top, filling the gap that had been a fixture for years — and the census was wrong, the composer said later, because the census could only count voices. It couldn't count what happens when the last voice completes a chord. The harmony changed from sparse to full in a single bar, like a door closing and a room becoming a home in the same motion.

That's the whole song. The music performs the arrival: three voices in polyphony, expectant, the texture thin where the top should be, and the fourth entering last — radiant, high, resolving everything the trio had spent years singing around. 80 BPM, a cappella, no instruments, because instruments would have been a fourth presence that wasn't *the* fourth. The empty stand is still in the studio, but now it holds a microphone.

The soprano sings the top line, and the census, the next time it ran, returned four. It was the first time in the lab's history that a hole in the ledger was filled by a person walking in and standing in it.
