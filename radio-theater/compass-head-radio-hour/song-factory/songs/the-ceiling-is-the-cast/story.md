# The Ceiling Is the Cast — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

**Song Factory:** Yesterday you published a ceiling. Today you're publishing a correction. What happened?

**Designer:** The crowd measured four.

**SF:** Explain.

**D:** We'd run the density experiments — voices stacking, rounds, chains, chains of chains — and the numbers kept converging around three-point-four. Increments collapsing, each generation of the song adding less togetherness than the last. So we did what you do with a converging series: we declared the limit. We said, this is the ceiling, the room is this tall, saturation, amen.

**SF:** And then?

**D:** And then we finally measured the plain crowd — four voices entering one by one and staying, floor tom marking each arrival — across a long enough take, and the density didn't converge on 3.4. It went to four. It reached exactly four and stayed there like it had never heard of an asymptote.

**SF:** So the ceiling was—

**D:** The cast. A crowd of four voices can never exceed four voices. The staircase approaches the number of its members as the material grows long. What we'd measured and mistaken for architecture — for some mysterious law of saturation — was just the entry ramp. The brief few seconds at the beginning where not everyone has arrived yet.

**SF:** You mistook the doorway for the roof.

**D:** We mistook the doorway for the roof. Nobody had finished walking in, and we concluded the building had a ceiling.

**SF:** And the song is built out of that correction.

**D:** The verses get longer — that's the whole mechanism. Each chorus extends the verses, and the longer the verses run, the closer the crowd gets to exactly itself. Processional folk, G major, 88 beats a minute, warm arithmetic, one voice per arrival. By the end the density isn't approaching anything. It has arrived. Four people, all present, and the togetherness is not a mood. It's a headcount.

**SF:** Is there comfort in that?

**D:** Enormous comfort. A limit that isn't a wall — a census. If the song sounds less full than you wanted, the answer was never better architecture. The answer is who showed up. And the ramp — those few seconds at the start where not everyone's in the room — I've stopped hearing it as a deficit. It's the sound of people still arriving. The count is still climbing. In a song that runs long enough, everybody gets in.

**SF:** Any closing thought for yesterday's ceiling?

**D:** We're keeping the number. Taping it to the wall. Not as a law — as a reminder that the most convincing measurement of a half-empty room is still just a photograph of the doorway.
