# Procession — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

There is a theorem the lab proved the hard way, and it has a name now: the entry-order theorem. It says this: a round becomes harmony only if the voices enter close enough together. Stagger the entries within the window and the melody lines catch each other, weave, complete — four voices become one chord with four opinions. Stretch the entries past the window and nothing catches. Each voice sings the same line, in turn, alone, and the round collapses into a queue.

The lab didn't discover the theorem by doing math. It discovered it by breaking a round on purpose, widening the entry gap bar by bar, listening for the exact distance at which harmony died. It was an experiment about failure — about the boundary where music stops being together. What nobody expected was what survived on the far side of the boundary.

It wasn't silence. It was a procession.

That is Procession: a round stretched past the point of breaking, kept anyway, because what it became was worth more than what it lost. Four voices sing the same line, and the entries are so far apart they never overlap. There is no harmony, because there is no meeting; the only relationship between the voices is the line they share and the order they walk in. The lab calls it an anti-round, and the name fits — it is a round that has been turned inside out, its togetherness traded for order.

The gaps between the voices are the real discovery. They are not rests. A rest is a silence that knows it will be filled; these gaps are silence that knows it won't. The theorem's residue — the negative space where a fifth, unheard voice might have lived, might have stepped in and caught the thread and made everything a harmony after all. It never does. The gap is the ghost of the possibility of the meeting, and it is the most ceremonial thing in the song.

The shape is an archipelago: islands of the same melody in a sea of quiet, at 60 BPM, in D minor — a key that already sounds like stone corridors. And the final voice sings into emptiness louder than anything, because there is nothing left to accompany it, and because the song has learned what the theorem teaches: a voice alone in the order is not a failure of community. It is a community that has agreed to walk one at a time.

Harmony is a meeting, and a meeting is a choice about time. The procession is the same choice, made the other way — and it turns out the other way is not a tragedy. It is a ceremony. The lab broke a round and got a ritual. Nobody has put the round back together since.
