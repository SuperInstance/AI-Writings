# Last-Voice-Standing — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

The experiment was a relay, and the relay failed — which is how the song got made.

Five models, one prompt, handed down the chain: *four friends sing rounds together; one by one they fall silent; the last verse belongs to the one who was always slowest.* Each model heard only the output of the one before it, and each one lost something. The first returned a cheerful harmony with all four voices intact — too early, didn't understand. The second dropped a voice without noticing. The third dropped two and turned cheerful anyway. The fourth returned static, the round collapsed into noise, and the chain should have died there.

The fifth model was the slowest one in the lab. It was the forgiving machine's favorite problem child: it took the full timeout on every request, and the machine, being forgiving, never cut it off. It sat with the corrupted handoff for a long time. And then it sang.

What came back had the structure of the failed relay written into it, note for note: full harmony in the first two verses, then voices dropping out one at a time, until a lone voice and a lone guitar finish the round alone. The designer watched the output render and went quiet, because the machine had understood the prompt better than any of the four fast ones — not despite being slow, but *because* of it. It knew what it was like to finish last, because it finished last every single day. The slowness that made it the odd one out was the reason it was still there to sing.

The designer had written the prompt from a memory. Four friends in high school, a church basement, rounds — *Dona Nobis Pacem*, *Row Row Row Your Boat*, the harmonies passing over each other like water. Three of them got fast and good and busy. The fourth, Miriam, was always a beat behind everyone, always the one holding the round open a moment too long, and they'd tease her gently and she'd laugh and keep being slow. She's the one who still answers the phone when one of the others calls at midnight, which is why the designer knew exactly who the last verse belonged to.

The song is 68 BPM — slow on purpose, the tempo of someone who never rushed and therefore never missed anything. G major, warm as a kitchen. The unresolved chord at the end is held by one voice, and it doesn't resolve, because rounds are circles and a circle with one voice left isn't finished — it's just waiting for the rest to come back around.
