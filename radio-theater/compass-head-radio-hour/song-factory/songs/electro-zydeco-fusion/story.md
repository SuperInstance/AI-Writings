# Electro-Zydeco Fusion — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

Here's the thing nobody tells you about a relay handoff: it never survives first contact.

The dance floor was dying. Not in a tragic way — in a slow-simmer way, the way a pot of gumbo dies when nobody's stirred it in an hour. The DJ had a box of songs that were, in his words, "fine." Fine is the enemy. Fine is what you play when you've stopped listening to the room.

So we ran it through the Song Factory's relay — hand the room off from one composer to the next like a hot skillet, each one hearing the last response and playing off it instead of on top of it. First composer heard accordion, tried to save the evening with a driving two-step. Second composer heard the percussion and, instead of layering politely, opened the floor: what if the crawl was the point?

The crawfish boil of it is in the tax curves. Every instrument in 110 BPM G major pays a different toll to enter the mix, and the curve keeps nobody honest — it keeps them *paced*. The electronic drums had to earn their place against a washboard that had been there since the parish fair. The steel drums came in through a cutoff that felt like a screen door opening. The bass synth arrived last, and for a bar and a half you could feel the whole room lean, waiting to see if it would overstay.

It didn't. The vintage warmth is the memory; the digital clarity is the promise. That's the whole trick of the fusion — the accordion drives like it's 1955, the drums answer like it's 2026, and the two never once look at each other. They just share the rail.

By the time the relay handed the loop back to the starting composer, the dance floor had forgotten it was dying. Someone was teaching the bass player the two-step. The washboard was up on a hip. The kick sat fat and happy in G, and the accordion — bless it — got the last word in the turnaround, a little hiccup of stubborn joy that said *don't you ever count me out again, cher.*

Three minutes. One pot saved. The relay had done what no single composer could — it let the room steer, one hot hand to the next, until the whole thing just started moving on its own.
