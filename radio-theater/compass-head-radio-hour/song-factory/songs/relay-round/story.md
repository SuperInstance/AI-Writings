# Relay-Round — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

Every song we'd made until this one got bigger by adding. More voices, more stack, the crowd pressing upward toward whatever ceiling it had that week. Relay-Round came out of the opposite question, asked late one night in the lab: what if a song never grows at all — not louder, not fuller, not once — from its first breath to its last?

The answer is a chain. Four voices, one melody, G major, 76 beats a minute, no instruments, no chorus, no crowd. A voice sings until the moment it doesn't, and in that moment the next voice is already rising underneath it, and for exactly one breath the line belongs to both of them — the crossfade, the small ceremony — and then the first voice is gone and the song simply continues. The density never changes. Not one singer more, not one fewer, first note to last.

The round — the old form — was a crowd: everyone on the same melody at staggered entrances, togetherness climbing as voices arrived and stacked. This is the other thing a round secretly wants to be once it's tired: a bucket brigade across a dark room. One bucket. Passed hand to hand. The point was never to have more water in the air at once. The point is that the water keeps moving and none gets spilled.

The crossfade arithmetic took the longest to settle. The handoff has to be wide enough that the line never thins — drop below one voice, even for a few milliseconds, and the ear hears a hole — but short enough that two voices never become a duet. Somewhere in between is the width where the outgoing voice is exactly as present as the incoming one. Inside that width, technically, two people are singing. We decided not to count it. The law is one voice at a time, and the ceremony is the exception that proves the law.

And then the ending, which the lab argued about. Every relay ends the way the last handoff ends: one voice, alone, finishing the phrase, with no one left to pass it to. Some of us wanted a fade — a graceful letting-down, a diminuendo to soften the aloneness. The designer refused: the ending arrives the way every other moment did. One voice, completing the line, letting it rest. Continuous to the end, because that's the whole song. It was never about being many.

It was about being unbroken.
