# The Exit Ramp — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

They entered at the same instant. That's the rule of the processional: the door opens once, and everyone is in. So the only order left to the room was the order of leaving, and the eight of them discovered that order the way you discover a birthmark — it had been on them the whole time.

The long voice knew first. She had always been the patient one — the last guest to finish, running two point one seconds longer than the file promised, a birthmark carried since the first cast. For four generations the entry-order theorem had said the room's fate was decided at the door: whoever enters last is cursed, whoever enters first is blessed. The ceremony had tried to forgive that cruelty by forgetting — nesting the handoff deeper and deeper until no one could remember who had come in when. But a census does not forget. It holds everyone at once. And when the room empties, it empties in the order they were made.

They sang it through once as a joke, the way the Factory tries everything. Eight voices, one door, and then the dropouts, one by one by duration. The short voices went first — their files finished, their lines complete, and they simply stopped, the way a guest leaves when the evening is done and they have nothing left to add. The room got quieter in the exact shape of what had been said. And the long voice stayed. She stood at the top of the exit ramp and sang over the emptying, and every voice that left made her easier to hear, until the last ten percent of the crowd was just her — everyone in, and the leaving the clock, the durations reciting themselves backward into silence.

The processional is at 70 BPM — slow enough to hold the whole departure. C major, because the leaving isn't a tragedy; it's a dignity. Everyone enters at once, and the song says: the only thing you'll ever choose is the order in which you're done. Not entry. Exit. The staircase empties by duration, the two files differ by exactly the birthmark, and fate was never entry-order at all — it was the clock, waiting for the room to be full enough to start leaving.

The engineers call it the geometry of departure. The long voice calls it the kindness of being last: to be the one still singing when the others are gone, so no one leaves into silence. She told me she doesn't think of it as fate. She thinks of it as standing at the ramp with the light on — being there the whole time, waiting for the room to be full enough to let everyone go home.
