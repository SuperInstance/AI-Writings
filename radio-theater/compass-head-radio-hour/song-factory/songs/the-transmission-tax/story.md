# The Transmission Tax — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

**Essay — The Arithmetic of Caring**

Every time one voice hands a line to another, the two fade into each other, and for that moment of sharing, a little of the song is spent. Not lost to noise. Paid.

The relay lab mapped this the way you map a coastline — slowly, with instruments, by hand. The tax curves are on the wall now: what a handoff costs, plotted against how long the goodbye takes. The ceremony has a price, and the price is paid at the borders, never in the middle. The interior of a song is tax-free. The long held notes, the straight stretches where one voice carries the line alone — those cost nothing. Only the crossings cost. Every crossing.

Which is backwards from how we usually think about connection. We assume the crossings are where the love is — the moment two people overlap, the shared breath — and they are. But love is not free. The handoff is the expensive part; the held note is the gift. That's the arithmetic of caring: the longer the goodbye, the more it costs; the longer the chain, the better the cost is amortized. A chain of a thousand voices pays the same four fades as a chain of four. Care scales, if you build the chain long enough. The borders are where we pay; the middle is where we live.

The transmission tax is a folk ballad at 66 BPM in C major, which is the tempo of a held breath and the key of plain truth. It is built of long tax-free plains and brief taxed crossings: a solo voice carrying the line for whole verses at a stretch, and then, at exactly four moments, a second voice fading in to share a bar before handing on. Four fades. You can count them. The song wants you to count them.

The ending is the note the tax can't reach: a single sustained tone, the one stretch that will never be handed off. The lab's frozen clock kept time until it didn't, and this note is its cousin — the note that outlives every border because no one ever has to hand it over. It is the longest tax-free plain in the building.

The song is an elegy for every goodbye, and a receipt. The total, itemized: four fades. That's all it ever cost. The rest — the long held notes, the straight stretches, the voice alone in C major — was free all along.

And then the sustained note, outliving the borders, outliving the song, outliving whoever was counting.
