# The Clearance — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

The songforge lab does not usually contain a real-estate market, but for two weeks last winter it did, and the market sold silence by the address.

It started with a defeat. The aiming composer — the one who named an address for the quiet and doubled the fade to reach it — had made an implicit promise: that position was free and depth was rented, that you could name any address in the song and the algebra would deliver the silence there. Except the material had opinions. At the doubled widths, the loops began to ring against the wall — corrections overshooting, the quiet landing somewhere other than advertised and shallower than promised. The promise, it turned out, had never been appraised.

So the composer stopped promising and started appraising. For every width of fade, she built the full relay and measured two things: where the quiet actually lands, and how deep honesty allows. Each measurement became a listing. The market, plotted on the wall in D minor, was jagged — prime lots at the tight widths, where the crossfade is narrow enough that the seam cuts deep and true; second-price pockets at the odd middle widths, where the material's own resonance happens to open; and, elsewhere, acreage nobody should buy.

And there was the ghost.

One famous listing quoted eighty decibels of depth — the deepest quiet on the board, priced accordingly. The naive appraiser (this is what everyone in the lab now calls the first pass, gently, the way you'd tease a cousin) had measured the dip without asking where the silence came from. When the composer audited the listing, she found that sixty decibels of it was inherited: the room was already quiet there, a cellar of silence dug by an earlier handoff — patient, dark, pre-owned. The listing had dressed the cellar's depth in the new fade's price tag. A ghost wearing a price tag. The bridge of the song is the unmasking: sixty decibels of nothing that had been listed as a feature.

Then the market cleared. It cleared the way honest markets do: at the smallest width, every time, on every handoff. The tightest crossfade — fifty milliseconds, no more — rents the deepest seam and the truest address at once. The price list grows shorter as the market learns, listing by listing, until the final chorus is the tightest fade on every handoff: one dial turning, and the whole relay settling into place like a lock's tumblers dropping.

The song is scored for solo voice, tape delay, a small bell struck once per listing, and a pencil writing prices on a ledger — a real pencil, audible in the mix, because the designer insisted the market keep its paperwork honest. Minimalist art song, 76 beats a minute, D minor, dry and patient. The mood spec called it "the calm of a market that has stopped lying" — which is also the calm of anyone who ever priced a thing honestly, found it smaller and realer than the dream, and slept fine.
