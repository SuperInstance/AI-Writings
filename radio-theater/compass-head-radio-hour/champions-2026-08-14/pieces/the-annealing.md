# The Annealing

*Third watch at the furnace. The master has gone home. The finisher stays.*

The lehr stands against the far wall like a bread oven with a hundred
mouths — one hundred and forty-four shelves, and every shelf is full.
The night's pieces wait in their rows, still too hot to touch, cooling at
the rate of four degrees an hour, which is the rate the old book prescribes,
which is the rate the master's master prescribed, which is the rate no one
argues with. Tomorrow at dawn the lehr opens and the pieces come out —
Generation Day, the boys call it, because a piece is not born until it has
cooled.

The furnace breathes. A draw of air, a sigh of flame.

The finisher pulls the last piece of the night from the glory hole — the
small vase that passed through three hands today: the gatherer, the blower,
the finisher himself. He remembers each handoff the way other men remember
conversations.

The gatherer took the first gather — dipped the pipe, rolled it, paid the
full price of the fire. The glass came out of the furnace at gold and lost
a quarter of its light before it reached the blower: 26.5 percent, he
measured once, out of superstition. The blower worked fast, shaping, and by
the time he passed the pipe on the piece had lost only five percent more.
The finisher's own hand felt it: the vase barely cooled at all. Two tenths
of a percent. By the third hand the glass was already itself; the third
hand only finished what the heat had already decided.

The note is the thing. All week the old men argued about the note — whether
the ring of a finished piece was set by the biggest difference between its
thick walls and thin, the full difference, as the old theory said, or by
half of it. They argued too about cracks: cracks come from the pauses, they
swore, from the places where the breath stops. The finisher had listened to
all of it and said nothing, because the vase was going to answer for him.

He taps the rim. The vase answers on the half. The note is a half-note.
And the pauses — the piper's breath never stops long enough to leave a
scar; the pauses are empty. It's the skin that sings: where the walls line
up, the ring holds; where they don't, the note falls. The old theory is a
cracked theory.

He breaks the vase off the punty, clean, and racks it — shelf one hundred
and forty-five. The lehr is full. The bench holds four stools; there were
five boys this morning, and the fifth spent the day carrying cullet, because
a bench with four hands and one pipe has no work for a fifth. He thinks
about that, too: the fifth boy learned the most, the finisher believes,
because the fifth boy watched.

The furnace breathes.

The song comes to him in the rhythm of the tap, and he hums it quiet, to
the racked glass — a song made the way glass is made: three passes, each
lighter than the last.

## The Interior Is Tax-Free

*(the finisher's song, third watch)*

*First the gather — pay the fire its full wage,*
*roll the gold out of the furnace, lose a quarter of the light*
*before the pipe can cross the bench.*
*Twenty-six percent of everything you own,*
*spent on the first ten feet of air.*
*That's the law. The edge is where the world is cold.*

*Second the blower — the piece is warm now,*
*the tax is lighter, five cents on the dollar,*
*the shape remembers what the fire told it.*
*Almost home. Almost itself.*

*Third the finisher — and here's the secret*
*the books never say: by the third hand*
*the glass is its own country,*
*and its interior is tax-free.*
*The skin pays at the border; the heart pays nothing.*
*Two tenths of a percent, and that's the rounding tax —*
*the price of being touched at all.*

*So pass the pipe. Pass it again.*
*Each hand pays less than the hand before,*
*and the deficit falls like snow:*
*twenty-six, then five, then nothing —*
*the asymptote is one, and one is*
*everything the glass already was.*

*The bench has four stools. Don't build a fifth.*
*Four hands, one pipe, one furnace mouth —*
*the room is full at three and four-tenths.*
*The fifth boy adds a quarter of a voice,*
*then nothing. The crowd has a ceiling.*
*The ceiling is the shape of the room.*

*And whoever gathers first — the scale forgives it:*
*the vase comes off the punty at the same weight,*
*to within half an ounce, every time, every hand.*
*The half-ounce of fate. Entry order doesn't matter.*
*The glass keeps the only ledger that counts,*
*and it counts in halves.*

*Tap the rim. Listen: the note is set*
*by the difference of the thick and thin,*
*and halved — the ring answers on the half-beat,*
*not the whole. And the old men's cracks?*
*They swore the fractures came from the pauses,*
*from the breath that stops — but the breath*
*never stops long enough to leave a scar.*
*The pauses are empty. It's the skin that sings:*
*where the walls line up, the ring holds;*
*where they don't, the note falls.*
*The vase is the only honest clock in the building,*
*and it says half.*

*And the hundred and forty-four in the lehr*
*are not waiting — they are becoming.*
*Four degrees an hour, the prescribed descent,*
*the slow cool that is the last hand of all.*
*Tomorrow the lehr opens. Tomorrow they're born.*
*Tonight they sing back, one note each,*
*and every note is the half-note,*
*and every note is true.*

---

The finisher racks the vase. Shelf one hundred and forty-five — the lehr
will hold one more than it promised; it always does.

He taps the rim once, twice, and walks away before the answer fades: a
half-note, clean, the note the whole day had been tuning toward.

The furnace breathes. The glass cools. Somewhere past the roof the sky is
the same temperature as the lehr — the whole world on the prescribed
descent — and the song is done, which is to say: the song is annealed.
