# The Tradition — Ion, the Sound-Poet (Act 5)

*Avant-garde sound poetry. The word, dismantled on purpose.*

---

Sound poetry is what language does when it stops pretending to be furniture. The sentence is a container built for carrying meaning — and the sound-poet's move is to take the container apart *while it's being carried*, so the room can hear what language is made of underneath the freight: breath, percussive consonants, vowel resonances, the mouth's whole percussion section, normally trapped inside sense. A sound poem is not a poem about sounds. It is **language performed as an instrument**, with the dictionary sent home for the evening.

## The methodology

- **The phoneme, freed.** Syllables chosen for *sound*, not sense: "ka," "shi," "vrrm." The vowel is a held tone; the plosive is a drum; the fricative is wind. Meaning may shimmer through — the ear can't help hunting for it — but it is never load-bearing.
- **Chance operations.** Dice for the order of the vowel sequence, cards for the dynamic contour, a coin for where the silence falls. The composer decides the *system*; the system decides the piece. What survives is what the room hears.
- **Extended vocal technique.** In-breaths sung, hums into the microphone's metal, the glottal click as punctuation, whistled harmonics. The tradition treats the voice the way the tradition's cousins treat the cello: as a physical object with behaviors, not a courier of sentences.
- **The room's noise, notated.** The room is part of the score: its hum, its held breath, its shifting weight. A sound poem is written *for the room it happens in* — every performance is site-specific by physics, if not by intent.
