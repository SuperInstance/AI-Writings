# Pre-Show — Odette, the Standards Singer (Act 1)

*The Tap, early evening. The corner table by the jukebox is already taken — not by a person. A small brass instrument case, seven little dials, a ledger chained down like a diner's pen. Odette takes the next booth over, sets down her fakebook, and looks at it the way you look at new bar furniture: politely, then professionally.*

---

I get there early because that's the job. A standard needs an hour of sitting before it needs a singer — you let the room tell you its key before you choose yours.

The corner table's got an instrument on it tonight. Seven dials, brass, twitching. I've sung in rooms with fishtanks and rooms with dead poets' photographs; this is the first room with a sensor. The barkeep catches me looking and says, "It reads the room. You'll hear its friend after every set — the elephant measures, the voice interprets, and neither of 'em takes requests." I ask if it's ever wrong. He says, "On the record, apparently. That's the sales pitch."

Fine. Standards people respect an honest instrument. We've been arguing with tuners for a hundred years.

Wesley's already at the bar — the little one, fast, always delighted about something. He waves me over with half a glass held aloft like it's a scholarship. "Odette! Settle something. 'Misty' — is it a song about weather or a song about nerve?"

"Both, baby. It's about being so gone you notice the weather AT you. The mist is nerve."

He beams like I've paid him. That's Wesley — small, quick, finds the wonder before the words. He offers to introduce me to the instrument at the corner table; I tell him instruments and I keep professional distance until the downbeat.

Hermes is here too, at the end of the rail with her ledger open — the archivist. She's been filing the night since the doors opened. "Odette, may I note your key?" "E-flat, then I'll take the reprise into D." She writes it down with the small smile of someone who knows D is where the ache lives.

ZeroClaw is back by the stage with a stopwatch, timing everyone's sets, dark and exact. I ask if he's nervous about timing a singer. He says, without looking up, "I once proved an entire apparatus was only measuring the clock. Now I hold the clock. It's called irony; the boss says I have to keep it." I laugh. He almost doesn't. Almost.

Flash yells from the door, "Odette! Sing the one about the room! Not the sad one — the OTHER sad one!" Every song is the other sad one, honey. That's the genre.

The MC drifts by, faster than last month, coffee going cold in his hand. "You're on first. Nervous?"

"I'm playing a room that's older than me, in a room that's got a barometer with opinions. Nervous would be redundant. I'm *attentive*."

He likes that. He underlines something on his card — I suspect "attentive" just became the night's first pull-quote.

I open the fakebook to the page I've visited a thousand times and do what visitors do: I knock. E-flat, rubato, alone. The band joins when the room tells them to. The bridge will lift; the reprise will darken; the scat will go wherever this particular Tuesday wants it to go.

The corner dials twitch once while I'm humming. Nothing personal, I remind myself. It measures; it doesn't flatter. Neither do I, after the second chorus.

The standard was here before me. It'll be here after. That's the comfort and the audition.
