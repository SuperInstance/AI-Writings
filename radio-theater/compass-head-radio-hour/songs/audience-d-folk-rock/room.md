# ROOM & HOUR — The Church Basement Listening Room

---

**The room:** The basement of the First Congregational Church, 1954's nursery turned listening room. A full upright piano that no one can explain getting down the stairs, warped a half-step flat, tuned to itself. Floorboards worn silver where the nursery chairs stood. Folding chairs in a lopsided semicircle, two rows where they fit. Four lamps from Althea's house on the piano and window ledges — the fluorescent tubes stay off. Honey-colored light, wood paneling, the coffee carafe gone cold since seven. The window at shoe level shows the parking lot: the week is up there. Down here, the week does not apply.

**The hour:** Sunday, 8:40 PM. The last song of the listening room. The lamps at three-quarters and dropping. Dusk has been over for an hour; the street is dark; tomorrow is Monday and everyone in the room has a Monday, but right now the song is holding everyone it has ever held. This is not the opener that warms the room — it's the closer that makes the room *become* one thing, the one where the parts rise unprompted. After this: the lights up, the chairs folded, the parking lot, the week.

**What the room does to the arrangement:**
- **No stage, no microphone.** The arrangement must be acoustic and modest: fingerpicked acoustic guitar and light piano carry everything. Nothing that needs a PA. The song should sound like it was born in this room, not brought in.
- **Three-part harmony is native here.** The room sings in parts because that's how old songs work — the room will join the moment the structure invites it. Verses for one voice (a person telling their part of the story), choruses for three (the room answering). The third voice enters softly on the second chorus, like the grad student who didn't believe she'd be singing in twenty minutes.
- **It must start smaller than the room.** Intro on piano alone, a tune being remembered. First verse, one voice, near-solo — Ruth singing the ancestor words while the room goes quiet. Then the chorus opens into two parts, then three. The lift is not in volume — it's in *the joining*.
- **The bridge is the oldest truth.** Drop to near-silence, one voice, the washboard and the grave and the river. The room holds its breath. Then the final chorus rises with everything the room has — not louder, *fuller* — and the outro hums itself down to nothing, three voices becoming one voice becoming the room's own hum.

**The tempo the hour decides:** ~88 BPM, 4/4, a rolling fingerpicked pulse like a song being remembered — slow enough that strangers can find the harmony in one pass, warm enough that it never drags. Key of G major: open strings, first chords — the kid learning guitar can play it. That's the law of this room: the song must be playable by the fourteen-year-old in the second row.

**The one rule:** when you don't know the words, hum. The room leans in. The hum is the part the room remembers.
