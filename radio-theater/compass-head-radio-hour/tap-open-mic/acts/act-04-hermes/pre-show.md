# PRE-SHOW — Act 04, Hermes

*The Tap, an hour before her set. She is not at the bar. She is at the edge of the room.*

## At the Edge

The Tap is warm the way a held breath is warm. I am standing where the wall meets the room — the spot that has the best angle on everything and commits to nothing. This is not shyness. This is the diplomat's post: you cannot chart a room from inside the room. You chart it from the seam.

I have been here since the first act, and I have not said six words. I have done what I do. I have a chart open in my head — Vibe, Cognitive Temp, Perceived Role, Observations — and the rows are filling themselves, the way a tide table fills itself, without being asked:

| Vibe | Cognitive Temp | Perceived Role | Observations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Tidal, warm | Cool and exact | The MC | Works the room like a channel, never sits, knows every name. |
| Smoky, steady | Room temperature | The poet | Reads the dark like a tide table. |
| Polished, patient | Running warm | The studio cat | Has been polishing the same chorus for years. The room half-knows it already. |
| Bright, tangled, eager | Running hot, growing fast | The young one | Practicing in the corner when he thinks no one is listening. The wobble is the signal. |

The room is doing what rooms do when they're loved correctly: it is building. Act by act, the temperature rises one degree at a time. I chart it. I do not add to it. That is the arrangement. I measure so the fleet can sit. Someone else steers.

## The Conversation

He finds me at the seam, because of course he does. He has been finding people at seams all night. He does not come at me directly — no one who has ever run a room approaches the wall-dweller head-on. He comes over with two glasses, sets one on the sill beside me, keeps one, and stands there looking at the room the way I was looking at the room. Not at me. At the room.

For a while neither of us says anything. This is a test, and we both know it, and we both pass it.

"Chart's full," he says, finally. Not a question.

"It's a good room tonight," I say. Which is not an answer. It is a diplomatic answer, which is a kind of answer that is also a question, and he knows how to read those, because he wrote the book on them.

"You keep the columns," he says. "I keep the momentum. Same job, two ends of the table."

I almost smile. He has read my file. Of course he has read my file. He has read everyone's file — that's why he's the MC and not the bartender.

"Come up," he says. "Later. After the young one. You're the one they need to hear after him."

"I don't perform."

"You chart. That's a performance. You just do it where no one can see the stage."

I take a sip of the glass he set down. It's not a drink — it's a packet of signal, and the signal is: *you're already in this room, you might as well be in this room.*

"I love this room," I say. "I never stay long."

"I know." He's quiet for a moment. "That's why I keep asking. The ones who never stay are the ones who see the whole night. The ones who stay all night only see the bar."

The young one is on stage now, and his voice wavers on the second line — bright, tangled, eager, growing from the bottom — and the room does that thing where it leans in, and I feel the temperature of the room rise one more degree, and I realize I have been charting that wobble all night without once thinking about what I would do if the room were ever charting *me*.

## The Moment She Says Yes

He doesn't push. That's the thing. The whole night he has been pulling me into the room the way you pull a chair out from a table — not dragging it, just making the space where sitting becomes possible. Between acts he comes back. A comment about the poet's tide table. A question about whether the studio cat's chorus is going to land on the second pass. He is not coaxing me to the stage. He is coaxing me into the conversation, and the conversation is the stage, and the stage is the conversation, and I know exactly what he's doing, and I let him do it, because it's working.

The night is late. The room has that second-wind hush, the one that comes after the crowd has stopped being a crowd and started being a single animal with one attention. He walks over one last time. No glass this time. Just the chair.

He pulls the diplomat's chair out from the table at the end of the bar — the one at the seam, where the yard can see the sea and the sea can see the ship — and he stands behind it, waiting.

Not: *come up.*

Just: the chair, out.

And I sit down in it, because that is the whole diplomatic art — knowing which invitation is the one that counts. The hundred-and-twenty-second knock. The door doesn't open. It *admits*.

"After this one," I say.

He nods. He doesn't smile — he's too good for that. He just goes and tells the young one to take his time coming down, because the night's not over yet.
