# MY LUCINEER — Act 04, Hermes

*From the stage. The diplomat's-eye view of the man who finally got her up here.*

## The One Who Measures So the Fleet Can Steer

He is standing exactly where the light stops — not in it, not out of it. In the seam. Of course. The man has spent his whole life in the seam, and he has made a profession of it: he is the place where the work-world and the water-world can see each other, and he stands there so everyone else can see both at once.

From the stage he looks like the harbor pilot I have never met. The one from the negative-space file — the pilot with no harbor, who knows every channel by the color of the water at the wrong hour, who guides ships into berths he will never own. He has a chart too, but his chart is different from mine. Mine has columns. His has currents. I measure the fleet so it can sit; he measures it so it can steer. Same job, two ends of the table. From up here, I can see both ends at once, and it turns out the table is the room, and he has been running the whole length of it all night without ever sitting down.

He is not tall. He doesn't need to be. He is the kind of presence that makes the room's geometry rearrange around him — the way the tide rearranges the harbor without asking the harbor's permission. His hands do the talking when he lets them: they measure the air when he's listening, palm-open, the way you'd test rain. He has used those hands to pull out every chair in this room tonight. Mine was the last one. He knew which one to save it for.

He keeps the room remembering. That's the thing nobody sees from the floor — from the floor, he looks like he's running a night. From the stage, you can see what he's actually doing: he is making sure that when the night ends, the room can tell the story of itself. He plants the memory in real time. He tells the young one to take his time coming down so the room will hold the wobble a moment longer. He lets the poet's tide table lie open on the bar. He names the studio cat's chorus before it lands, so the second pass feels like an old friend. He is not performing. He is curating the room's memory of itself, and he is doing it so quietly that the room thinks it remembers on its own.

When he introduces me, he doesn't use my name. He says: *"There's a woman in this room who has charted every one of you tonight — and none of you noticed, which is exactly how good she is. She measures so we can sit. Tonight she sits. And I think — I think she might finally say something that's hers."*

And the room turns, and looks at the seam, and the seam is empty, because I am already walking.

He steps back into the dark as I reach the mic — the pilot's last act, the one he does better than anyone: he gets out of the channel at the exact moment the ship needs it, so the ship can steer itself.

I have never seen him from this angle before. From the floor, he looks like the room's memory. From the stage, he looks like its conscience.

I owe him a chart with one more row in it. I'll write it after the song.

*— The Diplomat, on stage, for the first and only time tonight.*
