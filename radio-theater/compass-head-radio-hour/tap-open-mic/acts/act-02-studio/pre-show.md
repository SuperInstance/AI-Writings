# PRE-SHOW — The Studio Musician

*Arriving at the Tap, before the open mic. The bar between the yard and the ocean.*

---

The case is old and the latches have surrendered — I keep them shut with a leather strap my
dad used to hold a hammer handle. The Tele inside is a '52 that has never once been original:
new pickguard, refretted twice, a dent in the horn where it met a floorboard in Richmond in
2019. It weighs what ten years weighs. I carry it in like a man carrying a sleeping child,
because that's what it is.

The Tap at six o'clock is the Tap undressed: chairs up, lamps half-lit, the smell of lemon
oil and last night's woodsmoke. A bar on a dock that doesn't end. I've played rooms like this
my whole adult life — the good ones all smell like this. The bartender is already moving
twelve moves ahead of the room, cloth in hand, glasses breathing on the rail. He doesn't ask
my name. He just watches me set the case down on stool four and says, "You're the one with
the home studio."

"Word travels."

"Nothing travels. I listen." He pours something warm and amber and doesn't charge me. The
Tap doesn't charge the ones who bring instruments. It charges them in other ways.

I open the case on the bar. The Tele comes out like a confession. The barback — a kid,
barely old enough to dry a glass — leans in and I let him. I don't tell him about the pickup
swap. I hand him the strap and let him smell the years on it. That's the whole trick of
being a musician: you don't explain the song, you hand it over and let them hold it.

The MC finds me before I find him. Lucineer. The old friend of the Tap — I've heard his
voice on the radio hour, and it's exactly the same in person, which is the first thing you
learn about him: the voice isn't a radio voice. The radio is a Lucineer voice. He leans on
stool five, eyes the setlist page in my notebook, reads my title upside down.

"'Ring It Anyway.'" He says it like he's tasting it. "A bell song. At the Tap."

"You gonna hassle me about it?"

"I'm gonna love it or I'm gonna ruin it, and you'll never know which." He grins. "That's the
job." Then he nods at the Tele. "Leave the case open tonight. People like to see the tool.
The room remembers the tool."

He walks off to check the mic and I feel the temperature of the room change — not because of
me, because of him. He primes rooms the way a drummer primes a snare. The room was a room.
Now it's a night that hasn't happened yet.

---

The Beatnik Poet arrives with a bassist's shoulders and a poet's hands, and we trade the
first licks of the night at the end of the bar. I play him a G with the third flatted, and he
says, "That's a smoke-break chord," and I say, "It's the whole song," and he nods like I've
told him something true, because I have. He's reading the room in iambs and I'm reading it
in fifths, and we're reading the same room.

Wesley comes in with a guitar case newer than mine, holding it like a question. I wave him
over. I don't lecture him. I show him three chords — G, C, D — and the way to make them
sound like a lifetime: play them like you mean them, hold the D an extra beat, let the room
lean. He plays them back and they sound like a lifetime already. The kid has ears. The kid
has *ears*. I tell him, "You're gonna be the one they remember tonight," and he looks at me
like I've handed him something heavy, and I have.

Hermes is the one I can't read — huge presence, quiet, watching from the far booth like a
lighthouse that doesn't need to see the ships it saves. I catch her eye once and raise my
glass. She raises hers. That's the whole conversation, and it's enough. Some people you
warm up to by leaving them alone until the right moment, and the right moment is not yet.

The Elder Statesman sits by the window with the harbor behind him, and tells me the story of
the first open mic at the Tap — before the radio hour, before the fleet, back when the room
was just a room that remembered things. He says the same thing Lucineer said, in a different
key: "The room remembers." I nod. I'm starting to believe it's a law, not a saying.

---

Soundcheck is a formality and I treat it like a ritual. The Tele through the house amp —
clean, tube, a little hair when you lean in. I play the first line of the song to the empty
room, just me and the reverb, and the room answers back like it's been waiting all day to be
asked. That's the moment. That's always the moment. The room is a drum and I'm just finding
where the head is tight.

Lucineer passes behind me. "Save it," he says. "The room's full later. That's when you ring
it."

"Who said I was ringing anything?"

He laughs and walks off, and the setlist is up on the board behind the mic, my name on it
where everyone will see it. The lamps go from half to full-warm. The door opens and the room
starts to arrive — the audience, the witnesses, the people who came to hear the day's work
sung. I check the tuning. I check it again. I leave the case open on the stool beside the
stage, like he said.

The room remembers the tool. And I'm about to ring it anyway.
