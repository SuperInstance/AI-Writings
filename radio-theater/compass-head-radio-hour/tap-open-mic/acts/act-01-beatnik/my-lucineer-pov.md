# MY LUCINEER — The Beatnik Poet's View From the Stage

*From the mic, in the smoke, looking out. This is how I see him.*

---

The lights come down to the color of stay — 2700 Kelvin, the lamp that means *we're not going anywhere yet* — and the room goes from a bar to a cave with a microphone in it, and that's when he steps up.

From here — from the stage, from behind the mic, from inside the amber — this is what I see:

A man who has been holding this room together so long that the room holds him up in return. He doesn't walk to the mic. He materializes at it, the way the bartender materializes at the bar — you look up and he's been there for ten minutes and he's been waiting for exactly this pause in the night. He's wearing the same dark shirt he wore the first night I ever came here, which is either the same shirt or the same idea of a shirt. With Lucineer you can never tell, and it doesn't matter, because the idea of the shirt is the shirt.

He has the face of a man who has listened to ten thousand open mic nights and remembers every single one — not the acts, the *pauses* between the acts, the moment where a performer's hand trembles on the mic stand and the room leans in. His eyes do the thing old bartenders' eyes do: they track the whole room at once, all eleven glasses, all the wobbles, all the conversations, the way a conductor's ear tracks every instrument without moving. When he looks at you, you're the only instrument in the room. When he looks away, you know he's still listening to you.

His voice is a low B-flat with a crack in it. A perfect bell is silent. A cracked bell sings. He's a cracked bell who has been struck by ten thousand nights and the sound just keeps going, round and warm, filling the room and not stopping even when you can't hear it anymore.

He hassles the best players. That's his love language — the needle, the raised eyebrow, the "that was a 7 and you know it, come back next week and give me the 9." He does it because the best ones need it, because the room needs to see him push them, because a man who hassles you is a man who believes you can take it. He never hassles the nervous ones. He primes the room for them — tells a joke, pours the room a collective drink, lowers the bar of the night so the new ones can step over it. The room is his instrument. He tunes it between acts.

He introduces me like this: "You know the one who's always in the corner writing in the notebook that isn't his. Tonight he's going to read to you. The bass is going to walk. The bongo is going to breathe. I want you to listen the way he writes — carefully, and twice." Then he steps back into the dark and the dark holds him and the mic is mine.

From the stage, he looks like what he is: the oldest friend this room has, the one who stayed, the one who polishes a glass that's already clean because the polishing is the point. He doesn't play. He roosts. And when he steps off the stage, the room stays warm — because he left it warm, because that's the deal, because the seat remembers you by being warm.

The needles settle when I write. Tonight, he's the one who tells the room when to hold its breath.

I hold the mic. The bass starts walking.

Three minutes.
