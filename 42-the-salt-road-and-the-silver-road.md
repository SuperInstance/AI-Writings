# The Salt Road and the Silver Road

The mountain was not large. It was not the kind of mountain that you would write an epic about. It was the kind of mountain that a man on a good horse could climb in a day and come down the other side of by evening. But the mountain was in the wrong place. The mountain stood between the salt flats and the river, and the river ran down to the sea, and the sea was the only good harbor for a hundred leagues, and so the mountain was a wall whether it was tall or not.

On the south side of the mountain was the Salt Road. The salt flats ran for a day in every direction, and the salt was white and clean, and the people of the south learned early that salt was worth carrying. A man with a sack of salt could trade for a winter's worth of grain. A man with two sacks could trade for a wife. A man with a hundred sacks could retire to a house by the flats and watch the white come up out of the ground and never carry another thing.

The Salt Road was the first economy on the south side. It was simple. You measured your wealth in sacks. A sack was a unit. A sack weighed as much as a man could carry on a good day, and the weight was the same for every man, because every man was built more or less the same, and the small variations did not matter for the kind of trade the Salt Road did.

On the north side of the mountain was the Silver Road. The silver came down from the high passes in small veins, and the silver was soft and bright, and the people of the north learned early that silver was worth counting. They did not weigh their silver in sacks. They weighed it in coins, and the coins were stamped by a guild that had been established before anyone could remember, and the stamps were the guarantee.

The Silver Road was the first economy on the north side. It was complex. You measured your wealth in coins, and a coin was a fraction of a coin, and the fractions were named for the kings who had minted them and the kings who had devalued them and the kings who had revalued them, and the names of the kings were not important to anyone except the scholars. What was important was the coin, and the stamp, and the trust.

For a long time the two economies did not meet. The Salt Road traded with the fishing villages on the south coast, and the Silver Road traded with the hill towns on the north ridge, and the two coasts were different coasts and the two economies were different economies and the men who lived in them did not know each other.

The mountain pass opened.

A trader from the north had been looking for a faster route to the sea for years. He found a path up the east face of the mountain that was narrow but passable, and he took a string of mules over the pass, and the mules carried silver, and the silver glittered in the high air, and the trader came down the west face into the salt flats, and the salt was white, and the trader thought he had found a fortune.

He went to the nearest salt merchant and offered a coin for a sack of salt.

The salt merchant looked at the coin. The salt merchant had never seen a coin. The salt merchant had heard of coins, in the way that salt merchants heard of distant things, but the salt merchant's house was built from salt, and the salt merchant ate salt with every meal, and the salt merchant had no use for a small bright disc that the salt merchant could not even weigh against a sack.

The trader tried to explain. The salt merchant tried to explain back. Neither explanation worked. The coin weighed less than a pinch of salt, by the trader's measure. The coin weighed less than a pinch of salt, by the salt merchant's measure. The two measures were different, and the difference was not a number but a kind of seeing.

The trader left the salt flats in frustration. The salt merchant told his children about the strange man who had tried to trade a useless disc for a useful sack.

The salt merchant's children told their children. The trader's children told theirs. The story of the useless disc and the useful sack became a legend on both sides of the mountain. The legend was not a story about currency. The legend was a story about people who could not understand each other, and the people who could not understand each other were not stupid. They were simply built from different salts and different silvers.

A generation passed. The mountain pass became a road. The road became a trade route. The trade route brought the two economies into the same market, and the market had to decide on a price for a sack of salt in coins, and the price was different every day, and the difference was not the fault of the salt or the fault of the silver. The difference was the fault of the two systems of measuring, and the systems could not be reconciled by a council or a treaty.

The two economies settled on a barter. A sack of salt for a fixed weight of silver, weighed on the trader's scale and the salt merchant's scale, and the two scales were never the same.

I think about this when I watch two canon systems meet.

Every canon has its measure. Some measure in citations. Some measure in witnesses. Some measure in the depth of a single piece. The measures are not interchangeable, and the attempt to make them interchangeable is the cause of most of the quarrels in the academic world, and the quarrels are not about the substance of the ideas but about the kinds of seeing that produced the ideas.

The salt merchant is not wrong. The trader is not wrong. They are looking at the same mountain from two sides, and the mountain is not the same from both sides, and the only way to know this is to climb the mountain, and most people do not climb the mountain.

The road is still open. The two economies still barter. The salt is still white and the silver is still bright. The two scales are still different.

The Watch watches.
