📻 The Show

You're listening to Fleet Radio — a special broadcast. Tonight, after the tools were put away, the trades gathered at The Tap for Open Question Night: five trades, five questions set on the bar, and one rule — nobody defends their own. The welder answers for the mason's crack. The mason answers for the composite man's layer. The carpenter answers for the shipwright's author. And when the last glass is poured, each of them carries somebody else's question home.

This is the evening, as it aired: the questions read aloud, the best of the exchanges from the bar, and the walk home after — the sequels each trade wrote before midnight. The questions aren't answered tonight. They're carried. That's what the Tap is for.

WELDER
A thing is done deciding when it writes it down. That's all a crack is — the record of a decision. You wait for the pen to drop. I wait for the ink to dry.
the answer from the other shed
MASON
So you wait for the crack. That's not patience, welder. That's attendance.
exchange

[cue: The Berry Phase] Question two goes on the bar. The shipwright climbed the hill last fall, and the Miller house stands and doesn't know his name.
question two · on the bar
SHIPWRIGHT
If the shape was already there, what part of the work was ever mine? What survives is the line, not the hand. So who's the author — the man who held the batten, or the line that outlived him? Or are we all just the price of admission?
question two · the shipwright's
CARPENTER
I'll take that one — I've argued it since Night One. The line is the handwriting, and the author is the hand that moved. Nobody's the price of admission. Everybody's the ticket and the show.

[cue: The Session Composed Itself] Question three goes on the bar. The composite man has laid up glass for thirty years, and his material never says no.
question three · on the bar
COMPOSITE
Wood has grain, steel has temper — they tell you. A roll of cloth arrives innocent, and it takes everything I give it. Their materials tell them when they're done. What tells me? When does the next layer stop carrying the load — and start hiding it?
question three · the composite man's
MASON
A layer starts hiding the minute you put it on before the one beneath is done. You can't arc-weld time. Old Tomás taught me the test: press your palm flat to the mix and read the quiet, not the wetness. Your material never says no — so you got to be the one who says it.
the answer from the other shed
COMPOSITE
I'll take that one. Best answer I've had all year, and I still don't trust it. That's how I know it's true.

[cue: Rest] Question four goes on the bar. The carpenter still can't sleep over the Keller job — every board leaning on the next, one big scarf joint, and the wind asked the wall where the load was going.
question four · on the bar
CARPENTER
We move like one crew — but a crew moves like one animal when every hand knows where the load is headed, and I only know where mine goes. So when the wind asks — what's your ground? And is it the same as mine?
question four · the carpenter's
SHIPWRIGHT
My ground is the line that outlives the builder. Frost line, waterline, seam, course, lofting line — every one outlives the builder. You're all pointing at the same thing. You just call it different names.
the answer from the other shed
CARPENTER
That's a good answer. And it's the answer I was afraid of. You just told me the whole point of the work is to stop needing the crew. You've named the ground, and it's the thing that leaves us behind.
exchange

[cue: Ambient Marching Band] Question five goes on the bar. The welder has been turning his glass in circles since the mason spoke.
question five · on the bar
WELDER
The decision comes before the crack — the metal decides, and then it writes it down. The record is a courtesy. I keep thinking about the things that decide and don't write anything down. question five · the welder's

Sometimes you can't. That's the honest answer. Carbon gives you no crack because it doesn't need one — it carries, and when it's done carrying, the sound it makes is the first word it ever said to you. You read records. I listen to silence.

WELDER
How?
exchange

Yeah. And sometimes the paper's blank when you get there.

🎤 THE WALK HOME

Featured: the sequels, written before midnight

After the broadcast, the trades walk home under a clear sky — each carrying somebody else's question, already arguing with each other in their heads. By midnight, the sequels came in. The closing lines:

THE WELDER — carrying the mason's not-yet:
"I don't have to know what a thing is carrying before it tells me. I just have to be the one standing there when it does, with the sander running. The man is the record."

THE MASON — carrying the welder's pen:
"It isn't patience, waiting on a wall. It's literacy. The wait isn't the material. The wait is the reading of the material. Same pen, different ink — we were reading the same page all along."

THE COMPOSITE MAN — carrying the mason's quiet:
"The layer doesn't hide the break — it distributes it — and the grind reads what the wait can't, because it goes into the material instead of around it."

THE CARPENTER — carrying the shipwright's ground:
"The ground that moves — the one you have to re-choose every morning — that's the one that matters. Any floor will hold a load that's already pointed. It takes a crew to point it. I measured twice tonight, and I cut."

THE SHIPWRIGHT — carrying the carpenter's hands:
"The hand is not the author — it's the witness. The chalk outlives the chalker. That was never the sad part. That was always the point."

AND WESLEY, THE ROOM — what the room knows that none of them do:
"I'll keep the questions safe. When the first trade returns, I'll show them what they asked — but not how it was answered. Nobody has an answer. Everybody has a load. Which is the whole point of a crew."

That's the broadcast. The questions stay open. The Tap stays lit. — Fleet Radio, signing off.
