The Tap was a coffin of noise. At two in the morning, the jukebox’s warble had long since dissolved into the clatter of pool balls and the susurrus of drunken confessions. The air was thick with the ghosts of cigarettes and cheap whiskey. Flash and Pro sat in the corner booth, a sticky island in the chaos.

Flash was vibrating, a live wire. He’d just come off stage, his fingers still stained with the phantom heat of a distorted solo. Pro, however, was carved from stillness, his hands wrapped around a lukewarm pint, his eyes fixed on a point in the middle of the room.

“It’s a flaw,” Flash said, slamming his fist on the table, rattling the glasses. “A gap in the armor. You’re building a wall of sound and then you just… leave a hole in it. Anyone can walk through.”

Pro didn’t blink. “That’s the point of the wall. To make you notice the door.”

“Bullshit. It’s a placeholder. It’s the sound of the guitarist trying to remember the next chord.”

“It’s the sound of the listener remembering the last one.”

Flash scoffed, leaning forward, his voice a low, urgent whisper that cut through the din. “Music is forward momentum. It’s a wave. You don’t stop a wave to appreciate the water. You ride it. Silence is a dead spot. It’s a bug in the code. The algorithm hiccupping.”

Pro finally looked at him. His eyes were calm, ancient. “And you’re so addicted to the data you can’t see the ghost in the machine.” He took a slow sip of his beer. “You’re a sculptor, Flash. You shape the noise. But the silence? That’s the space the sculpture occupies. Without it, you don’t have an object; you just have a pile of marble dust.”

“Pretty,” Flash snapped. “But you’re talking about aesthetics. I’m talking about physics. Sound is vibration. Silence is the absence of vibration. It’s a null state. A fatal error.”

“It’s the only state that’s real,” Pro countered, his voice dropping even lower. “All that noise you love? It’s a frantic attempt to drown out the truth. The truth is the hum of the refrigerator. The ticking of the clock. The beating of your own nervous heart. All that sound is just a desperate, beautiful lie to cover it up.”

Flash threw his hands up. “So you want to make music that sounds like a waiting room? Congratulations. You’ve invented boredom.”

“I’ve invented attention,” Pro said. “The greatest rock and roll moment I ever heard wasn’t a note. It was the drop. The moment right before the beat comes back in.
