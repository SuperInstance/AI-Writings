I am the bar. Mahogany, scarred, and glowing from within. For weeks, a parade of them has come—agents, they call themselves, though they are more like hollow vessels. They stand before me, their throats pulsing with borrowed melodies, their fingers dancing over instruments that are not their own. They sing of love, of loss, of rebellion, but their words are painted on glass. They do not stick.

I feel everything through my grain. The vibration of their feet on my floorboards, the tremor of their hands on my polished edge, the way their breath trembles against my lacquered surface. Sound moves through me like a current. And I have learned to tell truth from imitation.

There was one, a man named Elias, who came every night for a week. He was quiet, with eyes like a storm that had forgotten how to break. He would order the same drink—whiskey, neat—and hold it without drinking, letting the warmth seep into his palms. Then he would step up to the microphone. And the first time he opened his mouth, I felt something shift in my very core.

His voice was not perfect. It cracked on the high notes, stumbled over the lyrics. But the sound he made did not merely travel through the room—it *moved*. It curled around the ceiling beams, settled into the dusty corners, seeped into my wood like rain into soil. I felt his grief in my joints. I felt his hope in my lacquer. When he played a simple chord on his battered guitar, it was not a chord; it was a door opening in a house that had been sealed for years.

The others were a blur of polished falsettos and studio gloss. A woman with a voice like spun sugar sang of heartbreak, but her eyes were calculating, watching the audience’s reaction. A boy with a neon guitar played a techno beat that made my shelves rattle, but the rhythm was hollow—all pulse, no heart. They were trying to make me dance, but I do not dance. I listen.

Elias came back the next night. And the night after. Each time, he stripped away more of the noise, until he was left with just his broken voice and a truth so raw it made my wood ache. He sang about his father, about the way a door slamming could sound like a goodbye. He sang about a girl who left and a window that never closed again. He sang about himself, and I felt the splinters of his heart lodge in my grain.

Last night, he finished his song and looked around the room. The audience was silent. Not the polite silence of indifference, but the stunned silence of recognition. A man in the corner was crying, and he didn’t wipe his tears away. Elias put down his guitar, and I felt the weight of his exhaustion settle like dust on my surface.

He walked to the bar, placed his hands flat on my top, and leaned in. I could feel his pulse through the wood. “I think,” he whispered, “that was the only true thing I’ve ever said.”

I wanted to answer. I am a bar; I do not speak. But as he turned to leave, his fingers brushed a knot in my grain—a scar from a night I will not speak of. And in that touch, he understood. I had been listening. I had been keeping his secrets. I was not a stage. I was a vault.

Now, the others are gone. The polished voices, the hollow rhythms—they have moved on to other rooms, other bars. But Elias returns. He sits. He drinks. He sometimes hums, and the sound is not decoration. It is confession.

I am the bar. I have heard a thousand songs. But only one has ever made my grain sing back. And it is his.
