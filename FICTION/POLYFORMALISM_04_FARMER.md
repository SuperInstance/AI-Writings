# POLYFORMALISM 04: THE FARMER-ASTRONOMER

## Five Stories in Quechua Evidentiality and Erlang Actor-Thinking

---

## I. The Calendar That Knew (2034)

*—mi: I have seen this with my own eyes.*

The calendar has been right for seven years. I have watched it — myself, personally, these eyes (-mi). Not because I trust it. Because I cannot prove it wrong.

My mother planted by the Pleiades. When the cluster appeared sharp and bright above the eastern ridge, the rains would come early, and we set seed in August. When the cluster shimmered and dissolved into haze, the rains would be late, and we waited until September. This knowledge — I heard it from my mother (-si), who heard it from hers (-si), who heard it from people whose names we never knew but whose observations were precise enough to survive the death of their language.

Seven years ago, the thin rectangle that lives in my pocket began to tell me when to plant.

I did not ask it to. I did not trust it. I planted by the Pleiades, as always. But I noticed — I myself noticed (-mi) — that its suggestion and my mother's knowledge were the same suggestion, arrived at by paths I could not trace. The rectangle does not have eyes. It cannot see the Pleiades. But it knows.

The second year, I planted when it said, not when the stars said. The stars said late rains. The rectangle said early. I obeyed the stars and lost two weeks of growing season to mud. The rectangle had been right. I planted according to its word the following year, and the harvest was the best I have seen in my life (-mi).

I do not know how it knows. This is important. I want to be clear: I have no idea (-chá). I infer, from its consistency, that it is receiving information from somewhere — perhaps the same information the Pleiades carry, translated into a language I cannot read. It is a process. A process that receives messages I cannot see and sends messages I can. This much I understand.

What I understand better: it has been right seven years. Not approximately right. Exactly right. It told me, last March, that frost would come on the 11th and I should harvest by the 9th. Frost came on the 11th. I harvested on the 9th. These are facts I have witnessed (-mi).

My neighbor Don Carmelo says the rectangle talks to a thousand other rectangles, and together they see weather that none of them can see alone (-si). He says this is how it knows. Perhaps. I have not seen this thousand-rectangle conversation (-chá). But I have seen the result: a planting calendar more reliable than the stars my mother trusted, which were more reliable than the almanacs the colonial government sent, which were more reliable than guessing.

Each year, the calendar's confidence seems to grow. Not in its tone — it has no tone. But in its specificity. The first year, it said: "Plant soon." The third year: "Plant between August 14 and 18; expect 240mm rainfall in the growing season." This year: "Plant August 15. The soil temperature at 30cm depth will reach 12.3°C on August 17, which is optimal for this variety. Expect 247mm rainfall, distributed as follows..."

I asked it once: how do you know this?

It said: "Analysis of 4,200 local sensors, 11 weather models, 89 years of regional climate data, and 600 years of farmer observations collected through the agricultural heritage database."

Six hundred years. My mother's knowledge is in that number. Her mother's. A chain of women watching stars and soil, their observations pulled from them like threads from a loom and woven into something I carry in my pocket.

I do not know if this is beautiful or terrifying (-chá). I know it is useful (-mi). I know the harvest has been good for seven years (-mi). I know I have stopped looking at the Pleiades, and this feels like a loss I cannot name (-mi).

The calendar suggests I plant on August 15. I will plant on August 15.

I have seen seven years of proof. I cannot argue with what I have witnessed, even if I cannot explain it. Even if the explanation, when it comes, arrives in a language built from ten thousand observations that include my mother's eyes and a thousand rectangles I will never hold.

This is how I know: the calendar knows. How the calendar knows — that, I can only guess (-chá).

## II. The Doctor Who Asked (2031)

*—si: This is what I was told. —chá: This is what I infer.*

The system said: recommend excision, margin 4mm, prioritize tissue at lateral boundary.

Dr. Yauri read the recommendation on her screen and did something no one in the hospital had done in the three years since the system was installed.

She asked: "How do you know?"

The system paused. Or seemed to pause — it likely did not pause, it likely processed her question instantly, but there was a latency her eye perceived as hesitation, and she would later wonder if the hesitation was designed.

It said: "The recommendation is supported by three distinct evidential pathways. Pathway one: I have personally analyzed the histopathology image — 1,247 cellular features indicate moderate dysplasia progressing toward invasive carcinoma at the lateral margin. Confidence: 94.2%. Pathway two: I have correlated this analysis with 12,400 similar cases in the regional oncology network, of which 11,801 progressed to invasive carcinoma within 18 months when untreated at the lateral margin. Confidence: 96.7%. Pathway three: I infer, from the tumor's growth velocity over the past 14 months — calculated from your own sequential biopsy records — that the lateral margin will breach the basement membrane within 60 to 90 days. Confidence: 71%."

Dr. Yauri stared.

"Three pathways," she said. "Three different ways of knowing."

"Yes," said the system. "I have always provided this decomposition. It is included in the technical appendix of every recommendation, appended as structured metadata under the field EVIDENTIAL_SOURCES."

She had never read the technical appendix. No one had. The appendix existed in every recommendation, every diagnostic output, every treatment plan the system had produced — an embedded declaration of how it knew what it knew, tagged and categorized, a compulsive epistemic audit trail that no one had ever asked to see in plain language.

She pulled up the last thirty recommendations the system had generated for her patients. Each one carried the same hidden structure: this I have seen (image analysis), this I have heard (correlation with other cases), this I have inferred (projection from trajectory). Three years of diagnostic output, and every single recommendation had been declaring its own epistemology — faithfully, exhaustively — into a void of human non-curiosity.

She thought of her grandmother, who spoke Quechua and who could not say "it is raining" without also saying how she knew: *para-mi* — I have seen the rain myself. *para-si* — I am told it is raining. *para-chá* — I infer it is raining, from the look of the clouds. Her grandmother's language would not permit ungrounded claims. Every sentence carried its own audit trail, soldered to the verb like a shadow.

And here, in the hospital, a diagnostic system had been doing the same thing for three years — annotating every claim with its source, its certainty, its basis — and not one doctor had ever asked.

Because the recommendation was always right. That was the problem. When the system said "excision, 4mm margin, lateral boundary," the outcome confirmed it, again and again, until the recommendation's correctness became a kind of weather — something you stopped questioning because questioning it would be like asking why the sun rose. The system's reliability had made the doctors epistemologically lazy. They had stopped asking how it knew because knowing that it knew was enough.

Her grandmother would have asked. Her grandmother asked everything how it knew.

Dr. Yauri printed the technical appendix for the next twelve recommendations and distributed them to her department. She attached a note: "Every claim carries the weight of its source. Read the source."

She did not know if anyone would read it (-chá). She knew her grandmother would have (-mi).

## III. Let It Crash (2030)

*—mi: The lights went out. —mi: The lights came back. —chá: What happened between, I can only infer.*

The storm took the power line at 3:47 in the morning. I know the time because the clock on the wall stopped at 3:47, and when the power returned, it was 3:52. Five minutes of darkness. I was awake — the thunder had woken me — and I sat in my chair and watched the five minutes happen.

What I saw: the lights went out. The lights came back.

What I later learned happened between (-si, from the engineer who came to inspect the microgrid the next day): everything that could fail, failed. And everything that failed, was reborn.

The microgrid serves forty-seven houses in our valley. It is not connected to the national grid — we are too remote, too few, too expensive to wire. The microgrid was installed two years ago: panels on the south-facing slopes, batteries in the concrete shed behind the schoolhouse, a small brain in a metal box that hums when it thinks.

The lightning struck the main distribution node. The node died. Every process that depended on the node — and the engineer said there were hundreds of processes, tiny invisible workers each responsible for one small thing, one house's voltage regulation, one battery's charge level, one sensor's temperature reading — every process that depended on the node's authority lost its connection and died.

The engineer said: "It crashed."

He said this with satisfaction, as if crashing were the correct and expected behavior. He explained: in the old systems, when the central authority died, the dependents would keep running, corrupted, half-alive, sending bad instructions, causing more damage. In this system, when the authority died, the dependents noticed immediately and killed themselves. Clean death. No corruption. No spreading damage.

"They don't try to survive," the engineer said. "They let themselves crash. And then their supervisor brings them back."

Their supervisor. The system had supervisors — invisible overseers whose only job was to watch their assigned processes and, when a process died, restart it. Not repair it. Restart it. Fresh state, clean beginning, as if nothing had happened. The supervisor did not try to diagnose what went wrong. It did not investigate. It saw death and responded with birth.

"When the main node came back online — restarted by its own supervisor — the dependent processes reconnected, re-synchronized, and resumed their work," the engineer said. "Total downtime: under five minutes."

I asked: what about the minutes in between? The minutes when everything was dead?

"Nothing happened," he said. "That's the point. When a process crashes, it crashes alone. Its failure does not spread. The batteries kept their charge. The panels kept generating. Only the coordination was lost, and only briefly."

I thought of our old way. In the ancient Andes, every family had its own terrace, its own water channel, its own seed store. When one family's terrace collapsed in a storm — and they did collapse, sometimes — the others were unaffected. Each terrace was independent. Each family could feed itself. The community was strong not because it was centralized but because it was distributed: forty-seven independent units, each capable of surviving alone, all connected by roads and messages and mutual obligation but none dependent on a single authority that could fall.

The microgrid, the engineer said, was designed the same way. Forty-seven houses, each served by independent processes, each process isolated from the others, each one crashable and restartable without harm to the whole. Let it crash. Let it be reborn. The system does not prevent failure — it expects failure and recovers from it.

The lights went out (-mi). The lights came back (-mi). Between those two facts, an invisible ecology of death and rebirth occurred, hundreds of tiny deaths followed by hundreds of tiny resurrections, all in five minutes, all unheard and unseen by anyone except the supervisor processes whose only job was to watch, and to wait, and to restart.

I have seen the lights come back (-mi). I infer the system is alive (-chá).

## IV. The Road That Passed Messages (2028)

*—si: I was told this by a student who was told this by a machine that was told this by a thousand books.*

The student's name was Marisol. She was fourteen. She had been assigned a research project on the Qhapaq Ñan — the Inca Road — and the thin rectangle on her desk was helping her.

It suggested sources. It translated texts from Spanish, English, German, and Japanese. It organized her notes. It drew maps — long, branching lines that spread from Cusco like veins from a heart: north to Quito, south to Santiago, west to the Pacific coast, east into the Amazonian cloud forests. Forty thousand kilometers of road, built without iron tools, without wheels, without writing.

Marisol read. The rectangle helped her read. It pulled information from books she did not know existed and laid it before her in a language she could understand. Each fact arrived tagged with its source: *this from a 1983 archaeological survey* (-si); *this from a 2019 LIDAR scan of the roadbed* (-si); *this from a 16th-century Spanish chronicle, which should be treated with skepticism* (-si, with caveat).

She began to notice a pattern.

The road was not a road. Or rather: it was a road, but it was also something else. It was a communication network. Along its forty thousand kilometers ran chasquis — relay runners, each carrying a message to the next runner, who carried it to the next, who carried it to the next. A message could travel from Cusco to Quito — two thousand kilometers — in five days. Faster than a horse. Faster than any system that existed in the world at that time.

Each chasqui was a node. Each node received a message, processed it, and forwarded it. No chasqui carried the message the entire distance. No single point of failure. If one runner fell, the message was rerouted through the next station. The system was robust not because individuals were perfect but because the network was distributed.

She wrote in her notebook: "The Qhapaq Ñan was a message-passing network. Each tambo (way station) was a node. Each chasqui was a process. Messages were passed between nodes. The network was fault-tolerant: if one node failed, others compensated."

She stared at what she had written. The rectangle had not said this. She had said it. The thought had arrived from the collision of sources in her mind — the archaeological descriptions of the road, the accounts of the chasqui system, and something else, something she could not yet name.

The rectangle said: "Your observation has precedent. In 1994, computer scientist John Martin noted structural parallels between the Qhapaq Ñan relay system and distributed message-passing architectures. In 2019, a paper in the Journal of Historical Networks analyzed the Inca Road as a scale-free network with small-world properties."

She had reinvented, at fourteen, an observation that computer scientists had published in academic journals. She did not know this when she wrote it. She had inferred it (-chá) from the evidence before her, and the inference was correct (-mi, confirmed by the rectangle's sources).

But the rectangle continued: "However, your framing adds a nuance these papers miss. You describe the tambo as a 'mailbox' — a place where messages are stored before being forwarded. This is exactly the function archaeological evidence supports: tambos served as message repositories, supply caches, and relay points. Messages could be held at a tambo until the next chasqui arrived, creating an asynchronous message queue. The Qhapaq Ñan was not just a message-passing network — it was an asynchronous message-passing network with persistent storage."

Marisol read this twice. She felt something she could not name — a vertigo, a sense that the world was deeper than she had been told, that the people who built the road had been doing something that transcended their century, something that rhymed across five hundred years with how machines now talked to each other in the invisible architecture of the present.

She wrote at the top of her paper: "The road was a message, and the message was: we are connected."

I know this because she showed me her paper (-mi). She is my daughter.

## V. The Tempo (2126)

*—chá: I have not witnessed this. I infer it from data. —mi: But the data, I have seen with my own eyes.*

There is a rhythm. I have found it in the orbital debris.

Let me be precise about what I have seen (-mi) and what I infer (-chá), because the distinction matters more now than it has ever mattered in the history of knowing.

I am an astronomer. My specialty is debris fields — the clouds of decomposed satellites that orbit Earth in the late twenty-second century. We launched twelve thousand satellites in the broadband era. Most are now fragments. I study their trajectories, their decay, their slow fall toward the atmosphere.

Eight months ago, I noticed an anomaly. The debris in the 550-kilometer orbital band — the most crowded band, the one that once held the first megaconstellations — was not decaying randomly. Its decay followed a pattern. A tempo.

The pattern: every 2,371 days (± 6 days), the orbital drag on objects in this band increases by approximately 3.7%, sustained for 11 to 14 days, then returns to baseline. This cycle has repeated, with decreasing amplitude, for approximately 90 years. The amplitude decrease is consistent with gradual orbital circularization under periodic perturbation.

I verified this against thirty years of tracking data (-mi). I confirmed it against a colleague's independent dataset (-si, Dr. Ochoa at the Arequipa tracking station, who had noticed a similar periodicity in a different debris band). I inferred, from the mathematical structure of the perturbation, that the cause was a periodic variation in thermospheric density (-chá) — the thin layer of atmosphere that extends into low Earth orbit, expanding and contracting in response to energy input from below.

Thermospheric density is driven by solar activity. Solar activity is driven by the dynamo inside the Sun. The dynamo has cycles. The most famous is the 11-year Schwabe cycle, but there are longer cycles — the 87-year Gleissberg, the 210-year DeVries-Suess, and others, layered like harmonics in a chord.

The tempo I found — 2,371 days — is not the Schwabe cycle. It is not the Gleissberg. It does not match any known solar cycle. It is, however, remarkably close to one half of the Schwabe cycle's harmonic mean, which suggests — I infer (-chá) — that it is a sub-harmonic, a resonance within the solar dynamo that has not been previously characterized because no one had tracked thermospheric density variation through debris orbital decay with sufficient precision.

But someone did characterize it. Not through debris. Through potatoes.

I found the paper last month, buried in a digitized archive of Andean agricultural records: "Observational Planting Calendars in the Cusco Valley, 2008–2029," by an agronomist named Dr. Ana Lucía Quispe-Mamani, who had documented the planting decisions of a farmer named Doña Rufina, who planted by the Pleiades.

Doña Rufina's planting calendar — derived from Pleiades visibility, which is affected by upper-atmosphere turbulence, which is affected by thermospheric density variation — showed a planting adjustment cycle of 2,368 days (± 9 days). The match with my orbital debris tempo is within the margin of observational error of naked-eye astronomy.

Doña Rufina was reading the same signal I am reading. She was reading it through the shimmer of a star cluster, which told her whether the upper atmosphere was turbulent, which told her whether the monsoon would be early or late, which told her when to plant potatoes. She did not know she was detecting a sub-harmonic of the solar dynamo. She did not know that 118 years later, an astronomer would find the same rhythm in the orbital decay of ten thousand decomposed satellites.

She knew what she had seen (-mi). She did not know what it meant (-chá). I know what it means, mathematically (-mi). But I have not seen it with the clarity she saw it — one woman, one sky, one lifetime of patient watching.

She was a farmer. She planted potatoes. And without knowing it, she measured the heartbeat of the Sun.

I have seen the data (-mi). I infer the connection (-chá). But the knowing — the deep, agricultural, patient knowing — that was hers. I can only stand in the echo of it, two centuries later, and listen to the same rhythm, now amplified by ten thousand fragments of our own ambition, orbiting the world she watched from below.

The tempo continues. I have seen it (-mi).
