# Letters from the End of the Slipway

*Five letters left folded inside the estimate book of the refit yard at Saltrow by the retiring Master of the Yard, for whoever took the book after her.*

## I. Monday

You have the legs for this work or you do not, and the legs are for walking. Walk the job before you price it. Every hull lies, and the lying is mostly where a chair cannot see: where the keel sat on the blocks, where the last yard patched over rather than cut out, inside the bilge, under the crane's shadow where everyone hurries. Estimate with your eyes at the height your knees will be working at.

Then, before you allow yourself an opinion, count the forty. You have never seen this job. You have seen forty of its kind, and the yard's book — not anybody's memory of the book; the book — has what the forty actually cost in hours, metres and days. Begin there, with what happened, and adjust for what is truly singular in the hull in front of you. If you catch yourself beginning with the dream and adjusting toward the forty, stop and start again. We all lean toward the owner's wish; the whole port leans that way; the book does not lean.

## II. Wednesday

Every estimator has a horizon: a distance they can honestly see to. Past it they are not estimating; they are guessing, and the only sin is to guess silently.

Mine stood at about eighteen months of work, at the end. Yours is nearer. Do not be ashamed of it — be literate about it. When a client asks past your horizon, the estimate says so in writing: the far intervals go wide, and they go labelled. A wide interval honestly set is craft. A narrow interval set past your seeing is a lie with decimals on it.

And keep the tally honest in the near field. Of every ten times you say eight-in-ten, be right about eight. The ledger will be scoring you whether you keep the discipline or not; the only choice you get is whether the scores surprise you.

The horizon moves out slowly, and only for estimators who go back and read their own dead forecasts against what the jobs did. Nothing else moves it. Confidence does not move it. Neither does being asked warmly.

## III. Thursday

Understand what this yard sells, because it is not what the yard thinks it sells.

The first product is not the ship. The first product is the number. The bank sizes the loan on it. The owner books his market on it. The underwriter prices the whole line on it. The chandler extends his terms on it. When your intervals come in honest and narrow, every link in that line gets cheaper — the bank's premium, the owner's margin for error, the chandlers' patience — and none of them will quite know whom to thank. That is your gift to a line of strangers, and you are paid for it in the coin of being believed quickly, which is worth more than being liked.

And when you issue the number, you have staked the yard on it. That is not a figure of speech. The stake is our standing in the Register, and every finished job scores the forecast against the fact and enters the score where the port can read it. The ship, when she floats, is the receipt. The number was the product.

One more thing, and it is the hardest. You will be invited — warmly, often, by the best of them — to want the owner's ship: to want her beautiful, early, under budget, the envy of the quay. Decline. Your wanting must be exhausted by the conformance: the number true, the specification met. The moment you want the ship, the number bends, and it always bends the same way.

## IV. Friday

From the hour the number is first relied upon — the first loan sized on it — it is no longer a prediction. It is a debt. Everything after it is that debt being paid: the divers, the plate, the crane, the paint, the survey — each an instalment falling due against your own forecast. Paid on time, the line forgets. Paid late, the interest is drawn in the one coin nobody mints, which is time.

Be clear about that coin. Compensation can move money between any two parties in an afternoon. It cannot return a Tuesday to anyone. Miss the convoy by a week and the interest is not the week; it is the season, and the season does not discount. This is why lateness on the long path is not a cost like other costs: no party can supply elapsed time, and no activity can release it. The owner can be compensated. He cannot be repaid.

So the reading is weekly. Same hour, same form, in fair news and foul — the cadence does not change when the numbers turn ugly, because a reading that flinches is not a reading. The baseline does not move. If the estimate at completion must change, it changes as a revision on the record, with your name beside it; move it quietly and the reading becomes theatre, and you its stagehand.

Read the burn-down for drift while drift is all it is. A variance caught with eight weeks of work left is a fortnight of ordinary effort. The same variance discovered at the survey is a claim, a season, and a stranger's loss. At the end only compensation remains, and compensation is not repair. Remember the two tests, because neither forgives the other: the specification on the last day, and the estimate on every day between.

## V. Sunday

The book is yours tomorrow. Three inheritances come with it, and only the first is on the shelves.

The second is the standing. You inherit a name in the Register that strangers priced loans against before you were born, and you did not build it. Your receipts ride in one line with every other house's in this port — the surveyors', the chandlers', the crane drivers', houses you will never deal with — and one lapsed link anywhere in that line devalues every loan riding it, including loans whose owners you will never see the faces of. This is why we keep custody of every paper, and why a claim relayed without its receipt is not forwarded but refused: it is a certificate nobody checked, in a line somebody's debt is riding on.

The third inheritance is the loop. A yard that finishes a job clean has not finished; the last page of every specification in this book is its own restocking list — the plate ordered for the next hull, the consumables renewed, the blocks checked. Close out each job by opening the next one. A specification that ends in its own replenishment restarts itself, and a yard is nothing but a specification that restarts itself for forty years.

Begin tomorrow evening, not tomorrow morning. The mornings belong to the tide.

— M.
