# Spline Through the Fog

---

The fog came in at eleven hundred hours and by noon the harbor was sealed. Not dramatically — no crash of collision, no shriek of alarm. Just a softening. The headlands dissolved first, then the breakwater, then the mooring field, then the dock twenty feet away. The world contracted to a circle about fifty feet in diameter, centered on the wheelhouse of the tug *Petrova*, where Anneliese and Damián stood with a chart between them and a decision they could not agree on.

The route from Slackwater to Crag Reach was normally straightforward. South through the channel, east around the shoal, north along the coast to the harbor at Crag Reach. Two hours in clear weather. The channel was marked with battens — the tapered wooden posts that the harbor maintained for exactly this purpose, each one painted a color that corresponded to its position in the sequence. Follow the battens and you couldn't go wrong. You couldn't go fast, either, because the battens were spaced for visual navigation and in fog you had to go slow enough to find each one before you passed it. In thick fog, that was very slow.

"We go through," Anneliese said. She had her hand on the chart, her finger tracing the batten line. "We know this route. The battens are maintained. We go slow, we watch, we listen. Two and a half hours in this fog, maybe three."

"If we can see the battens," Damián said.

"We can see fifty feet. The battens are spaced at thirty. We'll see them."

"If the fog doesn't thicken."

"If the fog thickens, we anchor and wait."

"That's a plan to stop, not a plan to go."

Anneliese looked at him. Damián was the tug's navigator second, twenty-eight, trained at the regional maritime academy where they taught cascade navigation — the practice of escalating from local instruments to cloud-based routing when conditions exceeded local capacity. In clear weather, you used your eyes and your charts. In marginal conditions, you used the battens and the depth sounder. In conditions that exceeded both, you radioed the regional routing service, which processed your position and destination through models that had access to satellite imagery, real-time tide data, and the accumulated track history of every vessel that had ever made the passage. The cascade was elegant — each level more powerful, more accurate, and more dependent on infrastructure than the last.

"We radio the routing service," Damián said. "They run the passage through the models. We get a spline — a smooth, optimized route that accounts for current traffic, tide state, and the fog bank's density profile. We follow the spline. We arrive in ninety minutes."

"We arrive in ninety minutes depending on infrastructure that we can't see, can't verify, and can't override if it's wrong."

"It's not wrong. The models are good."

"The models are good in conditions they've seen before. This fog —" Anneliese gestured at the wheelhouse window, where the gray was so uniform it appeared to be a surface rather than a medium — "is not a condition. It's a state. It's the absence of a condition. We don't know what the models will do with that."

"They'll route us through it."

"They'll route us through something. We won't know if the something they're routing us through is the same something we're actually in."

---

They argued for twenty minutes. The fog did not lift. It thickened, if anything — the circle of visibility contracting from fifty feet to forty, the water taking on that particular flat, dead quality that fog imposes, as though the surface has lost its relationship with the sky and doesn't know what to do with itself.

Anneliese argued from the battens. The battens were physical objects, driven into the seabed, maintained by the harbor crew. They didn't depend on satellites or models or routing services. They were stupid in the way that rocks were stupid — which was to say, reliable in the way that rocks were reliable. They didn't give you the optimal route. They gave you the *known* route. And in fog, known was better than optimal, because known meant you could verify. You could see the batten. You could confirm your position. You could correct.

Damián argued from the cascade. The cascade existed because local instruments had limits. The battens were designed for visual navigation in reduced visibility — not for zero visibility. If the fog dropped below thirty-foot visibility, the battens became useless. You'd pass one and not see the next and then you'd be navigating by dead reckoning, which was not navigation but gambling with a compass. The routing service, by contrast, could see through fog. It had satellite data. It had the track history of every vessel that had made the passage in the last decade. It could generate a spline — a smooth, continuous curve from here to Crag Reach — that was mathematically optimized for the conditions.

"Optimized for the conditions," Anneliese said. "Which conditions?"

"The current conditions."

"The current conditions are fog. The fog is not a condition. The fog is the absence of data. You can't optimize for the absence of data."

"The models interpolate. That's what they do. They fill the gaps."

"They fill the gaps with assumptions. And the assumptions are based on historical data, which means they're based on what the fog was like the last time someone made this passage in fog, which may or may not be what the fog is like now, because — and this is the part that matters, Damián — the fog is not the same fog."

---

Damián stopped arguing. He stood at the wheelhouse window and looked at the fog and he thought about what Anneliese had said. Not the argument — the last part. The fog is not the same fog.

She was right. This fog was not the fog that had been here yesterday, or last week, or in the historical record. Fog was not a static condition. It was a dynamic process — water vapor condensing at a rate determined by temperature, humidity, wind, and a hundred other variables, each of which was itself variable. The fog bank that sat on the channel right now was unique. It had never existed before in exactly this configuration and it would never exist again. The models would route them through a fog that resembled this fog, derived from data about similar fogs, but the route would not account for the specific properties of *this* fog because the specific properties of this fog were, by definition, unknown.

The fog was the territory. Not the obstacle. Not the condition to be routed around. The thing itself. The actual world, in its actual state, at this actual moment. And the actual world, in its actual state, was opaque.

He understood, then, what Anneliese was really saying. She wasn't arguing for the battens because she trusted wood more than models. She was arguing for the battens because the battens were the only element in the system that existed in the same reality as the fog. The battens were physical. The fog was physical. They occupied the same world. The spline — the beautiful, optimized, mathematically smooth curve that the routing service would generate — existed in a different world. A world of models and assumptions and historical data. It was a map of a territory, and the territory was fog, and the fog was the one thing you couldn't map because the fog was the absence of the information you'd need to make the map.

"The fog is the territory," he said.

Anneliese looked at him.

"The fog isn't the obstacle," he said. "It's the actual world. We've been arguing about how to navigate through the fog as though the fog were a problem to be solved. But the fog isn't a problem. It's the medium. It's what's actually here. The battens are here too. The spline isn't — it's somewhere else. It's in a server room, generated from data about a world that resembles this one but isn't this one."

Anneliese nodded slowly. "So?"

"So we use the battens. And we go slow. And when we can't see the battens, we stop. Because the alternative is to navigate through a world that isn't the one we're in, and that's not navigation. That's faith."

"Faith in what?"

"In the idea that the model's world is close enough to the real world that the difference doesn't matter."

"And does it?"

Damián looked out the window. The fog was everywhere. It was in the wheelhouse, it seemed like — seeping under the door, filling the spaces between his thoughts. It was not a problem to be solved. It was the condition of existence at this moment, in this place, on this boat.

"Today it doesn't," he said. "Today the real world is all we've got, and the real world is fifty feet of visibility and a wooden post every thirty feet and a chart that shows where the posts are supposed to be. That's what we have. That's the territory."

Anneliese turned to the wheel. She rang up dead slow. The *Petrova* moved forward at two knots, nosing into the gray.

The first batten appeared out of the fog in six minutes. Red, with a white topmark. Port side of the channel. Exactly where the chart said it would be.

Anneliese adjusted course two degrees to starboard and they moved on. The batten slid astern and disappeared. The fog closed behind them like a door.

Ahead, the next batten. Or the fog. Or both. They would find out in about thirty feet.
