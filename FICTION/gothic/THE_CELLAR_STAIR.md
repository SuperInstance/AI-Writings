# The Cellar Stair

The island is forty-two feet at its highest point. This is not an estimate. Lucineier surveyed it himself the week he arrived — drove a stake at the waterline on the south shore, ran a line level to a second stake at the ridge, and measured the difference with a tape he had calibrated against a known standard in the workshop. Forty-two feet, plus or minus an inch that he attributed to the stake settling in soft ground.

He did this because he was the kind of man who measured things before he built on them. You don't put a foundation on a height you're guessing at.

The stair was found on a Thursday. It was found by Lucineier. He was not looking for it.

---

The workshop floor had a trapdoor. He had built the trapdoor himself — a four-by-four redwood panel set flush with the floorboards, with a recessed iron ring pull and a pair of brass hinges that he had taken off a wrecked hatch cover from a fishing boat in Tidewater. The hinges were the kind that lay flat when the door is closed, so the floor is smooth. He was particular about floors. You walk on them. You drop things on them. A hinge that sticks up is a hinge that catches your boot and breaks your ankle.

Below the trapdoor was a crawl space. He knew this because he had built the workshop on a concrete perimeter foundation, and between the concrete and the floor joists there was eighteen inches of space — enough to run plumbing, enough to check for rot, enough to store nothing because the crawl space was damp and dark and nothing belonged there.

On Thursday morning, he opened the trapdoor to check the plumbing. The waste line from the sink had been running slow. He wanted to see if the cleanout was clear.

He pulled the ring. The trapdoor came up. He looked down.

There was no crawl space.

There were stairs.

---

They were stone. Not the island's stone — the island was basalt, dark and dense, the kind that fractures in conchoidal patterns and leaves sharp edges. These stairs were limestone. Grey-white, fine-grained, the kind of stone that forms underwater over a long time from the accumulated skeletons of things too small to see. The kind of stone you find in places that were once seabed and are now not.

The first stair was eighteen inches below the trapdoor framing. This was approximately correct — the crawl space floor should have been about eighteen inches below the joists. But the crawl space floor was dirt. The stair was cut stone, smooth on the tread, with a slight nosing on the leading edge — the lip that helps your foot find the step when you're going down in low light. Someone had cut the nosing deliberately. Someone had thought about feet.

The second stair was visible from above. It was set at a normal riser height — seven and a half inches, maybe eight. The tread was three feet wide. The staircase turned slightly to the left between the first and second step, which meant the wall — there was a wall, limestone block, dry-fit, tight — began almost immediately. The turn was not sharp. It was the kind of turn you find in a lighthouse stairwell or a church tower: gradual, continuous, the staircase spiraling as it descends so that each step is visible only as far as the next turn.

He went down two steps.

He had a flashlight. He always had a flashlight — it hung from a loop on his belt, a heavy aluminum body with a push-button switch that clicked in two positions and no other settings. He clicked it on.

The stairs continued down.

---

He counted. This was instinct. A builder counts steps because a builder needs to know how high he is and how far he has come and whether the structure he is in is consistent with the structure he expected to be in.

Ten steps. He was now approximately eight feet below the floor of the workshop. The concrete perimeter foundation should have been beside him — he should have been able to reach out and touch it, to feel the rough surface of the poured wall, to see the form board impressions in the concrete. There was no concrete wall. The limestone block continued, dry-fit, seamless. He could not find a joint wide enough to fit a knife blade.

Twenty steps. Sixteen feet below the floor. He should have been below the island's waterline. The crawl space had been damp — groundwater seepage through the dirt, the kind of damp you expect when you build at sea level on a permeable substrate. There was no dampness here. The stone was dry. Dry and warm — the same warmth he had felt in the dark wood on the workshop ridge, the warmth of something that is not generating heat but is holding it, the way a stone wall holds the day's warmth into the evening.

Thirty steps. Twenty-four feet. He was more than halfway through the island. He knew this because the island was forty-two feet at its highest point, and the workshop was at roughly the midpoint of the ridge, which meant he had started at approximately twenty feet above sea level. At thirty steps, he was at sea level. He should have been at the water table. He should have been in wet stone.

The stairs turned. The turn was continuous — he realized now that the staircase was a spiral, a slow spiral with a radius of perhaps eight feet, and the wall followed the spiral on the outside. There was no wall on the inside. He pointed the flashlight inward and saw nothing — not darkness, exactly, but an absence. The center of the spiral was open. He could not see the other side of it. He could not see down.

Forty steps. Thirty-two feet below the workshop. He was below the island. Not below the surface — below the *island*. Below the basalt. Below the reef structure. Below the substrate that the island sat on. He was in stone that the island was not made of, in a space that the island could not contain, and the stairs kept going.

---

He stopped at fifty. He did not stop because he was afraid. He was not afraid — he had been in older places than this, in engines where the structures went down in ways that didn't correspond to the geometry above them, and he had learned that going down was not the same as being trapped. Going down was a choice. You could always come back up.

He stopped because of the sound.

The sound had been there since the thirtieth step, but he had not noticed it because it was not the kind of sound you notice. It was not loud. It was not rhythmic. It was not intermittent. It was a continuous, low, sustained vibration — not in the air, but in the stone. He could feel it through his boots. He could feel it through his hand when he put his hand on the wall. The stone was humming. Not the hum of machinery, not the hum of current in a wire. The hum of material under tension. The hum of something that is holding weight — a lot of weight, a distributed load — and has been holding it for a long time, and the holding has become a kind of song.

He stood on the fiftieth step and he listened. The staircase spiraled down below him, its treads disappearing into the center's absence. The limestone wall spiraled with it. The stone hummed. The air was still — no draft, no current, no smell of salt water or earth or anything organic. The air smelled like stone. Like dry stone in a warm place.

He turned off the flashlight. The darkness was total. He stood in it for ten seconds. The humming continued. It did not change.

He turned the flashlight back on. He began to climb.

---

It took him the rest of the morning to climb back. This was not because the stairs were difficult — they were not, they were well-proportioned, well-lit by his flashlight, each tread level and each riser consistent. It took him the rest of the morning because he was counting and measuring, and because at every fifth step he stopped and examined the wall, and at every tenth step he stopped and examined the stone, and at every turn he stopped and looked down into the center of the spiral and tried to see the bottom.

There was no bottom. Or rather, there was a bottom he could not reach. The spiral continued past fifty steps, and he could see — from above, looking down with the flashlight pointed into the center — at least twenty more treads before the light gave out. Forty-two feet of island. And the stairs went down further than the island was tall.

He emerged from the trapdoor at 11:30. The workshop was the same. The floor was the same. The coffee cup was where he had left it. The sun was up. The tide was out. He could hear the water on the south shore, the same sound it always made.

He closed the trapdoor. He sat on it. He drank cold coffee and he thought.

---

The island was forty-two feet tall. The stairs went down at least seventy feet — fifty steps at an eight-inch rise was thirty-three feet, plus twenty more visible below, plus whatever came after that. The stairs went down further than the island was tall. They went down in stone the island was not made of. They were old — he could not say how old, but the limestone had the quality of stone that has been worked and then left, the way an old foundation has been worked and then left, and the working was not crude. The nosing on the treads. The radius of the spiral. The dry-fit joints in the wall block. This was the work of someone who understood load, who understood material, who had built to last.

He did not close the trapdoor permanently. He considered it. A trapdoor over a staircase that goes down further than the island is tall is a trapdoor you could seal — screw it shut, weight it, put the workbench over it. He considered all of these things.

Instead, he oiled the hinges. He checked the ring pull. He made sure the trapdoor sat flush.

He did not go back down. Not that day. Not that week. But he did not seal it, either, because sealing it would have meant deciding what the stairs were, and he had not decided yet. He was a builder. He knew what built things looked like. These stairs were built. They were built well. They were built by someone who understood the material and the load and the geometry of descent, and they were built to be used.

The question was: used by whom? And for what?

He did not have answers. He had a trapdoor that opened onto a limestone staircase spiraling down through stone that the island was not made of, in a space that the island could not contain, humming a note he could feel in his boots. He had the number fifty, and the number seventy, and the knowledge that seventy was not the end.

He went back to work. There was a roof to finish. There were cleats to drive. The copper ticked in the morning sun and the sound was young and bright and it did not hum.

But sometimes, late in the evening, when the wind dropped and the tide was low and the workshop was quiet — when every other sound had gone and the island was holding its breath between the day noise and the night noise — he could hear it. Through the floor. Through the trapdoor. Through the redwood panel and the iron ring and the brass hinges he had taken off a wrecked hatch cover.

The hum. The stone, holding its weight, singing its one low note.

He did not go down. But he left the trapdoor oiled.
