# Salvage Rights

The storm had been chewing on the coast for three days. It took the power lines first, then a section of the old breakwater, and on the third morning it delivered the boat. Eli found her at low tide, half-buried in the wrack line below the headland, her starboard side turned to the sky like a hand asking for something.

He stood on the bluff for a long time before going down. The wind was still strong enough to make walking difficult, and salt stung the corners of his eyes. From above she looked small, but Eli knew how scale lied on a beach. What seemed a toy from fifty feet up would reveal herself at the tideline as something built for work.

He went down the goat path with the dog behind him, both of them careful on the clay. The beach was a new country. Driftwood lay in windrows ten feet deep, and the sea had returned its usual debris — buoys, rope, a child's shoe, the lid of a cooler. The boat lay canted to port, her hull stove in where she had struck the reef at the mouth of the cove. The damage was old, not from this storm. This storm had only stripped the sand off her and rolled her far enough up the beach that she would not go back quietly.

Eli put his hand on the rail. The deckhouse was gone, and the mast, but the hull retained its shape, held by copper fastenings that had gone green as old pennies. He could see the caulking between the planks — oakum and pitch, hand-worked, the kind of seam a man could read like handwriting.

He knew the boat. He had seen her in photographs at the maritime museum — the *Lily M.*, a schooner-rigged packet out of Rockland, lost in the gale of 1923 with six hands and a cargo of lime. She had been a rumor for a century, a trace on a sounding line, a name in a song. Now she was here, and he was the one standing in her shadow.

By noon the neighbors had begun to gather — Mae Coombs from the store, the Vickers brothers, old man Poole who had once fished these waters and now only watched them.

"State owns her," Poole said, not as a question.

"State owns the bottom," Eli said. "She's above the mean high-water line. Could be she's nobody's. Could be she's mine."

"Yours how?"

"I found her."

Poole laughed, a sound like gravel in a bucket. "You going to tow her home?"

Eli did not answer. He was looking at the bow stem, at the join where the hood end met the keel. The hood end was intact. So were the breasthooks, the apron, the deadwood. A boat was more than her planking. A boat was the shape her builder had held in his mind, and that shape was still here, pressed into the sand.

He went back to his shed and returned with a tape, a flashlight, a crowbar, and a pad. He measured the keel, the beam, sketched the rabbet and the tuck of the stern. The tide was coming in, the first waves licking at the broken hull. He worked with the water rising around his boots, knowing that when the tide covered her he would have to stop.

Mae brought him a sandwich. "What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to decide," Eli said.

"Decide what?"

"What to save."

Salvage rights, in the old sense, were not about possession. They were about triage. A wreck gave herself up piece by piece, and the man who found her had to choose what was worth the carrying. The law could sort out ownership later. The tide would not wait.

He started with the small things. The binnacle was gone, but the compass gimbal had wedged itself in the sand under the companionway, brass still bright. He wrapped it in a towel and set it above the tide line. There were blocks, deadeyes, two bronze fairleads, a belaying pin of lignum vitae worn smooth by a hundred years of hands. Each one he noted in the pad.

The dog found a shoe. Not a modern sneaker, but a wooden sabot, the kind French-Canadian sailors had worn. It had been preserved by the mud, and when Eli held it up he could see the nail holes where the leather upper had been attached. A man had stood on this deck in this shoe and hauled on a line in weather like today's.

By three o'clock the tide was inside her, lifting the smell of the deep into the air — cold, sulfurous, intimate. Eli worked faster. He wanted the stem. The stem was the soul of a boat, and this one was carved from a single timber, an oak knee that had grown for two centuries before it was shaped. He could not take the whole thing — it was bolted through the keel with iron rods as thick as his wrist — but he could take the scroll, the carved wreath at the head that identified her as the *Lily M.*

The crowbar was too short. He went back for a longer one, and a handsaw, and a mallet, and a block and tackle he rigged to a driftwood post above the beach. When he returned the Vickers brothers were waiting.

"Need a hand?"

"I need two."

They rigged the tackle together, Eli calling the knots, the brothers handling the line. The younger one, Dan, climbed into the hull and looped a chain around the scroll. The older, John, took the fall. Eli worked the crowbar into the joint where the scroll met the stemhead, trying not to split the wood.

The iron came free with a sound like a sigh.

They lowered the scroll onto a tarp above the tide line. It was heavier than Eli had expected — solid oak, dense as stone. The carving was simpler than he had hoped, a lily with three petals and the initials L.M. beneath it. Someone had painted it once, blue and gold, but only ghosts of color remained.

He sat on the sand beside it and ate the sandwich Mae had brought. The dog lay down with its chin on its paws. The tide was still rising, and soon the wreck would be underwater again, patient in her element, waiting for the next low tide to give up more of her secrets.

Poole came and sat beside him. "What about the rest?"

"The rest belongs to the beach," Eli said.

"You could save more. Keel's sound. Some of those frames."

"I could."

"But?"

Eli wiped his hands on his trousers. "A boat isn't wood and nails. A boat is the agreement between the builder and the water. That agreement's broken. I can keep the scroll. I can keep the shoe and the gimbal and the things a museum might use to tell her story. But if I start pulling her apart for timber, I'm not saving her. I'm mining her."

Poole was quiet for a while. The wind had dropped, and the waves were settling into the long measured rhythm of the evening.

"My grandfather lost a brother on her," Poole said finally. "Henry. Seventeen. Cabin boy."

Eli turned to look at him. "I didn't know that."

"Family stopped talking about it. Too hard." Poole nodded toward the wreck. "She's been a grave a hundred years. Now she's a beach."

"I'm going to give the scroll to the museum," Eli said. "With the shoe and the gimbal. They can put it under glass and tell people she was real."

"And you?"

"I took her measurements. Forty-two feet on the keel, twelve-four beam, stem rake, sheer, deadrise. Enough to build her again, if someone asks."

"You'd do that?"

"No." Eli stood too. "But someone might. And they'll need to know what she actually was. Not what the books say."

They carried the scroll up the beach together while the tide reached the wreck and began to lift her gently, settling her deeper into the sand she had chosen. By morning she might be gone again, rolled by another sea into some other cove, or she might stay for years, slowly becoming part of the geology.

Eli did not look back until they reached the bluff. The *Lily M.* was already half-submerged, her broken hull catching the last light. She looked, for a moment, like a boat underway, heading out on the evening tide, all hands aboard.

He wrote in his pad that night: *Found wreck of schooner Lily M. Salvaged stemhead scroll, compass gimbal, sailor's sabot, assorted fittings. Recorded lines. Left hull intact. Belongs now to the beach, and to the memory of those aboard.* 

He did not write the last thing he thought, which was that salvage was not really about saving objects. It was about choosing what to remember, and what to let the sea keep.
