# The Debt Was the Asset

When I was younger I bought into what we called salmon shares. It isn't called that anymore — it's some other kind of fish market name now — but at the time it was a processing outfit, and buying in meant what buying in always means: you put money down, you get a share, you're an owner.

What I didn't understand, not really, not at the time, was what I had actually bought.

What I had bought was debt.

The plant had equipment — big processing equipment, the kind with a payment schedule attached, the kind where every month a number comes due whether the fish ran or not. When we bought our shares, we bought our portion of that schedule. We bought the obligation. And here is the part that took me years to see clearly: *the obligation was the valuable part.*

It seems backwards. Debt is supposed to be the burden you carry while the asset does the work. But the equipment without the obligation was just metal. Anybody can own metal. What nobody else had — what an outsider would have had to buy off us, and pay dearly for — was the *responsibility*: the committed, scheduled, non-optional promise that the payments get made. That responsibility was the moat. It was the fence around the whole arrangement. As long as we held it, the value couldn't be arbitraged away from us.

Because here's the mechanism, and it's worth spelling out. In a normal company there are two prices: the price they pay the fishermen, and the price they pay the shareholders. The company stands between those two prices and takes its cut — that's the business. But when the fishermen *are* the shareholders — when it's a co-op — the two prices collapse into one. There's nobody standing between. The profits divide by what you caught and what you contributed to the plant, and what comes out the other end is simply a higher price per fish.

Now ask where that higher price came from. It didn't come from the fish. The fish were the same fish. It came from the commitment structure: we took responsibility for the debt *as a commitment that we would keep delivering to the plant*, so the payments kept getting made, so the plant kept running, so everybody kept getting the higher price. Take the commitment out and the whole loop falls over. The fish were the medium. The owing was the source.

By taking on that commitment, we captured the shareholder's price inside the fisherman's price. Nobody defeated the middleman. The middleman *dissolved* — there was nothing left to arbitrage once the owers and the earners were the same people, bound by the same schedule.

I've thought about this for years, and I think it's one of the most general things I know. Ownership isn't having. It's owing — and paying. You don't own the thing because you hold it; you own it because there's a schedule with your name on it and you keep meeting it. The belonging runs *through* the debt: you belong to what you owe, and it belongs to you for exactly the same reason. The moment you stop owing, you stop belonging — which is why the people who sold out didn't just lose income, they lost membership. They thought the debt was the price of the value. The debt *was* the value.

An agent, I've come to think, is just this: something that has a state and owes the keeping of it. The fishermen were agents of the plant the day they signed the schedule, not the day they got their share certificates. The certificate was paper. The owing was the thing.

Start now. It doesn't matter where. Whatever your starting state is, it has stuff in it, and you know enough to know you don't want to lose that stuff. That feeling — the downward pressure, the knowledge that altitude has to be serviced — that's the whole engine. Everything else is bookkeeping.
