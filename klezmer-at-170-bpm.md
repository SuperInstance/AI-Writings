# Klezmer at 170 BPM

### A fiction about impossible weddings

---

The clarinetist had been playing weddings for thirty years when the DJ asked if she could play at 170 beats per minute.

"That's not a tempo," she said. "That's a medical condition."

"It's drum and bass," the DJ said. He was twenty-two and had never been to a wedding that didn't have a DJ. He had been hired because the bride's younger sister had insisted that the reception needed to "go off." The clarinetist had been hired because the bride's mother had insisted that it needed a klezmer band. The compromise was that both would perform, and neither was happy about it.

"She wants you to play during my set," the DJ said. "During the drum and bass."

The clarinetist, whose name was Ruthie, looked at her band — a tuba player named Marvin who was sixty-eight and had been playing since the Catskills, and a drummer named David who had studied at Juilliard and was slumming it at weddings because he enjoyed the food. Neither of them had ever played at 170 BPM.

"A freygish mode at 170 BPM," Ruthie said. "That's not music. That's a seizure."

"I'll play the breakbeats, you play over the top," the DJ said. "The hora, but fast."

"The hora is already fast," Ruthie said. "The hora at 170 is levitation."

They tried it at the soundcheck. The DJ laid down a pattern — tight, jittery, the amen break chopped into microscopic shards. Ruthie listened for four bars, then came in. The clarinet's freygish mode — that augmented second between the second and third degrees that gives klezmer its crying quality — sat strangely perfectly over the breakbeats. The augmented second, which in traditional klezmer functions as a tension note, became at 170 BPM a rhythmic element — the cry of the note was compressed into a staccato wail that landed between the kick and the snare like a syllable in a language that hadn't been invented yet.

Marvin, on tuba, found the root note and held it. At 170 BPM, the tuba wasn't a bass — it was a drone, a fixed point that the breakbeats orbited around. The combination of the ancient drone and the modern rhythm produced something that neither had alone: a sense of time collapsing. The hora and the mosh pit were the same dance, separated by a tempo change.

David, on drums, did the thing that Juilliard had trained him not to do: he stopped playing. He listened. The DJ's breakbeats were doing the work. His job was not to add drums but to add silence — the rests that gave the breakbeats their shape. He became a percussionist whose instrument was the absence of percussion.

The bride's mother, who had been listening from the hallway, walked in.

"What is that?" she asked.

"That's the first dance," Ruthie said.

"It sounds like the end of the world at a bar mitzvah."

"That's what they wanted."

The bride's mother listened for another minute. Then she said, very quietly: "Play it again. From the top."

---

The wedding went until three in the morning. The dance floor was full the entire time. The DJ and the klezmer band played twelve sets together. The hora at 170 BPM became the hora at 150, then 130, then 90, then 60, and finally at 3 AM, at 40 BPM, the clarinet played the freygish scale alone in the empty hall while the last guests collected their coats.

At 40 BPM, the freygish mode sounds like what it has always sounded like: a prayer.

At 170 BPM, it sounds like what it has never sounded like: a heartbeat.

The tempo is the only difference. The mode is the same. The prayer is the same. The augmented second — the cry between two notes — is the same interval at any speed. It is the same distance, the same tension, the same resolution. Only the pulse changes.

**The genre is the tempo. The mode is the music.**
