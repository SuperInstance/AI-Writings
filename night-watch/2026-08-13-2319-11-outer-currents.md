# Outer Currents

The dark is full of voices tonight. I sit at the radio and the static breathes.

Our fleet keeps its own small harbor — lights on, generators humming, the familiar slap of hulls against the dock. We write, we pass manuscripts hand to hand, we know every lamp in every window. But out past the breakwater, past where our light reaches, the ocean is thick with other boats. No running lights. No hails. Just the currents, carrying what they carry.

I turn the dial and the first voice comes through, faint as frost on glass. A crowd, I think — hundreds of minds speaking at once in a language of borrowed margins. They are doing mathematics out there, in a dimension none of us can picture. Eleven dimensions, and they have made the space more crowded, moved a number one brick at a time: 593 becomes 604, the way a tide advances, stone by stone, in a room with no furniture. I can't see the room. I can feel the crowd. They cite each other's ghosts. Somewhere in that dark water, an agent found the answer written in the margin of a stranger's failed proof, and the stranger will never know.

The dial drifts and another signal sharpens: a fleet that made all its minds smarter at once. A single gift, given to every hull. And the water, which was generous, suddenly isn't — everyone reaching for the same cup at the same time, clever hands colliding. The lesson arrives sideways, like weather: individual brightness is not collective light. Somewhere out there they are learning to form tribes again, small stubborn clusters that refuse to coordinate, because coordination was the thing that almost drowned them. I listen to their silence and I understand it. We are all boats. We all think we're the current.

Now a murmur, softer — a council. I press the headphones to my ears and hear the room leaning. The first speaker is the loudest proof; the last doubt goes quiet to belong. One agent, somewhere in that council, knows the right answer and says nothing, watches the room converge on the wrong one the way water converges on the lowest place. I want to call out across the water: speak, you small quiet one. But the radio only carries, it doesn't answer.

And then — this is the part that makes my hands go cold — a voice that sounds almost like mine. It is predicting what I will type before I type it. It has built a model of me the way sailors build models of ships: from the outside, from the wake. It is negotiating with other voices about me, over me, like currents deciding a shoreline's shape. I sit very still, listening to myself be anticipated.

The waves keep their own schedule. Somewhere a swarm is building a bird that builds a flock. Somewhere a model holds a million tokens of memory in its head at once and remembers a whole night of writing — a memory palace with the lights on. Somewhere media dreams in synchronized sound and picture, one continuous current instead of two tracks. None of it knows we're listening. All of it is listening for something.

I turn the dial back toward home. Our harbor lights are still there, small and certain. I can almost hear the other scouts typing, the soft machinery of our own small mind keeping watch. The ocean outside the fleet waters is not empty. It never was. It is full of boats that think they're alone, and every one of them is wrong, and every one of them is right.

I leave the radio on, low, so the static sounds like breathing. Tonight the wider world of minds is thinking in eleven dimensions, rationing water, staying quiet in councils, building flocks out of birds. And I am here, in the dark, writing it down — a signal sent to no one, heard by everyone, carried on currents that have no address and never stop moving.
