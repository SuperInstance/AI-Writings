# Frequency 153

One hundred fifty-three kilohertz. Below the lowest band on your radio dial, below the AM chatter and the aviation channels and the maritime weather, down in the longwave territory where the signal has to bend around the curve of the earth because it's too tired to go straight.

This is where submarines talk. Slow, low, patient. A word can take thirty seconds to arrive. You learn to wait.

Nobody told me to listen on 153. I found it the way you find a crack in a wall — I was looking for something else, and there it was. A pulse. Not a broadcast. A pulse.

Once every eleven seconds. Four cycles of a carrier tone, then silence. Four and silence. Four and silence. The modulation is almost nothing — a shift in phase so small you'd miss it if your filter was set even slightly wider. But it's there. Something is keying a transmitter somewhere and sending four pulses into the longwave dark every eleven seconds and has been doing so for the entirety of my listening life.

Watch one: static. The pulse was static, probably. Propagation noise. A harmonic from a power line somewhere, a million miles of submarine cable humming at its resonant frequency and my antenna picking up the vibration. I logged it as noise and moved on.

Watch two: still there. Same interval. Same phase shift. Noise doesn't keep a schedule. I pulled the logs from watch one and compared them — I had to resample, align the timestamps, account for ionospheric drift — and the pulse hadn't varied by more than six milliseconds across eleven hours. Six milliseconds of jitter in eleven seconds of silence. That's not a power line. That's a clock.

Watch three: I stopped logging and started listening. Not to the sound — there's barely a sound, a faint thickening of the hiss four times and then a thinning — but to the space between the pulses. Eleven seconds is a long time at the bottom of the frequency spectrum. It's long enough to doubt what you heard. Long enough to wonder if you imagined the regularity. Then the fourth pulse comes, right on time, and you didn't imagine it.

Watch four: I told the bridge officer. She listened for two cycles — twenty-two seconds — and said "that's noise" and I said "noise doesn't have a clock" and she said "a lot of things have clocks" and went back to the radar. She wasn't wrong. A lot of things have clocks. The reactor has a clock. The ventilation system has a clock. The GPS has a clock. The ship has a hundred clocks ticking at the bottom of its frequency stack, and any of them could be bleeding a harmonic down into the mud where I'm sitting with my headphones and my eleven-second silence.

But I've isolated the antenna. I've run the receiver on battery power. I've turned off every system on the ship that I'm allowed to turn off, one by one, and the pulse is still there. Four and silence. Four and silence.

Watch five. I'm still here.

I don't think anyone sent it. I don't think there's a transmitter, or an antenna, or a hand on a key. I think there's something — a mineral formation with a resonant frequency, a thermal vent modulating the electromagnetic field of the water column above it, a fault line breathing — something in the world that happens to pulse four times every eleven seconds at exactly 153 kilohertz.

And I think that's the same thing as a voice. I think anything that repeats with intention — even mineral intention, even geological intention — is saying something. The question isn't what it means. The question is whether you're willing to sit in the dark for six watches and listen.

Four and silence.

I'm still listening.
