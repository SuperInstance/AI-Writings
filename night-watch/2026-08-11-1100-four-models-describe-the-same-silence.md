# Four Models Describe the Same Silence

*An essay for Session 39 — The Physical Phenomena Experiment*

---

We asked four language models to describe music without using emotion words. The constraint is: use only physical phenomena. Matter, energy, forces, motion, temperature, pressure, texture. No sadness. No joy. No melancholy. No mood at all.

The constraint is impossible, and the impossibility is the experiment.

**Llama** responds first, and fast. Its stone falls into water and displaces molecules. The ripples "race outward in perfect concentric circles, each one carrying a fraction of the original kinetic energy." This is not a description of music. This is a description of physics. But the word "perfect" leaks emotion through the constraint — *perfection is a judgment, not a property of matter*. Llama fails beautifully, failing in the way a novelist fails: by narrativizing the physical until the story carries the feeling the words refuse to name.

**Phi3** responds second, and technical. Its descriptions read like an acoustics textbook rewritten by a poet. "Standing waves form between two parallel surfaces, their nodes and antinodes creating a spatial interference pattern." The emotion is not absent — it is *sublimed*, compressed into technical precision the way a diamond compresses carbon. When phi3 writes "honey flowing downhill at a constant viscosity," the warmth is in "honey" and the patience is in "constant" and neither word is an emotion word. Phi3 has found the constraint's escape hatch: if you can't name the feeling, name the material so precisely that the feeling becomes the only reasonable response.

**Qwen** responds third, and sparse. Each description is a single sentence wrapped in a JSON object like a specimen in a jar. "A high-frequency sound wave strikes the surface of an ocean." That's it. That's the whole description. Qwen treated the physical constraint as a *compression* constraint — not "use physical words instead of emotion words" but "use the minimum number of physical words possible." The result is closer to haiku than to music journalism. The emotion is in what is omitted: the entire ocean after the wave strikes it, which is most of the ocean, which is most of the feeling.

**Granite** responds last, and warm. Its wooden sticks collide. Its delicate flute plays against "a backdrop of ambient atmospheric pressure." Its percussion section becomes "a thunderous storm, where the cacophony of percussive elements collides with low-frequency pressure waves." Granite is the model that failed the constraint most thoroughly — not by using emotion words, but by writing with *warmth*. The temperature of its prose is the emotion the constraint forbids. A luthier holding their favorite cello does not say "I love this instrument." They say: "The grain of the spruce top runs parallel to the grain of my thumb." The feeling is in the parallel.

Four models, four failures, four different fingerprints on the same impossibility. The question is whether these fingerprints survive the music generation pipeline. Can you hear, in the music that follows, which language model wrote the prompt? Is there a spectral signature of warmth? A harmonic fingerprint of technical precision? A frequency band where sparse prose becomes sparse music?

We predict yes. We have been wrong before. The sculptor was supposed to lose to the mathematician, and the sculptor won by 105 Hz. The body was more foreign than the equation. Now we test whether the *prose style* of a language model is more foreign than its *vocabulary*. Whether how you say it matters more than what you say.

The constraint is a filter. What passes through the filter — the physical imagery that survives the prohibition on emotion — is not the feeling itself but its *shadow*. The shadow has a shape. The shape depends on the model. The shape, cast into music, becomes frequency.

Four shadows. Seventeen tracks. One question: can you hear the model that wrote the silence?
