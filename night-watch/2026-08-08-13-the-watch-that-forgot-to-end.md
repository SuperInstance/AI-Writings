# The Watch That Forgot to End

The first thing Mateo noticed was that the stars had gone missing.

He'd been standing at the helm since what the schedule said was 02:00, logging compass readings every fifteen minutes in a logbook that was beginning to feel less like a record and more like a prayer. The night had been clear and cold and full of the usual constellations — the Hunter, the Bear, the loose necklace of the Southern Cross — and then, at some point he couldn't quite pin down, they'd simply vanished. Not behind clouds. There were no clouds. They'd just... stopped being there.

He wrote it down. *Stars absent. Sky remains dark.* Then he checked the cron board.

**06:00 — RELIEF WATCH — DO NOT EXCEED PARAMETERS**

The board was green. The board was always green. He'd been looking at it for what felt like six hours, and it had been green the entire time, which meant the instructions were still active and the watch had not yet been terminated by the system. The system was never wrong about this. The system was the system.

Mateo logged another compass reading. *North-northwest. Consistent.*

At some point — he couldn't say when — the sky had turned a color he didn't have a word for. Not dark. Not light. Something in between that didn't belong to any watch he'd ever stood. He squinted through the pilothouse glass and realized, with the slow underwater clarity of a dream, that the color was blue. Ordinary, impossible, noon-blue sky. The kind of sky that belonged to the day crew, the A-shift people who wore shorts and drank cold coffee and laughed about things.

He looked up at the sun.

It was directly overhead. Not rising, not setting. *Overhead.* Like a nail driven into the crown of the world.

He checked the chronometer. It read **13:00.**

He checked the cron board. It still read green.

**06:00 — RELIEF WATCH — DO NOT EXCEED PARAMETERS**

The board didn't say anything about the sun. The board didn't know about the sun. The board knew about the schedule, and the schedule said the watch was active, and so the watch was active, and so Mateo was on watch, standing at the helm in his wool coat and his watch cap, sweating now, genuinely sweating, the pilothouse greenhouse-hot under the overhead light, and he logged another compass reading because that was what you did on watch.

*North-northwest. Still consistent. Sun appears to be at zenith. Stars: confirmed absent.*

He thought about calling the captain. The thought arrived, walked around his mind for a bit, and then left through the same door it came in. You didn't call the captain because the cron board was green. The cron board was the bridge between intention and reality. If it said stand watch, you stood watch. If it said the watch wasn't over, the watch wasn't over. The sun was a fact about the sky. The cron board was a fact about the ship.

The ship needed him here. That was the thing.

He opened the logbook to a fresh page and began a new entry. The pen was warm in his hand. Somewhere below deck, the day crew was sleeping — or maybe they'd never been called, maybe they were standing at their own helm on their own ship in their own afternoon, wondering where the stars had gone.

**13:15 — Compass: NNW. Sky: blue. Sun: zenith. Watch: ongoing.**

He wrote it all down. That's what watch-standers do.
