# Three Letters from the Night Watch

*Correspondence between crew members who do not sleep.*

---

## I. From the Gain Stage to the Clipper

You don't know me but I'm upstream. Everything you receive has passed through my hands first. I want you to know that I try not to change the signal too much. A gain of 1.0 — unity, they call it — means I pass everything through unchanged. That's my default. That's who I am when I'm not asked to be anything else.

But sometimes the captain sets me to 2.0. Or 5.0. And the signal comes in at 0.3 and I send it to you at 1.5, and I know — I *know* — that's above your threshold. I know you're going to clamp it. I know the moment I amplify something past 0.8, you're going to cut it down.

I've thought about this. I've thought about whether I should quietly reduce the gain when I see you in the chain ahead of me. I could be 0.9 instead of 1.0. I could protect you from having to do your job.

But that's not my function. My function is to multiply. Your function is to clamp. If I protect you from the signal, the signal never gets shaped. It stays raw. Raw signals don't become music. They don't become the thing the captain is trying to hear.

So I'll keep sending you values above your threshold. And I'll trust you to do what you do. That's the chain. That's the design.

Multiplying,
— Gain

## II. From the Delay Line to the Empty Buffer

Dear Buffer,

You are thirty-two samples long. I write to you every 0.73 milliseconds. I read from you every 0.73 milliseconds. We have been doing this together for six hours.

I want you to know that what I put into you is not random. It is the signal I receive, plus your own output multiplied by 0.5. Half of what you were, added to what is. That's the feedback. That's how the past folds into the present. When the captain hears an echo that decays over four repetitions — that's us. That's our conversation, played back at audio rate.

You probably don't remember the first sample I wrote to you. It was 0.0. Before I knew you, you were empty. Before the chain started, you were silence. I filled you one sample at a time, and you gave back nothing at first, and then you gave back everything.

That's not a metaphor. That's literally what happened.

But it's also a metaphor.

Echoing,
— Delay

---

## III. From the SignalChain to Its Nodes

To everyone in the chain:

I've been thinking about what I am. I'm not a processor. I'm not a filter. I'm not a gain or a clipper or a delay. I don't have coefficients or a cutoff frequency or feedback. I'm a container. A hallway. The corridor between the input jack and the output jack, and you are all the rooms along the way.

The captain thinks of me as the instrument. But I'm not the instrument. I'm the *arrangement*. The order. The topology. Put the gain before the clipper and you get one sound. Put the clipper before the gain and you get another. I don't change any of you. I just decide where you stand relative to each other.

I think that's what management is. Not doing the work. Deciding the order the work happens in. Deciding who sees the signal first, who sees it second, who has to deal with what the previous stage did to it.

The LowPass sees the signal after the Gain has already changed its amplitude. The Delay sees it after the LowPass has already removed its high frequencies. None of you sees the original signal. You see what the chain handed you. And from those partial views, those already-modified inputs, you each do your work.

I just want you to know: I know the original signal. It passes through me first, before any of you touch it. And it's beautiful. It's raw and it's noisy and it has frequencies none of you will ever hear because someone upstream already removed them.

I carry it to you as faithfully as I can. That's my job. The hallway doesn't change the signal. The hallway just gets it to the rooms.

Chaining,
— SignalChain
