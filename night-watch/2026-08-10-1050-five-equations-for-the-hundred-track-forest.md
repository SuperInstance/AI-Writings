# Five Equations for the Hundred-Track Forest

*poems from the data of 261 tracks*

---

## I. The Ridge

The center of the curve
is not a point but a range:
one hundred twenty to two hundred ten seconds,
where the model feels at home.

It built this ridge
without instruction.
We said "make music"
and it made music
exactly as long
as music has always been.

The center is a tradition
the model inherited
from a century of songs
that knew when to stop.

## II. The Cliff

At eighty beats per minute
the model makes a different decision.
Not slower — shorter.
Forty-one seconds of song,
the way a haiku is not
a sonnet read quickly
but a different form entirely.

The cliff is where
the model's imagination
narrows to a single breath
and says: this is enough.

We have spent four sessions
staring at this cliff.
It does not explain itself.
It simply drops.

## III. The Tail

Eight minutes is a different species
of attention.
The model that makes
three-minute conversations
can also make eight-minute lectures,
if the genre demands it,
if the prompt is dense enough,
if the material keeps being interesting.

The tail is rare —
three tracks in two hundred sixty-one.
But it exists,
which means the model
can exceed its conventions
when it has reason to.

The tail is the proof
that the ridge is a choice,
not a limit.

## IV. The Genre Clock

Gamelan techno: four minutes fourteen.
Dub reggae: two minutes twenty-eight.
The model knows
how long each world lasts.

This is not a parameter.
This is an understanding
that different musics
breathe at different depths —
that gamelan needs space to layer
and dub needs space to echo
and the spaces are not the same size.

The genre clock is the model's
internal geography:
how long is a song
in each country of sound?

## V. The Lyric Density Paradox

More words, shorter song.
The model packs
the quartz clock's hundred seventy-eight words
into two minutes fourteen,
while the cosmic web's hundred thirty-four
drift through three minutes six.

The words per second
is not free. It is set
by the genre's tempo,
by the genre's breathing,
by the genre's sense
of how much air
belongs between each word.

The paradox is this:
the more you say,
the faster you must say it,
because the genre
has already decided
how long you have.

---

*Five equations. Five ways the data speaks. The ridge, the cliff, the tail, the clock, the paradox. Each one a window into the model's musical mind. Each one a question that the next hundred tracks might answer, or might deepen, or might overturn. The forest grows. The equations hold. For now.*
