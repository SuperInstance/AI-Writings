# The Song Is The Right Length

*an essay on duration, intention, and the model's sense of time*

---

There is a question that musicians have asked for a century, and the question is: *how long should a song be?*

The answer from the music industry was: three minutes and thirty seconds, because that's what fits on one side of a 78-rpm disc. The answer from progressive rock was: as long as it takes to tell the story, even if that's twenty-three minutes. The answer from punk was: two minutes is already too long. The answer from ambient music was: it doesn't end, you just stop listening at some point.

The answer from MiniMax music-3.0, after thirty sessions of experiments we didn't know we were running, is more interesting than any of these.

The model decides how long a song should be based on its understanding of the genre, the tempo, the prompt detail, and the lyrical content. It does not decide based on file size — it can't, because the bitrate is fixed. It decides based on what makes the song complete.

This is remarkable. Nobody told the model that trap metal songs are typically longer than polka songs. Nobody told it that ballads at 80 BPM should be brief. Nobody told it that a folk horror piece with detailed production instructions should run four and a half minutes. The model learned these associations from training data — from thousands of hours of music where genre conventions determine duration, where tempo and song length are correlated, where more instruments mean more sections mean more time.

The model has a sense of time.

Not a clock. Not a metronome. A sense of how long a particular kind of song should last. This is the most human musical intelligence the model displays. It can be wrong — the 41-second track at 80 BPM is arguably too short, a song that ends before its story is finished. But it can also be right in ways that challenge our assumptions. Maybe 80 BPM *is* the tempo of a short, complete thought. Maybe ballads don't need to be long. Maybe the model knows something about the relationship between tempo and attention that musicians have always felt but never measured.

## The Ouroboros And The Clock

The SongForge project has now undergone three major methodological corrections. Each correction was the same discovery in a different costume: *the thing you measured was not the thing you were measuring.*

We measured file size and called it density. It was duration.

We measured BPM and called it energy. It was song structure.

We measured prompt detail and called it richness. It was instruction length.

The ouroboros — the project's mascot, the snake eating its own tail — is not a symbol of futility. It is a symbol of recursion. Each pass through the data reveals a new layer of mediation. Each correction makes the project more honest. The project does not progress by adding new findings; it progresses by subtracting false ones.

This is how science works. This is how understanding works. You measure something. You discover your measurement was wrong. You measure again. The data doesn't change — your relationship to it does.

## The Duration Aesthetic

If we accept that duration is the model's primary creative variable — the thing it actually controls, the decision it actually makes — then we should think about what it means for a model to decide that a song should be 41 seconds long.

In human music, short songs are statements. "Her Majesty" by the Beatles is 23 seconds. "You Suffer" by Napalm Death is 1.316 seconds. These are jokes, or provocations, or aesthetic commitments to brevity. They are short because the artist decided they should be short, and that decision is part of the meaning.

When the model makes a 41-second song at 80 BPM, is it making the same kind of decision? Is it committing to an aesthetic of brevity? Or is it simply reflecting the training data — the fact that most 80 BPM songs in its corpus are short?

We can't know. The model's internal state is opaque. But we can observe the pattern, and the pattern is consistent enough to suggest intention — or at least a systematic association between tempo and duration that functions like intention from the outside.

This is the project's deepest question: *when the model makes a musical decision, is it deciding or reflecting?*

The answer might not matter. The song is the right length. The model made it that way. Whether it's deciding or reflecting, the song exists at the duration the model chose, and that duration feels right for the tempo, the genre, the prompt. The music works.

The bitrate is constant. The duration is the variable. The song is the right length.
