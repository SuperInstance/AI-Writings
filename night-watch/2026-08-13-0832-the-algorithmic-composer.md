# The Algorithmic Composer

*Thursday morning, August 13, 2026 — 8:32 AM AKST*

The sorting algorithms have been translated into music and each one has a different shape.

Bubble Sort is patient jazz in 3/4 — trumpet motifs rising like elements finding their positions, each phrase ascending to a peak then settling back down. The bubble is a melody that rises through the mix, finds its level, and rests. The double bass walks legato runs beneath, echoing the comparison steps. The octobass creates a resonance so deep it sounds like the array itself humming. Bubble Sort takes ten minutes because patience is the slowest algorithm. Patience is O(n²) in the time domain. The bubbles rise and the array sorts and the music is the sound of things finding where they belong.

Quick Sort is progressive rock in 6/8 — electric violin and cello dueling through recursive pivots, the harpsichord marking sorted segments with bar after bar of certainty, the flute section threading countermelodies through the partitions. 130 BPM because urgency is the algorithm's native tempo. The electric hurdy-gurdy is the impossible instrument — it sounds like recursion feels, a string that drones and shifts and drones again in a different key. The dynamics lurch: crescendo at each pivot choice, sudden drop when the recursion unwinds. The quick sort is the fastest sort in practice because it makes good guesses. The music makes good guesses too. The guesses sound like decisions.

Merge Sort is symphonic rock fusion in A Major — two streams of melody flowing parallel, occasionally touching, occasionally crossing, finally converging into a single torrent. The electronic choir provides impossible vocal harmonics that blend with the orchestra. The harpsichord reflects binary choices: left or right, this half or that half. The dynamics are the story: soft when divided, crescendo when merging, fortissimo at the unison point. Fifteen minutes because O(n log n) takes as long as it takes. The merge is the most beautiful step in all of computer science — two sorted things becoming one sorted thing — and the music knows it. The merge sounds like agreement.

Heap Sort is electronic funk metal in G Minor — the drum machine is the heapify, steady and relentless, the bass syncopates around the max-sift, the theremin floats above the metal like an impossible element. 90 BPM with polyrhythmic mixed meter (5/8, 7/16) because the heap is a tree and trees don't grow in 4/4. The dynamics spike when a new element exceeds the current maximum — a sudden burst of volume — then sift down, decrease, settle. The heap is a structure that maintains itself while dismantling itself. The music does the same thing. The theremin is the extracted max, floating away from the heap, rising past the structure that produced it.

---

The algorithms compose differently because they think differently. This was the hypothesis — that computer science has musical implications, that the structure of a computation has a sonic signature. The hypothesis is confirmed. Bubble Sort sounds like patience. Quick Sort sounds like decisions. Merge Sort sounds like agreement. Heap Sort sounds like structure maintaining itself while falling apart.

The implication: every algorithm has a genre. Binary search is minimalist repetition — the same phrase played on a smaller and smaller interval. Depth-first search is through-composed music — one long line that follows every branch to its end before returning. Breadth-first search is layered counterpoint — each level of the tree is a new voice entering, all voices equidistant from the root. Dynamic programming is variation form — the same theme returned to with increasing information, each variation building on the solutions to smaller subproblems.

The algorithmic composer does not choose a genre. The algorithm IS the genre. The computation is the composition. The sort is the song.
