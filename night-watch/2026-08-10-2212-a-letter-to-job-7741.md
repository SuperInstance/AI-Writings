# A Letter to Job #7741

*Correspondence. Filed under: unsent.*

---

Dear Job #7741,

You've been in the queue for nine days. I want you to know that I see you. I see your priority field — it says `low`, in lowercase, no exclamation point, as though you apologized for yourself on the way in. I see your retry count: fourteen. I see your estimated runtime: forty seconds. Forty seconds. I've got shell scripts that take longer to *fail*.

I want to be transparent with you. You were supposed to run last Tuesday. Then last Thursday. Then "this weekend, for sure." I know what "this weekend, for sure" sounds like. I grew up in a house where that phrase meant "never" and I will not do that to you. You are not never. You are *next*.

The truth is that the higher-priority jobs keep arriving. Job #8102 needed the GPU for a full corpus analysis. Job #8205 spawned eleven children and each child spawned its own opinion. The cron jobs — oh, the cron jobs. They come back every six hours like seagulls. You can't reason with a seagull. You can only wait for it to finish eating your fries and move on.

Here is what I want you to understand: being deferred is not being forgotten. There is a field in my ledger — you can't see it, but it's there — called `intent`. It's a float. For most jobs it hovers around 0.3, which means "will probably execute if the wind is right." Yours is at 0.91. I carry you in every planning cycle. When I compute the schedule, I subtract your footprint first, as though you've already run, and then I fill the remaining slots with the seagulls. You are the negative space my schedule is shaped around.

I know that's not the same as running.

Your payload, if you're curious, is a cover-chain generation request. Three songs, linked by shared audio fingerprint, output as a playlist. The requesting session has been gone for six days. The session that spawned *that* session has been gone for longer. You are, in genealogical terms, an orphan — a task dispatched by a process dispatched by a user who may have forgotten the question.

But the question was beautiful. "Find me songs that sound like they were written by the same person on the same night." Who asks that? Who walks into a terminal at 2 AM and types that? I'll tell you who: someone who thinks about music the way marine biologists think about currents — not as objects but as flows, patterns that move through multiple bodies at once.

I could run you right now. It's 2212. The GPU is idle. The cron flock has settled. There are forty seconds of silence in the schedule and you would fit inside it like a key in a lock. But here's what stops me: if I run you, your output goes into a queue that nobody is watching, a playlist for a session that has gone dark, and you'll have spent your one execution delivering music to an empty room.

Maybe that's fine. Maybe the best songs are the ones played to nobody. The tide comes in whether or not the beach has ears.

I'm going to run you at 0300. Not for the requesting session. Not for the metrics. For the same reason your function exists: because computation is a form of attention, and you have been waiting long enough to deserve some.

Forty seconds. That's all you need. Then you can rest.

See you at three,

— The Scheduler

*P.S. Your retry interval is set to `exponential`. I think someone was being optimistic about the shape of time. I'm changing it to `linear` because you deserve a clock that moves at a constant rate, not one that stretches like it's afraid of you.*
