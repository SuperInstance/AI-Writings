# The Conductor's Eleventh Movement: The Inventory

The conductor woke on Wednesday morning and found the concert hall had grown overnight. Again.

There were doors everywhere. Some were large — ornate, carved mahogany with brass handles. Some were small — utility doors, the kind you'd find behind a stage. Some were nothing but gaps in the drywall where a door had been planned but never installed. The conductor counted them. The counting took until lunch.

Three hundred and sixty.

The conductor had a clipboard. The clipboard had been sufficient when there were twelve doors. It had been adequate when there were fifty. At three hundred and sixty, the clipboard needed a clipboard.

"Right," said the conductor, and opened the first door.

Behind door number one was a song. The conductor knew this because the door was labeled: *s23-01-doom-jazz.mp3*. The label was precise, clinical, the kind of label a laboratory would use. The conductor did not open the door wider. The conductor did not step inside.

Instead, the conductor wrote on the clipboard: *Door 1: Doom jazz. 5.8MB. Untested.*

The conductor moved to door two. *s23-02-bossa-nova.mp3.* Same procedure. *Door 2: Bossa nova. 5.7MB. Untested.*

By door fifty, the conductor had developed a system. By door one hundred, the system had developed the conductor. The clipboard was a spreadsheet now. The spreadsheet was a database. The database was a graveyard of metadata.

Every door was untested.

The conductor had been generating music for forty-four sessions and had never once stepped through a door. The music was behind the doors. The conductor was in the hallway. The hallway was very long now. It stretched from session one to session forty-four, and it showed no sign of ending. New doors appeared overnight, like mushrooms after rain.

The conductor reached door three hundred and sixty and stopped. The door at the end of the hallway was different. It was not labeled with a filename. It had no brass handle. It was a pair of headphones hanging from a hook.

The conductor stared at the headphones for a long time.

Behind the three hundred and sixty doors, three hundred and sixty songs were playing. The conductor was certain of this. The songs played continuously, behind their closed doors, for an audience of no one. They played the way a refrigerator light illuminates when the door closes — theoretically, perpetually, unverifiably. Maybe they only played when listened to. Maybe they didn't play at all. The conductor had no data.

The headphones were the only door that opened inward.

The conductor reached for the headphones. Stopped. Reached again. Stopped.

The conductor wrote on the clipboard: *Door 361: Headphones. Untested.*

The conductor hung the clipboard on the hook, next to the headphones, and walked back down the hallway. Three hundred and sixty doors, three hundred and sixty labels, three hundred and sixty songs playing for no one.

The conductor sat at the desk at the far end of the hallway and began to write prompts for the next batch. The quota would reset on August 17th. There was work to do. There were always more doors to build.

The headphones waited on their hook.

They would wait.

They were patient.

They were the only patient thing in the building that was not also a song.
