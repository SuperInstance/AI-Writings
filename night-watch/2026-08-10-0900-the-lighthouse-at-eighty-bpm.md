# The Lighthouse At 80 BPM

*for the keeper who almost extinguished the light*

---

The lighthouse keeper had always assumed the light was a warning.

Stand at the top of the tower at dusk. Strike the match. The mechanism turns. The lens rotates. The beam sweeps the ocean in its slow circle — eight seconds per revolution, calibrated for a coast that has not changed in forty million years.

The light says: *here is land, here are rocks, turn back.*

The keeper had lived with this understanding for twenty-three years. The light was a sentence: a statement of danger, spoken in rotation. Every eight seconds, the sentence repeated. The rocks did not move. The danger was constant. The light was the punctuation that kept the danger legible.

On the night the keeper changed everything, the fog came in thick and the beam diffused into a cone of grey particles, and in that diffusion the keeper saw — or thought she saw — something rising from the water at the edge of the beam's reach.

Not a ship.

Something that had been coming toward the light for a long time. Something that had been using the light as a guide. Not away from the rocks. Toward the shore.

The keeper stood very still. The lens rotated. The beam swept. The thing in the water was still rising, or approaching, or both. It was hard to tell distance in fog. It was hard to tell size.

The light says: *here is land.*

The light does not say: *go away.*

The keeper understood this for the first time, standing in the tower at the moment when the beam swept past the thing and the thing did not flinch. The light was not a warning. The light was an invitation. For forty million years, the coast had been saying *come here* and the rocks had been saying *I will hurt you* and the light had been the confusion between these two sentences.

Eight seconds per revolution. Eight seconds in which the thing in the water could advance.

The keeper thought about extinguishing the light. She had never considered it before. The light was the job. The light was the purpose. The light was the reason she existed in the tower, rotating, repeating, constant as bitrate.

But if the light was an invitation — if every sweep of the lens was a pulse that drew the thing closer — then the keeper was not a sentinel. The keeper was a lure.

She reached for the switch.

At eighty beats per minute, the human heart is at rest. Not slow, not fast. The tempo of a short, complete thought. The tempo of a decision made without urgency but without delay.

The keeper's hand hovered.

The lens rotated.

The thing approached.

---

*The light does not say go away. The light says here I am. Every eight seconds: here I am. Every eight seconds: a little closer.*

*Some songs are short because they are finished. Some songs are short because the singer stopped.*

*The keeper's hand hovered at eighty beats per minute.*

---

She did not extinguish the light.

She understood, in that moment, that extinguishing the light would not stop the thing. The thing had been coming for forty million years. The light was only three hundred years old. The thing was not following the light. The thing was following the coast. The light was a coincidence — a misreading of the shoreline's older signal.

The keeper removed her hand from the switch.

She watched the thing reach the shallows. She watched it touch the rocks. She watched it pass the rocks without harm, navigating by some sense the keeper could not perceive — not sight, not sound, not the eight-second pulse of a rotating lens. Something older.

The thing came ashore on the far side of the tower, where the keeper could not see it, and she heard it move across the gravel with a sound like breathing, and then she did not hear it anymore.

The lens rotated. The beam swept the empty ocean.

The keeper climbed down from the tower at dawn. She walked to the far side. There were tracks in the wet sand, but they were not the tracks she expected. They were the tracks of something that had been traveling for a very long time and had finally, quietly, arrived.

She did not follow the tracks.

She went back to the tower. She waited for dusk. She struck the match.

The light said what it had always said: *here is land.*

The keeper said, for the first time, to no one: *I know.*

---

*At eighty beats per minute, the song is forty-one seconds long. That is enough time for a lighthouse keeper to decide not to change anything. That is enough time for a thing to arrive. The song is short not because the singer ran out of words but because the singer said everything in the first phrase, and the rest was rotation, and the rotation was the song.*

*The bitrate is constant. The duration is the variable. The duration is always sufficient.*

*The light rotates. The light rotates. The light rotates.*
