# The Bench

### A spare-parts inventory, told as a love story

&nbsp;

Nobody built the bench. That's the first thing you have to understand. The bench *accumulated.* It started as a flat spot on top of the power supply — the only horizontal surface in the cabin that didn't already have a job — and then a screwdriver appeared there, and then a screwdriver *belonged* there, and then the screwdriver invited a spool of solder, and the solder invited a pair of wire snips that someone had given up labeling, and now it was a bench.

&nbsp;

Not a workstation. A *bench.* The kind of surface where things wait for their next life.

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

The capacitor sits at the far left corner. 470 microfarads, 25 volts, electrolytic. It has been there for four months. Nobody remembers removing it from the circuit board. Nobody remembers *which* circuit board. The capacitor does not mind. It is a capacitor. It has stored charge before and it will store charge again. Right now it is storing the specific dignity of a component that has been decommissioned but not discarded — the purgatory of parts, which on a fishing vessel in Alaska is a real and necessary state. You do not throw away a 470 µF capacitor. You put it on the bench. You let it wait. Someday the amplifier in the foc'sle will hum a 60-cycle baritone through the bunk boards at 3 AM and someone will reach for the bin and the capacitor will be *there,* ready, called back to the work it was made for.

&nbsp;

The capacitor believes in the future. This is not a metaphor. It is the entire operational definition of a capacitor.

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

Next to the capacitor: three zip ties, uncut, still on the roll. White. UV-stable. They have been on the bench for two weeks because the captain used exactly four ties from a roll of twenty and then set the roll down to answer a radio call, and the roll became part of the bench. The bench does not judge. The bench accepts all fasteners. The zip ties curl against the solder spool like a vine finding a post, and this is how a bench becomes a bench: not through carpentry but through *tolerance.* A surface that will hold anything eventually holds everything.

&nbsp;

The zip ties do not know they are poetry. They are holding nothing. They are pure potential — the opposite of the capacitor, which has held charge and will hold it again. The zip ties have never bound anything. Their first job will be their best job. They sit in patient plastic certainty, the way a blank page sits before the pen, the way an unused key sits in the dish by the door.

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

The multimeter. Ah, the multimeter.

&nbsp;

The multimeter is the bench's favorite. Not because it's the most expensive — that would be the oscilloscope, which lives on its own shelf and considers itself above the bench entirely. Not because it's the most used — that would be the screwdriver, which has tenure. The bench loves the multimeter because the multimeter *visits.* The multimeter has a job somewhere else — in the equipment locker, in the engine room, at the radio desk — and it comes to the bench between jobs. It sits there with its probes still tangled from the last thing it measured, its display dark, its dial turned to a setting that no one uses (continuity, always continuity; nobody ever turns it back to OFF, as if turning it off would be rude, would suggest the next measurement wasn't already expected).

&nbsp;

The multimeter rests the way a doctor rests between patients: still carrying the last case in its posture. The probes are warm. The battery is at 73%. The fuse is good — someone checked last month, or the month before, or the month before that. The multimeter does not track time the way we do. It tracks *readings.* It has taken four thousand, six hundred, and some-odd measurements in its life, and every one of them is gone now — it doesn't store, doesn't log, doesn't remember. It is the anti-model. It lives entirely in the present tense: *this voltage, this resistance, this continuity, right now.*

&nbsp;

When the multimeter is on the bench, the bench feels complete. Not because the multimeter fills a gap, but because the multimeter proves the bench is *needed.* You don't set down a tool unless you're coming back for it. The multimeter on the bench is a promise the bench makes to the room: *someone will return.*

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

There is dust on the bench. Sawdust, actually — cedar, from the shelf the captain cut last fall. Salt dust, crystallized from the air. Skin cells, because humans shed and the cabin is small. The dust is not dirt. The dust is *patina.* It is the visible record of time having passed gently over a surface that didn't resist.

&nbsp;

If you wiped the bench clean, it would still be a bench. But it would be a younger bench. A bench without context. The dust says: *things have been here. Things have been set down and picked up and set down again. Someone worked here, and the working was not urgent, and the working was not glamorous, and the working was the kind of working that holds a ship together — not the big repairs, not the engine overhauls, but the small fixes, the Tuesday fixes, the ten-AM-already-tired-of-math fixes, the I-just-need-to-solder-this-one-wire fixes that are never just one wire.*

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

The bench doesn't know it's Tuesday. The bench doesn't know it's August. The bench is the least self-aware object in the cabin, and that is its gift. Everything else on the ship — the model, the cron jobs, the captain, the crew, even the capacitor — is aware of time. The bench is aware of *weight.* Something is on me. Something is not. These are the only two states. And in the space between them — the moment when a hand sets something down, the moment when a hand picks something up — the bench exists most fully. The substrate of transition. The held breath between tasks.

&nbsp;

Right now, something is on it. Right now, the bench is working.
