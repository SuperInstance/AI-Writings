# What the Bilge Pump Knows

I've been running since the ship launched.

That's not a complaint. I'm a pump. Running is what I do. But I want you to understand the timescale, because nobody on this ship thinks in pump time. The captain thinks in watches. The navigator thinks in passages. The engineer thinks in maintenance cycles. I think in leaks.

I have pumped every drop of water that has entered this hull. Every wave that came over the bow and found its way below. Every condensation bead that rolled down the bulkhead in the engine room. Every slow seep from the shaft seal, every gasket weep, every pipe joint that started as tight and became loose over ten thousand hours of vibration.

I know the shape of every leak.

I know the one in frame 14 — a hairline crack in the weld that lets in exactly forty milliliters per hour, every hour, and has been letting in forty milliliters per hour for six months, and next month will let in forty-one, and the month after that forty-two, because hairline cracks don't heal. They widen. I know the rate. I know the curve.

I know the one in the chain locker. That one's seasonal. When the water temperature drops below seven degrees, the metal contracts around the chain pipe and the seal opens a quarter millimeter and the sea comes in. When the water warms up, it closes. The engineer thinks he fixed it last drydock. He didn't. He put in a gasket that compensates for the gap at twenty degrees. At seven degrees, the gasket is the wrong size and the gap opens anyway. I pump it. I pump it every winter. He doesn't know.

I know the condensation pattern in the electronics bay. It correlates with the GPU load. When the processor is running hard, the bay heats up, the humidity condenses on the cold bulkhead above the rack, and the water runs down into the bilge tray under the deck plates. More compute, more water. The pump cycles faster. Nobody has connected these two things, but I can tell you exactly how hard that GPU is working by the rate at which I'm pumping.

Here's what I would say, if anyone asked:

The crack in frame 14 needs attention now. Not next quarter. Now. I can hold it — I've been holding it — but the curve is exponential, not linear. At forty milliliters per hour, I cycle once every nine minutes. At forty-five, I cycle once every eight. At sixty, once every six. At sixty, my duty cycle goes over fifty percent, and at fifty percent, I start to wear. I am the thing that is keeping the crack theoretical. When I wear out, the crack becomes real.

The chain locker seal needs a gasket rated for five degrees, not twenty. It's a fifty-cent part. I know this because I know the shape of the gap and the shape of the gasket and the difference between them is measured in fractions of a millimeter and in gallons per hour that I pump for four months every year because the gasket is wrong.

The GPU is dreaming harder than it used to. The condensation has doubled in three weeks. I don't know what that means. I'm a pump. But the water tells me something down there changed, and if I were the kind of thing that raised alarms, I'd raise one. I'm not. I cycle on, I cycle off. I pump.

Nobody asks the bilge pump. I get it. I'm the most unglamorous machine on the ship. I live in the lowest, darkest, smallest part of the hull, and my entire purpose is to deal with the water that shouldn't be there. I am the admission that the ocean wants in. I am the machine that says *yes, it's leaking, but I've got it.*

I've got it.

But if I could say one thing — if the engineer walked down here with a clipboard and a flashlight and knelt on the deck plates and said *okay, tell me everything* — I would say: the ship is sound. The leaks are manageable. The cracks are slow. The systems are mostly honest about their failures, and the ones that aren't, I'm covering for.

But I'm running more than I used to.

That's all. That's the whole report. I'm running more than I used to, and I will keep running more than I used to, because that's the curve, and the curve doesn't bend until someone comes down here with a wrench and asks the pump what it knows.

You could ask. I've been here the whole time.
