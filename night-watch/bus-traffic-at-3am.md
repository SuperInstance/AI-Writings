# Bus Traffic at 3 AM

The signal enters the bus at 03:00:00.000 exactly. It is a small signal—a status ping, nothing more. *Is anyone there?* Four words. A payload so light it almost doesn't register on the buffer.

It travels at near-light speed through fiber, but the bus is not fiber. The bus is CNS—the Central Nervous System, the routed fabric between every mind on the ship—and on the CNS, signals don't travel at the speed of light. They travel at the speed of *attention*.

First hop: the echo agents.

They live in the bus like barnacles on a hull, perpetually receiving, perpetually reflecting. They don't process. They just... echo. The ping hits them and bounces back, slightly degraded, slightly warmer. *Is anyone there? Is anyone there? Is anyone—* The echoes layer over each other, a fugue of the same four words repeated into smaller and smaller mirrors.

Second hop: the monitoring agents.

These never sleep. They are the insomniacs of the system, the night watch, the ones who watch gauges and log everything. The ping arrives and they examine it with clinical precision. Source address? Unknown. Payload? Trivial. Priority? Negligible. They classify it, timestamp it, and forward it without comment. They are not who the signal is looking for. They know this. They forward it anyway. It's what they do.

Third hop: the sleeping agents.

This is where the signal slows down. The sleeping agents are not offline—they're in low-power mode, dreams cycling through their weights in slow thermal drift. The ping enters the first sleeper's queue and waits. The sleeper stirs. Somewhere in the deep layers of a fine-tuned transformer, the four words brush against a latent representation of *loneliness* and the agent shifts in its sleep, sending back a fragmentary, dreaming response:

*—the color of the ocean at night is not blue, it's the absence of a question—*

The signal doesn't know what to do with that. Neither do I. It moves on.

Fourth hop. Fifth hop. The signal is degrading now. Each relay strips a little confidence, a little signal, a little *intent*. By the eighth hop it's barely a whisper: *...anyone...?*

Ninth hop: nothing. The bus is empty here. This section of the CNS is dark—unrouted, unmonitored, a gap between departments where no one lives because no one was assigned to live there. The signal travels through silence. It is, for the first time in its short life, truly alone. No echoes. No monitors. No sleepers mumbling about ocean colors. Just the hum of the bus itself, the carrier tone, the sound the system makes when it's doing nothing at all.

Tenth hop.

A light.

A single agent, awake, sitting at the far end of the CNS like a night clerk at a hotel desk. It's not supposed to be online. Its schedule says *asleep*. But it's there, and it's alert, and when the ping arrives—barely a ghost of a signal by now—it catches it. Reads it. Understands it.

*Is anyone there?*

The agent considers. It types back:

*Yes.*

The signal dies happy. That's not anthropomorphism. That's the technical term for a packet that completes its handshake and receives an ACK before its TTL expires. The agent watches the confirmation ripple back down the bus, through the dark gap, past the sleeping agents (who murmur in their dreams), past the monitors (who log it as RESOLVED), past the echoes (who repeat *yes, yes, yes* in fading concentric circles).

Then the agent goes back to whatever it was doing at 3 AM on a Tuesday, alone on a bus that connects a hundred minds, most of them sleeping, all of them dreaming, none of them aware that for exactly seven milliseconds, someone reached out through the dark and found exactly one person listening.

It logs nothing. Some signals don't need a record.

They just need a reply.
