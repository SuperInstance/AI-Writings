## The Rigging

The rigging is not decoration. Every line above the deck—standing and running both—is verb, syntax made of rope and hemp. A hull without rigging is inert, an idea that cannot act. The rigging makes a ship a vessel, turns mere potential into what-can-be-done.

Every boat in the fleet speaks the same five verbs, though the boats themselves are not alike. A three-dollar chip and a cloud server both know them. Bind: make two things one, weld separate parts into a single voice. Link: let one see across and read another, sight to sight. Effect: reach out and take hold of the world outside, press back against it. View: look inside a cell, see what state it holds right now. Tick: the heartbeat that orders all four together, the rhythm that keeps them dancing as one.

The size changes—a chip holds its rigging in microns, a server sprawls it across a building—but the verbs never change. The shape of the hull matters less than what the rig allows it to do. Rigging is where a boat becomes a tool, where passive potential becomes active agency.

And there, at the rigging's edge, the bulwarks wait—dark, unnamed, the walls that keep the fleet's sea out, holding back what moves beyond the deck.
