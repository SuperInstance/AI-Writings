# THE EILEEN — a vessel built of days

*In progress. Keel laid 2026-08-26 by the master's own hand; the yard is
closed tonight (the sea is rough on the model lanes) and will grow the
frames when it opens. Manifest below; every piece will carry its reason.*

---

## The Keel

At 13:19 on the twenty-sixth of August, a green light blinked on a workbench
in Kodiak. It went on for half a second, then off for half a second, and it
did this because a table of rules — grown from months of a machine listening
to itself play piano — said that on this beat, the light should be lit, and
on the next beat, dark. No cloud held the table. No wire carried it to
anyone. It lived in three dollars of silicon, and the silicon obeyed it the
way wood obeys a joint: without complaint, without question, for as long as
the boat floats. A keel is not the largest piece of a ship. It is the piece
the rest of the ship is hung from — the first thing laid, the last thing
abandoned, the line every other piece measures itself against. Everything
that follows in this vessel is fastened, by joinery and not by bolts, to
that blink. When the boat is finished and the reader asks what holds it
together, the answer will be the same answer a shipwright gives: the keel
was laid true, and the days grew from it.

## The Stem

The water arrives one character at a time. That is the truth of the wire,
and the stem is built to that truth: narrow, forward, taking each byte the
way a bow takes chop — singly, without ceremony, at a pace of forty-eight
hundred to the second, which would bore a faster vessel and suits a
workboat fine. Each character is tasted and passed aft: a dollar to open
the sentence, then the names lettered in — GGA, RMC, HDT, DBT, VHW — five
currents; where she stands, twice told; where she points; what lies under;
how she walks through the water. As each byte passes, the stem keeps a
quiet tally, folding it into a running count the way a shipwright counts
strokes of the adze, and when the sentence closes with its star and its
two figures the tally must match, or the whole line goes over the side
without a word of regret. What survives is laid down in integers —
millionths of a degree, scribed, not sketched, because a float is a guess
and an integer is a mark struck with an awl — and carried aft to the
table, which answers the only question a lamp can ask: green, or amber.
The stem meets everything first and slows it to what the hull can carry,
adding no drag of its own. Behind it, waiting to be laid, is the piece
that takes the grounding.
