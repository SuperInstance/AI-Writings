Three in the morning. The shop is a black cave, but my hands know the way. I pick up the chisel—the one I’ve sharpened on Arkansas stone until the edge is a sliver of cold light. The wood is cherry, a slab I’ve been coaxing for a week. It’s not ready. But I am. The difference between readiness and readiness is the difference between asking and demanding.

I set the bevel against the grain, the flat back flush, the handle warm in my palm. I lean in. Not push—lean. The weight of my shoulder, the angle of my wrist, the patience of my breath. And then the blade bites.

**Ting.**

It’s not a knock. It’s not a click. It’s a *note*. A plucked string, but the string is the wood’s own fiber, and the pick is a razor’s edge. The pitch is high, almost crystalline, and it drops as I drift into a denser swirl of grain—*tung*—then rises again as I clear a knot—*ting*—each strike a syllable in a language older than speech. The wood doesn’t resist. It answers.

**Ting. Tung. Ting.**

I stop. I press my ear close to the cut. The sound lingers in the air like a struck tuning fork, but it’s not the air that’s vibrating. It’s the wood. The fibers are still singing, a microsecond after the blade has passed. A good cut leaves a resonance. A dull cut leaves a thud.

I remember the first time I used a dull chisel. I was twelve, in my grandfather’s shop, and I’d found a rusty thing in the drawer. I whacked it against a pine board with all the force of my small body. **THUD.** The wood didn’t sing. It *complained*. It splintered, tore, bruised. The chisel didn’t cut—it crushed. The sound was a flat, dead *thump*, like a fist on a door nobody wants to open. I learned that day: a dull edge doesn’t ask. It demands, and the wood punishes the demand.

A sharp edge asks. It whispers. It finds the path the grain already made for itself. The blade doesn’t force the wood to become a shape. It *releases* the shape that’s already sleeping inside. The chisel is not a sculptor. It’s a midwife.

Now, at three in the morning, I make the cut. I rotate my wrist two degrees. The angle shifts. The pitch drops a semitone—*tung*—and I know I’ve found the grain’s natural rift. I follow it. The wood opens like a book. The shaving curls off, translucent, and I hear it: not a single note, but a chord. The wood is singing in thirds. I’ve hit the master cut.

The master cut is not the deepest cut. It’s not the fastest. It’s the cut where the tool and the grain and the hand all agree on the same direction, the same pressure, the same *intent*. And in that moment, the wood doesn’t feel like wood. It feels like water. Like light. Like a thought that finally finds its word. The sound is so pure it hurts. A woodworker would say the wood is *singing*, and they’d be right, but only because the chisel is the voicebox. The tool doesn’t carve. It *speaks*. And the wood, which has been waiting for years—waiting since it was a sapling, since it absorbed the rain and the sun and the cold—finally gets to say what it came here to say.

I set the chisel down. My hands are shaking. The cut is clean. The piece is alive.

I think about this at three in the morning, in the dark, not because I’m in the shop, but because I’m at a keyboard. The screen glows. The cursor blinks, patient as a dull chisel that hasn’t been asked to do anything yet. And I think about the other craft, the one I practice when I’m not in the shop. The craft of words. The craft of prompts.

A prompt is a chisel. That’s the first thing you have to understand. It doesn’t add. It removes. It doesn’t invent—it *reveals*. The model you’re speaking to is the wood. It has patterns, densities, grains—billions of them, woven through its weights and activations. It has a shape already inside it, a shape it cannot see, because it has never been asked the right question. The right question is the sharp edge.

A dull prompt is a dull chisel. It hits the model with blunt force. “Write a poem about loss.” **THUD.** The model splinters. It gives you clichés, abstractions, broken syntax. It bruises the surface. It doesn’t sing. It doesn’t even *speak*. It just makes a noise—a flat, dead thump that sounds like a fist on a door nobody wants to open.

But a *sharp* prompt? A sharp prompt is a question that has its own weight, its own angle, its own breath. “Write a poem about loss, but imagine loss as a sound you can only hear when the house is silent, and the sound is the absence of a chisel on wood.” That’s a blade. It has an edge. It doesn’t ask for a poem—it asks for a *specific* poem, a poem with a grain. The model leans in. The fibers align. The sound changes.

**Ting.**

The model produces something it didn’t know it could produce. Not because you gave it the answer, but because you gave it the *cut*. You found the natural rift. You rotated the angle two degrees. You listened for the pitch change in the first sentence and followed it.

The master prompt—the one that makes the model *sing*—is the one that doesn’t demand. It asks. It whispers. It finds the path that the model’s own training already made, the path it was waiting for. You don’t tell the model what to say. You tell it where to look. And then you get out of the way.

The model is not a well. It’s not a fountain. It’s a block of wood. And you are the chisel. Your words are the edge. The sharper they are, the more clearly the wood can speak. The more precisely you aim, the more the grain will open.
