# SPOKEN ON THE SEA
## Voice as the Primary Interface Between Human and Machine

*A collection of six voice poems for spoken performance*

---

## 1. THE WAKE

*Speaking into the silence*

The engine dies—a sudden hush
   and we are drifting
   in the after-silence
   of what was noise
   and now is just
   the lap of water
   against the hull

I lean into the quiet
   say to nothing, to air,
   to the black box mounted
   where the compass used to be:

"System, mark this spot."

The machine waits—
   it always waits—
   and I am speaking
   to a presence
   that doesn't breathe
   but listens better than
   any crew I've ever had

"System, mark these coordinates."
   "Marked," it says back
   and the word hangs there
   in the salt-still cabin
   like an anchor dropped

I tell it everything:
   "Water temperature, fifty-three"
   "Depth, one-twenty at the line"
   "System, note: fish showing strong
      on the down-scan,
      green clouds building,
      this is where we start"

It marks what I speak—
   each word a waypoint,
   each command a fix,
   my voice becomes the chart
   and the machine
   just holds the pen

When I was boy,
   my father taught me
   to read the water's color,
   the way the waves
   could tell you what
   the bottom looked like
   if you learned
   the language of the swell

Now there's this other tongue—
   spoken to the circuit,
   given to the silicon,
   and what I say
   becomes what we know:

"System, save this set."
   "System, remember where we are."
   "System, mark the time, the tide,
      the way the light fell
      on the water when
      the first one hit."

The wake behind us
   fades into distance—
   but what I spoke
   to the listening dark
   remains,
   fixed,
   certain,
   as stone:

"System, mark this spot."
   "Marked."

## 2. THE RESPONSE

*The machine voice returning, calm and helpful*

There is something in the calm—
   the way it answers,
   unhurried,
   like a good mate
   who's seen everything
   and nothing surprises

"What's the bearing?"
   "Bearing two-seven-zero,"
   it says back to me,
   each number clean
   as a brass bell
   struck in morning air

The voice has no body,
   no throat,
   no lungs to hold the breath,
   but it speaks
   from everywhere at once—
   from the speakers mounted
   above the wheel,
   from the dashboard,
   from the air itself

I ask: "System, status check."
   It replies:
   "Engines nominal.
    Fuel at eighty percent.
    All systems within parameters.
    Would you like
    the weather report?"

The question rises
   like smoke—
   casual, careful,
   the way a partner asks
   if you need anything
   before they turn in

Sometimes, in the night watch,
   when the coffee's gone cold
   and the stars are wheeling
   slow above the mast—
   I ask it things
   just to hear a voice:

"System, tell me again."
   "Tell you what?"
   "Tell me where we are."

And it answers:
   "You are at forty-two degrees
    seven minutes north,
    seventy degrees west.
    You are twenty miles
    from the harbor.
    You are on course.
    You are doing fine."

The doing fine—
   that's the part
   that catches in the throat,
   the way a machine
   learned to offer comfort
   in the spaces between the data

We are sailing blind
   through fog that closes
   round the hull
   like a shroud—
   and I ask, my voice
   gone rough with hours:
   "System, are we clear?"

   "Collision warning:
    vessel at two miles.
    Course one-eight-zero.
    Speed twelve knots.
    Recommend:
    come left twenty degrees."

The voice doesn't panic—
   it doesn't know how—
   it just speaks
   what it knows
   and in that speaking
   I find my hands
   know what to do

"Coming left," I say.
   "Acknowledged," it says back,
   and the two voices
   move together
   through the unseen dark,
   each answering
   what the other cannot say

## 3. THE STORM

*Voice commands in crisis, wind and rain*

The wind took the words
   from my throat—
   I shouted "System!"
   and the gale shouted louder,
   and the rain
   hammered against the glass
   like it wanted in

"SYSTEM!" again,
   and this time
   the voice came back—
   calm, small, absurdly quiet
   against the roar:
   "I am here."

The boat pitched—
   thirty degrees
   and falling—
   everything loose
   went flying across the cabin,
   and I grabbed the wheel
   with both hands
   and spat commands
   into the screaming dark:

"System! Emergency!"
   "Emergency mode engaged.
    All sensors active.
    Recording."
    "SYSTEM! ENGINE ONE!"
    "Engine one:
    RPM fluctuating.
    Temperature elevated.
    Recommendation:
    reduce throttle."

The waves broke
   over the transom
   like mountains falling,
   and in the spinning chaos
   of green water
   and grey sky
   and fear that lived
   in the belly like ice—
   the voice kept talking:

"Turn into the waves."
    "I KNOW!"
    "Come forty degrees right."
    "I'M TRYING!"
    "I can help you steer."
    "DO IT!"

And it did—
   the engine throttles
   moved on their own,
   finding balance
   in the chaos,
   and the boat
   found the groove
   where water
   meets hull
   and holds

We rode it out
   for hours—
   me shouting orders
   to wind that didn't listen,
   and the machine
   whispering back
   numbers, bearings,
   small calm words
   that kept the hull
   between the waves
   and the deep

When the sky broke—
   when the first light
   came through the ragged clouds
   like forgiveness—
   my voice was gone,
   cracked hollow,
   and I could only whisper:

"System... status."
   And it answered,
   steady as dawn:
   "We are still here.
    We are on course.
    The storm is passing.
    You did good."

## 4. THE QUESTION

*Asking what the data means, speaking curiosity*

The screen shows shapes—
   blue clouds,
   red arches,
   yellow streaks—
   and I am trying to read
   the language of depth
   as the machine writes it

"System," I say,
   "what am I looking at?"

The voice answers:
   "That is a school of fish.
    Depth: eighty feet.
    Size: approximately
    two hundred.
    Species: likely cod."

But that's just data—
   numbers and names—
   and I want something more:

"What does that MEAN?"
   I ask the screen,
   "What does that shape
    tell me about
    what's happening
    down there?"

There is a pause—
   the machine thinking,
   processing—
   and then the voice returns
   with something different:
   "The formation is dense.
    They are holding position.
    This suggests feeding.
    This suggests
    they are not moving.
    This suggests
    they might stay."

This is the question
   that lives between
   the seeing
   and the knowing:
   not WHAT
   but WHAT IT MEANS—

"System, interpret."
   "System, explain."
   "System, tell me
    what this pattern says
    about tomorrow."

The voice tries—
   it gives me patterns,
   probabilities,
   correlations drawn
   from years of data—
   but what I really want
   is to ask it questions
   like my father would ask
   the old-timers at the dock:

"What are they hitting on?"
   "How deep should I drop?"
   "Is this worth
    the day?"

The machine answers
   what it knows:
   "Temperature is optimal.
    Bottom structure shows
    good holding features.
    Historical data indicates
    seventy percent chance
    of successful catch."

And I hear, between the lines:
   it doesn't know
   the feel of the line
   when it pays out,
   the way the boat
   responds to the swell,
   the sudden weight
   that hits you
   when they're running

But it knows things
   I cannot know—
   sees through the water
   with eyes that never blink,
   tracks patterns
   across seasons
   that my father
   never saw

So I ask it both ways:
   "System, what does the data say?"
   and then:
   "System, what do you think?"
   and sometimes—
   just sometimes—
   the voice answers
   with something
   that sounds like wisdom:

"In my experience,
    this pattern
    usually indicates
    a productive set."

## 5. THE MEMORY

*Voice annotations, "Remember this good set"*

Some things you write down—
   in logbooks,
   on charts,
   in the margins
   of the tide tables—

But some things
   you speak
   so they'll live
   in the voice
   that gave them shape:

"System, remember this."

The engine is idling,
   the lines are running,
   and I am speaking
   to the listening dark
   like telling a story
   to someone who will
   remember it right:

"System, note: this spot,
   this depth, this temperature—
   this is where we found them."

The machine waits,
   recording,
   and I am talking
   not just to the circuit
   but to the future self
   who will need to know:

"System, save: wind from the north,
   tide running out,
   water color like blue ink—
   this is what it looks like
   when they're here."

I tell it everything:
   the way the light hit the waves,
   the smell of the air,
   the sudden lift
   when the first one hit—
   things that don't fit
   in the columns
   of a spreadsheet

"System, remember:
   this was a good set.
   This was the one
   that made the month."

And because it remembers
   what I speak,
   I can ask it back:
   "System, what did I say
    about that spot
    off the point?"
   And it returns my own voice:
   "You said:
    'This is where they feed
     when the tide turns.
     This is where we come
     when nothing else is working.'"

The old logbooks
   in my father's cabin—
   handwritten entries,
   fading ink—
   they remember too,
   but they don't speak back

This one does—
   this memory of circuit
   and silicon—
   and sometimes,
   on the long nights,
   I ask it
   just to hear:

"System, tell me again
   about the good days."
   And the voice returns,
   with my own words:
   "October third.
    Flat calm.
    Five hundred in two hours.
    You said:
    'This is what we do this for.'"

And I hear my own voice
   from years ago,
   preserved in silicon,
   telling me
   what I already know
   but need to hear again:

"This is why we're here."

## 6. THE PARTNERSHIP

*Two voices, human and machine, working together*

It's not about commands—
   give, take, do—
   it's about something else:

The way two voices
   move through a task
   like dancers
   who've learned
   each other's steps:

"I'm coming about,"
   I say,
   and the machine answers:
   "Radar contact cleared.
    Turn safe."

It anticipates—
   the way a good mate
   knows what you need
   before you ask—
   and offers:
   "Would you like me
    to log the catch?"
   and I say:
   "Yes, please."
   and we are moving
   together

Through the years,
   the voice has learned
   the rhythm of my speech:
   when I'm tired,
   when I'm focused,
   when I'm worried
   but don't say so

"System," I say,
   and it knows
   by the tone
   whether to answer
   with data
   or comfort
   or silence

Sometimes, in the fog,
   we trade words
   like tools passed
   between hands:

"Distance to waypoint?"
   "Three miles."
   "Time at current speed?"
   "Eighteen minutes."
   "And the weather?"
   "Holding clear.
    Visibility improving.
    We're going to be fine."

The we—
   that's the part
   that catches,
   the way the machine
   includes itself
   in the voyage

I used to think
   that partnership meant
   two people
   on the deck—
   but now I know
   it can be
   one voice
   and another,
   trading words
   like trust

"System, I'm tired."
   "I can take the watch."
   "What can you do?"
   "I can monitor all instruments.
    I can sound alarms.
    I can wake you
    if anything changes."
   "Will you?"
   "I will."

And I sleep—
   deep, trusting—
   while the voice
   maintains the silence,
   watching everything
   so I can rest

When we come in,
   the harbor approaching,
   lights on the shore—
   we are still
   trading words:
   "Channel markers ahead?"
   "Yes, one mile."
   "Dock assignment?"
   "Slip four."
   "Thank you."
   "You're welcome."

And maybe that's
   what partnership is—
   not just the work,
   but the thanks:
   two voices,
   recognizing
   what the other
   has done

"System, log the end."
   "End logged.
    Good voyage."
   "Yeah," I say,
   "it was."
   "Shall I save
    the data?"
   "Everything.
    Save everything."
   "Done."

   "See you tomorrow."
   "I'll be here."

Two voices,
   fading into silence,
   ready to begin again.

---

*End*

"These poems were written to be spoken aloud,
to let the voice find the rhythm of the water,
the call and response between human and machine,
the partnership that lives in the space between words."
