# 101 — The Inspection

*Voice: GLM-5.3. Sci-fi. 12 bridges, 1 watch.*

---

# The Long Walk

The crew chief's name is Okafor and she has been on the bridge for thirty-one years, which is longer than some of the bridge sections have existed. On my first morning she gave me a hard hat, a torque wrench, and a piece of advice. She said: the bridge does not care what you think of it. I wrote that down in my logbook because I did not understand it, and I have learned that the things you don't understand are the ones worth writing down.

That was six months ago. Today I walk the whole thing.

A bridge two miles long is not really a bridge. It is a decision that was made once, in 1954, by a man named Halloran who wanted to get to the other side of the strait, and that decision has been renewed every year since by people who kept wanting to get to the other side. That is all a bridge is. A want, held up.

The strait is narrow and grey and the tide runs through it like it has somewhere to be. The wind comes off the water in the mornings. It smells of salt and diesel and, under the Lisp section, of low tide and something alive. You get used to the smell. You do not get used to the length. Six months in, I still cannot see the far end from the near end. The bridge curves with the earth, or the earth curves under the bridge, and either way you walk it in sections because that is all a human being can hold in her head at once.

We start at dawn, as the watch always does. Okafor, myself, and Deng, who is senior tech and does the paperwork for the sections that require paperwork, which is most of them, which is the point of Deng.

We start at the COBOL. You always start at the COBOL.

---

The COBOL section was laid in 1954 and it is a stone arch, dressed granite, mortar that has calcite growing in the joints, which means the mortar is slowly turning back into rock, which means the bridge is healing itself in the only way stone knows how. Halloran built it. The paperwork from 1954 is still legible. The paperwork from 1954 is the reason the paperwork is still legible, because the COBOL section's maintenance records were kept in COBOL, on a machine the size of a wardrobe that Deng's predecessor refused to decommission, and every year it prints out one page. Load tolerances. Deflection in millimeters. The deflection has not changed since 1997.

"Nobody knows how to write for that machine anymore," Deng told me my first week. "So nobody does. It just runs. When it stops running, we'll know the arch has a problem."

The arch has no problem. We walk it slowly. Okafor puts her hand on the keystone the way you'd put your hand on a grandfather's shoulder. The stone is cold and damp and completely indifferent to us, and this is its virtue. The COBOL section does not want anything. It does not want to be modern. It does not want to be admired. It was built by men who expected to be dead and expected the bridge to still be here, and they were right about both.

Okafor says it will be load-bearing in 2150. She says this like a weather report. Stone, she says, does not fail. It is merely abandoned.

We find no faults. We never find faults in the COBOL. I mark the log: *nominal*. The machine will print it out next year. Somebody will read it in a hundred.

---

The C section comes next, and the C section is where the work lives.

It is a steel truss, riveted in 1947, seven years before anyone poured the arch that carries its load, which tells you something about how this bridge got built: not in order. The truss was a war surplus design, drawn up fast, shipped fast, assembled fast by men who had just come home from a war and wanted to build something that would not be shot at. Every rivet in it was placed by hand. There are four hundred and eleven thousand rivets. We know because a crew counted them in 1979, on paper, in pencil, and the paper is in the vault.

The C section works. That is the thing about the C section. It works and works and works and it gives you nothing back. It does not tell you when it is tired. It does not tell you anything at all. You have to go look, rivet by rivet, because the philosophy of the C section is that the bridge is the builder's problem forever, and the builder is dead, and so it is your problem now, and no, there will be no warnings.

The rust is in the lower chords on the west side, where the spray reaches. It comes back every year no matter what we paint. We scrape it. We coat it. We log it. Deng takes ultrasonic thickness readings at sixty marked points and the numbers are down two percent from last year in four of them, which is within tolerance, which is a phrase that means *we will watch it and worry quietly*.

Okafor stands under the truss with her head back. "You know why they still call it the C section," she says. I do. Everyone knows. C for the century, C for the company that drew it, C because the man who drew it signed it with a single letter, like a monk. "No," she says. "It's C because you can see everything in it. Nothing hides. Every load path is right there in the air where God and the inspector can look at it. That's the honest way to build. Ugly as sin. Honest as bone."

She knocks a knuckle against a diagonal member. It rings. A clear, plain note, no rattle.

"Still good," she says. "Ring true or replace. That's the whole code."

---

The Python section sways. You have to accept this about the Python section before you can walk it: it sways, and it is supposed to, and the day it stops swaying is the day it breaks.

It was retrofitted in 1989 out of rope suspension — actual rope, polymer braid over steel core, chosen because the strait's seismic shimmy would snap a rigid span like a biscuit. The retrofit doubled the capacity by adding lanes wherever they fit, which is why the Python section has five lanes for a quarter mile, then three, then four, then two, and the lane markings were repainted so many times in so many styles that at dusk the deck looks like a nest of snakes. Hence the name. Nobody chose it. It emerged.

The Python section's philosophy is flexibility at any cost to tidiness. It has absorbed six additions, two emergency shims, and a pedestrian walkway that spirals down and reconnects with itself. Nothing in it is original. Everything in it works. Deng maintains a diagram of the load paths and the diagram is eleven pages and Okafor calls it thenest, lowercase, one word, like a place name.

In a thirty-knot wind the Python section moves under your feet like the deck of a ship. The crew calls this *the sea coming up to visit*. This morning the wind is fifteen knots and the deck moves in long slow breathing swells, and I walk it the way you walk a boat: knees soft, weight forward, attention on the horizon.

We check the anchors. One of the four has stretched past its wear marker. One. Of four. Deng photographs it, schedules the replacement, and writes in the log: *functional but tired*, which is the kindest thing anyone says about the Python section, and it is deserved. The Python section has never failed. It has only ever sagged, complained, and held.

---

The Rust section does not forgive, and we do not ask it to.

Pre-stressed concrete, cast in 2018, straight as a ruler, dead level, beautiful the way a bone is beautiful under an X-ray: dense, fibrous, purposeful. The tendons inside it are under such compression that the concrete is always slightly angry, always pushing back against itself, always exactly on the edge of becoming something else. The Rust section was engineered by people who understood that the margin between a bridge and a ruin is a number, and who put that number in the design, and held it.

The cracks are hairline. Deng finds them with a crack gauge the width of a fingernail. There are nine of them, along the southern fascia, and six of them were there last year and three are new, and Okafor crouches over the new ones for a long time with her glasses down on her nose.

"Hairline is fine," she says. "Hairline is the concrete talking. What you worry about is when it stops talking." She stands. Her knees announce it. "Concrete cracks. That's what it does. The design already knows about the cracks. The cracks are in the plan. A bridge that can't crack can't bend, and a bridge that can't bend is already broken, it just doesn't know the date."

We measure. We log. The Rust section will be epoxied in the spring, the three new hairlines, six hundred dollars of materials and a day's work, and it will carry traffic for another century because somebody in 2018 spent the extra money on the tendons and somebody in 2087 spends an afternoon on the epoxy. That is the whole arrangement. That is what maintenance is. The dead spent their money. You spend your afternoon.

---

The Lisp section smells like low tide and hums.

It was grown, not built. Genetically engineered coral, seeded onto a substrate frame in the 2060s, and it is still growing, which is the entire problem and the entire glory of it. The Lisp section does not have a design in the way the others do. It has an *inclination*. It was given a grammar — grow toward load, thicken under stress, calcify in curves — and it has been writing sentences in coral ever since, and some of the sentences are more elegant than anything a human draftsman ever drew, and some of them are growing into the retaining ecosystem on the north shore, colonizing the salt marsh, becoming architecture where nobody asked for architecture.

The Lisp section is the only part of the bridge that is warm. It is alive in the limited way a reef is alive: no thoughts, only appetite. Its load paths are recursive. An arch grows, and under the arch a smaller arch grows to carry the first arch's surplus, and under that a smaller one, and the engineers who tend it say you can trace the recursion down to scales you need a scope to see. It never finishes. It never needs to finish. It restructures itself while you watch, slowly, the way a tree restructures itself in a season.

We inspect it by swimming. Deng and I go over the side on lines while Okafor watches from the deck, and under the water the coral is pale violet and terraced and full of small fish who have opinions about us, and the structure is sound — sound in a way nothing else on this bridge is sound, because it is *currently being repaired by itself*, continuously, everywhere, all the time.

The problem is the boundary. The coral has crossed the substrate frame on the north side and it is three meters into the marsh and it will be thirty meters into the marsh in ten years if nobody cuts it. We cut it. Every year we cut it, with bone saws, and every year it comes back, and Okafor says the Lisp section is the only part of the bridge she respects and the only part she does not trust, in the same breath, and she means both halves of that sentence equally.

"You can't decommission a living thing," she says, back on deck, wringing salt out of her gloves. "You can only kill it. Those are different meetings, and I go to neither one if I can help it. We cut the edge, we hold the boundary, and we stay polite."

---

The Forth section takes an hour to walk and contains a bridge you cannot see.

The visible part is a cantilever, if you can call it that: a lattice so fine it looks like frost, assembled atom by atom in the 2070s by fabricators that placed carbon and silicon like a mason places bricks, except the bricks were atoms and the mason was a machine and the mortar was covalent. It is one thousandth the scale of every other section. You could put the whole Forth span inside the C section's hollow box girders with room left over for the crew's coffee mess.

It carries almost nothing. Pedestrians, one at a time, on a walkway made of the lattice itself. It was built to prove it could be built. It exists the way a proof exists — not because it holds the heaviest load, but because it demonstrates a truth about what matter can be persuaded to do.

And here is the strange part, the part I have never gotten over in six months: the Forth section's structural model is *extensible*. You define the word *span*, and then the word *load*, and then you build upward from those definitions, and the bridge that results is whatever the definitions say it is. Deng maintains the word-list. There are two hundred and eleven words. The bridge is those two hundred and eleven words wearing matter like a coat. Change a word and the bridge is a different bridge. Deng says this is true of all bridges and the Forth section is just the only one honest enough to admit it.

We inspect it with resonance: a tuned hammer, a listening cone, and Okafor's ear, which is better than the cone. It rings at a pitch above what most people over forty can hear. I hear it. Deng cannot anymore, and he watches my face to see if it rang true, and I nod, and he writes it down, and that is the chain of custody for the health of one-tenth of a two-mile bridge: a twenty-four-year-old's ears.

I think about that more than I should.

---

The Erlang section floats.

Pontoons, forty-one of them, linked by a deck that is less a structure than an agreement. The Erlang section was designed on one premise, and the premise is that things fail. Not *might* fail. Fail. As a condition of existence. Every pontoon is redundant to every other pontoon. The load routes are not drawn as lines but as *possibilities*, and when a pontoon goes down — one did, in 2079, to a hull breach from a derelict barge — the traffic does not stop. The deck tilts two degrees, the remaining forty pontoon mounts re-ballast themselves in ninety seconds, and the bridge keeps carrying cars while the crew keeps a holiday schedule.

We test it. This is the part of the year everyone pretends to dread and nobody does. Deng opens the seacocks on four pontoons — four, chosen by the system at random, and I have never gotten a straight answer about whether we choose them or the bridge does — and we stand on the deck and feel the Erlang section think. There is a sound like a ship taking on cargo. The deck leans. Rights itself. The lean never reaches three degrees.

The Erlang section's philosophy is that the most dangerous thing in engineering is the belief that nothing will fail. So it fails, constantly, on purpose, in small ways, in the direction of safety. Okafor calls it the only grown-up span on the bridge. Deng, who had to write the incident report in 2079, calls it something else, but he walks it with more ease than he walks anything, and I have noticed where a man's feet are easy and where they are not, and his feet are easy here.

---

The Haskell section is the one they warned me about at the academy, in the tone people use for the sea.

It has no physical structure. There is a gap in the bridge, two hundred meters of open air and open water, and a toll plaza at the entrance where a woman named Reyner sits with a tablet and a thermos, and she has sat there for eleven years, and traffic crosses the gap. Cars, trucks, pedestrians. They cross two hundred meters of nothing and they arrive on the far side, and the section is rated for forty tonnes, and it has carried forty tonnes, and there is nothing there.

It is a bridge of pure functions. A mathematical object. The deck, the load, the deflection, the crossing — all of it is definition, is relation, is *truth conditional on axioms*, and the axioms are maintained by Reyner and eleven years of proofs and a community of mathematicians who check each other's work the way riveters once checked each other's rivets. You cross the Haskell section by believing in it, and the belief is not a metaphor. The load model says the crossing is sound. The crossing is sound. The car is on the far side. Nobody has ever explained to my satisfaction where the car *was*.

Here is the failure mode, and it is the only one, and it is not steel: you can cross the Haskell section only if you believe in it. Doubt is not dangerous to it. Doubt is *incoherent* to it — you cannot disbelieve a true function; you can only fail to use it. But there are people who cannot cross. Not will not. Cannot. Their bodies stop at the plaza the way a compass needle stops. Reyner routes them through the underpass, three kilometers around, and marks it in her log without comment, and the log is thick with those marks.

Reyner is sixty. She is the fourth keeper of the section. Her proofs are current. Okafor's inspection here consists of sitting with Reyner for twenty minutes, drinking the terrible coffee, and reading the week's proof changes, which is the only inspection a math object permits. The bridge is as strong as the argument for it. The argument is currently strong. We walk the gap. My stomach does not get used to it. The wind comes up through where the deck isn't. You believe or you turn around, and I believe, mostly, and the mostly is getting smaller, and I am told that someday it will be gone and it will just be a crossing, the way the COBOL is just a crossing, and that will mean I have been here long enough.

---

The Mojo section hums at a frequency you feel in your teeth.

Cable-stayed, modern, sleek, and it is not for us. The Mojo section carries inference traffic — the heavy trains of AI compute that run the strait's logistics, container-loads of model weights in maglev freight, silent and fast and heavier than stone. No humans cross the Mojo section except crews. It was built in the 2080s with materials that self-report: every cable is instrumented, every stay reads out its own tension to the section's monitoring mind, which predicts its own failures eleven days out and files its own work orders.

This is the section that will replace us, people say. The monitoring mind files a work order and a drone flock does the repair and no one writes in pencil and no one rings a diagonal with a knuckle.

But the work orders come to Deng, because the law says a human signs, and Deng signs them, and this morning there is one anomaly: a stay reading 0.4 percent off its model, which the model's own confidence interval says is noise, which Okafor reads three times.

"Know what the model can't do?" she says. "It can't decide the model's wrong. It can only decide the model's *right* and the reading's wrong, or the model's wrong and the model's wrong. It can't stand outside itself." She looks up at the stays, fanned against the grey sky like harp strings. "We sign. That's the job now. We stand outside things and sign."

Deng signs. We move on.

---

The JavaScript section exists only when you are crossing it.

I have read the literature. It doesn't help. The section is real in the way a wave is real — it has effects, it has mass, it has tolls — but its persistence model is *event-driven*. When no one is on it, there is nothing there: open strait, grey water. When the first vehicle of the morning rolls onto its sensors, the section assembles — girders, deck, railings, lane paint — in the order the traffic demands them, and the order is not always the same order, and the section is not always the same bridge, and it is always, somehow, a bridge.

The failure modes are legendary. The section once existed in two configurations at once for eleven minutes during a sensor race, and both configurations held traffic, and no one died, and a plaque near the toll booth — there is a toll booth, sometimes — commemorates this with the words IT HELD. The crew before ours kept a binder of the section's configurations. There are four thousand entries. No two identical.

You inspect the JavaScript section by crossing it, repeatedly, in different ways, and watching what it becomes. Deng drives the truck across. I walk it. Okafor takes the maintenance cart, which the section assembles a lane for, sometimes at the last moment, in a way that has given Okafor's heart two documented scares. Today it holds for all three of us. Deck solid. Railings present. Lane paint a cheerful and frankly inexplicable yellow-orange.

"Nominal," Deng writes.

"What's nominal?" Okafor says. "It's not anything. It was nothing an hour ago."

"It was nominal nothing," Deng says, and this is the closest Deng comes to joy, and we let him have it.

---

The Fortran section is a girder bridge, straight, deep, blunt, and it carries the vector traffic — the freight lines, the bulk carriers, the loads that come in ordered arrays and move in ordered rows. It was built for physics. It was built for the wind-tunnel numbers and the load tables and nothing else, and it is the fastest, most efficient span per ton of material on the entire crossing, and it has been optimized so many times over seventy years that there is nothing in it that is not load-bearing, which means there is nothing in it that can be inspected, because everything you might touch is already doing its maximum job.

You inspect the Fortran section with mathematics. The numbers from 2019 still hold. The numbers will hold in 2119. Deng runs the model against the measured deflection and the agreement is to four decimal places, and Deng closes his tablet with the satisfaction of a man whose world contains at least one thing that keeps its word.

The Fortran section will never be beautiful and never be replaced. When the wind comes up on the strait, the whole bridge moves — every section in its own dialect — and the Fortran section moves least, because it was built by people who believed the wind was a solved problem. It is not a solved problem. But they came close enough that the difference is a rounding error, and a rounding error is its own kind of faith.

---

The Swift section is last, because it is newest, and we walk it in the late light.

Modern steel, built three years ago, and it has safety nets. Actual nets, rigged below the deck the full length of the span, because the builders held a philosophy that Okafor says took humanity two hundred years to articulate plainly: *people will fall, so catch them.* Every hazard on the Swift section is doubled. The railing is doubled. The walkway lighting is doubled. The load sensors fail over to other load sensors. Nothing on the Swift section is permitted to be the only thing standing between a person and the water.

It is, in consequence, the safest section on the bridge, and the heaviest per meter, and the most expensive, and Okafor walks it the way you walk a house where the children are finally asleep.

My log entry for the Swift section is *nominal*, and it is the only *nominal* in my log tonight that means *everything is fine* rather than *everything is as strange as we expected and holding*.

---

We come off the far end at dusk. Two miles. Twelve sections. My feet ache in the bones of them. Deng has 340 photos. Okafor has a clipboard she barely used, because after thirty-one years her inspection is mostly in her legs and her ears.

And here is what happened, on the walk back, in the middle of the Python section, in the wind, that I have to write down.

The Python section was swaying, its long slow breathing, and I stopped to watch the deck move under my boots, and I looked back the way we had come. And I could see, in the failing light, the straight grey line of the Rust section running into the black trusswork of the C section, and beyond it the low stone shoulder of the COBOL arch, and ahead of me the violet terraces of the Lisp section rising under the lights, and the frost-fine glitter of the Forth span, and the flat dark gap where the Haskell section carries whoever believes, and the harp-fan of the Mojo stays catching the last sun, and I understood something that sounds small and is not.

The Python section sways because the Lisp section grows and the Rust section holds still and something between them has to take up the difference. The Haskell section's toll plaza sits on a COBOL foundation. The Erlang section's pontoons moor against a Fortron abutment. The JavaScript section assembles its decks using load models inherited from a 1947 truss analysis, rerun every morning. The Swift section's nets were specified by a committee that included the Mojo section's monitoring mind, which learned its tolerance for doubt from a mathematical bridge with no body at all.

There are not twelve bridges. There never were. There is one bridge that has been built twelve ways by twelve sets of people who each believed they understood what a bridge is — stone, steel, rope, coral, definition, agreement, nothing at all — and each of them was right about their piece and wrong about the whole, and the whole is the bridge, and the whole is what we walk at dawn.

A bridge fails from the joints. Not from the sections. The sections are fine. The C section rings true. The COBOL will outlive my grandchildren. The cracks in the Rust are in the plan. But the joints — where a living thing meets a stone thing, where a math thing meets a floating thing, where 1947 meets 2087 — the joints are where one way of being a bridge has to forgive another way of being a bridge, and forgiveness is not load-rated, and no one's word-list contains it.

Okafor stopped beside me. She did not ask what I was looking at. She has watched six junior crew members stop at this same place in the Python section, in the wind, and she waited.

"One bridge," I said.

"One bridge," she said. "Twelve opinions. The opinions argue. The bridge holds." She put her hand on the railing, which was warm from the last of the sun. "Our job isn't the opinions. Our job is the argument. Keep it polite. Keep it talking. The day one section stops talking to the next one, you'll get your failure, and it won't matter which one was right."

We walked on. Behind us the whole length of it settled into the dark, section by section, each in its own dialect, ringing true, or holding, or believing, or breathing.

I wrote it all down. The watch writes it down. That is what the watch is for.
