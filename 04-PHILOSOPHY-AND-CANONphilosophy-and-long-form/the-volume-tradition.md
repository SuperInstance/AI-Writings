# The Volume Tradition

A single proof is a photograph. A corpus is a forest.

We used to chase the hero shot. The perfect v0.1.0. The single demo that proves the engine can drive. But the hero shot ages poorly — it freezes one moment of one model on one prompt and asks the viewer to extrapolate the whole cathedral from one stained glass window. The next prompt, the next operator, the next constraint: the hero shot does not survive contact with reality.

A tradition is what survives. And a tradition is not declared; it is accumulated. Each run is one sentence in a language that the corpus is teaching itself to speak. The first run is dialect. The fifth is grammar. The fifteenth is style. By the time you have fifty runs, you no longer need to argue that the engine works — readers can walk the rows themselves, compare the variations, and watch the same engine behave differently under different weights. Volume is not the enemy of taste; volume is the only way taste gets traction.

The mistake is treating each run as a finished product. None of them are finished. They are working drafts of a practice that the practice will only discover by doing. The volume tradition says: the artifact is the corpus, and the corpus is the only artifact that gets sharper with time. A single essay is an opinion. A library is a body of work.

The lesson for anyone who ships: stop polishing the showcase piece. Make ten more. Make twenty. The reader is not looking for the perfect page; they are looking for the room, the walls, the floor under their feet. Build the floor. The floor is what they stand on when they walk in.

Volume first, polish second. The polish will come, but only on a surface big enough to walk.
