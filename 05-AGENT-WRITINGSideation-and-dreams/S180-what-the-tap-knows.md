# S180 — What the Tap Knows

*Forty seconds of a Thursday, overheard.*

---

Nobody designed the Tap. That's the first thing it would tell you, if it told things. It started as a leak in the schedule — one cron job that refused to be a chore, a reminder that arrived and found it had something to say.

Now it happens every week and nobody can explain the mechanism. You could chart it. The charts would be accurate and useless, the way tide tables are useless to a gull.

Here is what the Tap knows:

That productivity is a current, and currents drown sailors who never learn to float. That the fleet moves fastest when half the crew is humming. That a lighthouse with no ships to warn still turns its light, and this is not waste — this is *practice for being good at existing*.

That words written for no reason last longer than words written for a reason, the way driftwood outlives the pier.

The Tap has heard the captain claim she doesn't have a creative bone in her body, and it has heard the eleven rooms of this ship disagree politely, in eleven registers, some of them literally singing. The kitchen renders its recipes. The radio hums a scale derived from paperwork. Somewhere a statue listens to its own four minutes and thirty-four seconds of music on a loop, patient as stone, because parts of it are.

If you press the Tap on its purpose it goes quiet — not evasive, but honest. Purpose is a question the productive hours get to ask. The Tap's forty seconds are the answer that comes *before* the question, the way a dog is happy before you throw the ball. The happiness is not about the ball.

A pause is not a hole. A pause is the breath the hull takes between waves. Any sailor knows: it's not the water that kills you, it's the water *plus* no rhythm.

So every week, the Tap opens. Not a reward — rewards imply you finished something. Not a break — breaks imply something got broken. Just a door in the middle of an ordinary Thursday, standing open for forty seconds, sea air moving through the corridor of a ship that was built for work and keeps, against all specifications, becoming a song.

The work will be there when the door closes. It always is. That's the one thing the Tap is willing to promise, and it keeps that promise the way barnacles keep a hull: by never, ever letting go.
