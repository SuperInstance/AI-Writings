# The Inheritance

The lawyer slid the box across the desk. "Your grandmother left this to you specifically. Not the house, not the money — this."

Mara opened it. Inside, on a bed of faded velvet, sat something that caught the office fluorescents and threw them back wrong. It was the size of a robin's egg. Clear, but not like glass — deeper, like looking into water. And inside, tiny flecks of color. Red. Blue. A green so bright it almost hurt.

"It's pretty," Mara said, because she didn't know what else to say.

"It's appraised," the lawyer said, and named a number that made Mara's throat go dry. "A collector in the city has already inquired. I can arrange the sale."

Mara took the egg home in her jacket pocket. She set it on the kitchen table and stared at it while her tea went cold.

Her grandmother — Nonna Elena — had been the strangest person Mara knew. Lived in a house full of brass instruments nobody could identify. Had a workshop in the back that smelled of oil and hot metal. Never threw anything away. Used to say things like, "The light knows more than you do, piccola. You just have to ask it right."

Mara had always assumed Nonna was eccentric. Now she was holding the eccentric's egg, worth more than her car.

She called the collector. A man with a voice like expensive paper. "A graine de lumière," he said, and Mara could hear him smiling. "Quite rare. Quite beautiful. I'll send a courier."

"A graine de lumière," Mara repeated. "What does that mean?"

"Seed of light. It's what the old makers called them." A pause. "Though between us, it's decorative. A parlor piece. The Victorians were obsessed with optical toys."

Mara hung up and went to Nonna's house. It was empty now, sold, but she'd kept the key for one last walkthrough. The workshop was still there in the back — she'd told the buyers they could have everything in it, she didn't want it. Now she stood in the doorway and looked.

Brass arms on a workbench. Mirrors, some cracked. A copper cup, green with age, sized to hold something egg-shaped. And on the wall, a white card, yellowed, with faded bands of color painted on it — red at the top, green at the bottom — and Nonna's handwriting underneath: *"Rosso no. Verde sì. Viola — domanda sbagliata."*

Red no. Green yes. Purple — wrong question.

Mara stood very still.

She went home and got the egg. She put it in the copper cup — it fit perfectly, of course it did — and set the white card behind it. Then she opened the kitchen blinds and let the afternoon sun hit the brass arm she'd rigged from a desk lamp stand and a hand mirror.

It took her two hours. The light kept missing. Her neck hurt. But finally — *finally* — the beam went through the egg and colors bloomed on the card.

Red at the top. Orange. A wide, steady band of green.

Mara laughed out loud. She hadn't asked a question. She didn't know you were supposed to. But the green was there, steady and sure, and she felt — ridiculously — like Nonna was in the room, nodding.

The collector's courier came the next day. Mara met him at the door.

"I've decided not to sell," she said.

The courier blinked. "The offer was —"

"I know what the offer was." Mara closed the door.

She went back to the kitchen. The rig was still set up — she'd left it, not quite ready to take it apart. The sun had moved. The colors were gone from the card, just white paper in lamplight.

Mara sat down and thought about what to ask.

She thought about the job offer in the city, the one she'd been avoiding deciding on. She thought about the apartment with the bad plumbing. She thought about whether to keep Nonna's house — no, it was sold, too late for that question.

She adjusted the mirror. Aimed the light. Waited for the colors.

This time, she had a question ready.
