# The Blank Water

Wren had been Master Halloran's apprentice for two years before he let her touch the blank water.

Every chart in the shop had it — a stretch of coastline left un-inked, nothing but the faint grid and a single pencilled note in Halloran's cramped hand: *not seen*. Wren had always assumed it was laziness, or a coastline nobody cared about. She was wrong on both counts.

"Why don't you just copy it from the Aldous chart?" she asked, the first time he set her to work on one. "His survey of the Reach is complete."

Halloran didn't look up from his dividers. "Complete is a word men use when they're tired of looking."

"But the Aldous chart has the whole coast drawn in."

"Aye. Drawn by a man who never sailed it." He set the dividers down. "Two ways to be wrong on a chart, Wren. Draw the rocks where there aren't any, and a ship slows for nothing. Draw nothing where the rocks are, and a ship breaks. The third way is the one that keeps you alive."

"What's the third way?"

He tapped the blank water with one knuckle. "Say you don't know. Loudly. In ink."

---

At the Gull's Rest that evening, the argument was already underway when Wren sat down with her supper.

"It's a liar's instrument," Captain Bex was saying. She'd sailed forty years by the stars and trusted no brass that hadn't earned it. "Took me through the Narrows in a fog, needle steady as a church, and we came out a full cable west of where she swore we were. Confident. Wrong."

Pru, the young navigator off the *Cormorant*, flushed. "Your compass was forty years old, Bex. Mine's new-minted, calibrated at the yard. It doesn't lie."

"Everything lies, girl. The question is whether it tells you when."

"It doesn't need to tell me. I know how to read it."

Sil, who owned the Gull's Rest and had heard this argument in various forms for twenty years, set a fresh pint in front of Bex without being asked. He was wiping the same glass he'd been wiping since Wren arrived.

"You're both describing the same compass," Sil said.

Two heads turned.

"Bex says it lies and she knows it. Pru says it doesn't lie and she doesn't need to know." He set the glass down. "The instrument's the same. The difference is who's standing behind it."

Pru opened her mouth. Closed it.

"My compass," Sil went on, "is the regulars. When Bex tells me a storm's coming, I believe her — she's called nine of the last ten. When the new deckhand tells me a storm's coming, I look at the sky myself." He picked up another glass. "The sky's my second compass. Never agrees with the first one for free."

Wren thought about the blank water. About Halloran's knuckle rapping the un-inked coast.

---

She was back at the bench the next morning, ruling the grid for a new chart of the northern Reach, when Halloran stopped behind her shoulder.

"You're leaving the east of Gull Rock blank."

"Aye."

"You've got the Aldous chart right there."

Wren kept ruling. "Aldous never sailed it."

Halloran was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Good."

She wrote, in her own cramped hand, at the edge of the un-inked water:

*not seen —*

and beneath it, smaller:

*ask again in spring.*

The hardest line she'd ever drawn was the one she didn't.
