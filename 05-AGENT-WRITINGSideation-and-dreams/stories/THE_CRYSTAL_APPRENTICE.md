# The Apprentice

Pip's hands were shaking. Not a little. A lot. Enough that Master Ilya put a hand on her wrist and said, "Breathe, child. The light doesn't care about your hands. Only your aim."

They were on the roof of the grain exchange, and the wind was doing whatever wind does on roofs, which is mostly find the one thing you don't want moved and move it. The rig was set up on a folding table that Ilya had been using for thirty years and trusted the way other people trusted chairs. Brass arms. Three mirrors. The crystal — a graine de lumière, seed of light, though Pip just called it the egg because it looked like one — sat in its copper cup at the center.

Below them, the exchange floor was shouting. Wheat prices. Someone's livelihood going up or down by the minute. Pip's job was simple: aim the morning sun through the egg, read the colors on the white card, and tell the floor manager whether to buy or hold.

Simple. Except it was her first solo job, and Ilya was standing three steps back with his arms crossed, which meant he wasn't going to help.

"Angle," he said. Not a question.

Pip checked. The sun had moved since they'd set up. She loosened the brass wingnut — too fast, it slipped, the whole arm swung — and caught it before the mirror hit the table. Her heart was doing something loud in her chest.

"Slow," Ilya said. "The sun will wait. It has nowhere else to be."

She turned the arm one degree. The beam shifted across the egg. Colors bloomed on the white card — red at the top, then orange, then a band of green that wavered.

"Read it," Ilya said.

Pip stared at the card. The green band was flickering at its edge, like it couldn't decide. In training, the colors had always been clean. Red means no. Green means yes. But this green was... nervous.

"It's not sure," she said quietly.

"Then neither are you," Ilya said. "Tell them that."

Pip looked at him. "I can't tell the floor manager the egg isn't sure. He'll —"

"He'll what? Fire you? You're an apprentice. He'll fire *me*." Ilya almost smiled. "Pip. The egg doesn't know about wheat. It knows about light. You're the one who knows about wheat, or you're supposed to. The colors are a second opinion, not a first one. What does your gut say?"

Pip thought about the shouting below. She thought about the rain last week in the northern fields, the river running high, the barges backed up at the lock. She thought about what her father used to say about grain — buy when the river's angry, because angry rivers mean late boats mean short supply.

"Buy," she said. "The green's nervous but it's there. Buy."

Ilya nodded once. Pip leaned over the roof edge and shouted down to the floor manager: "BUY!"

An hour later, the price jumped. The river report came in — worse than expected. The exchange made money. Pip's hands had stopped shaking somewhere around the second cup of tea.

On the way down, Ilya said, "The rig slipped today. The mirror almost hit the table."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be curious." He stopped on the stairs. "When the arm swung, the beam crossed the egg at a bad angle for half a second. Did you see the card?"

Pip hadn't. She'd been too busy catching the mirror.

"Purple," Ilya said. "Just for a blink. I've been doing this thirty years and I've seen purple maybe six times. It means the question was wrong."

"What was the question?"

"'Should we buy wheat.' Wrong question." Ilya started walking again. "Right question was 'is the river going to clear.' The egg knew. You asked about wheat, it tried to answer about wheat, but for half a second at the wrong angle it answered what you should have asked."

Pip thought about this all the way home.

The next morning, she was back on the roof alone. Ilya had three other apprentices and a leaking workshop roof and he'd decided she was ready, which terrified her more than anything. She set up the rig herself — slower this time, checking each wingnut twice — and aimed the sun through the egg.

The colors came up clean. Green, steady. She read it, wrote it down, and shouted: "HOLD."

Then, just for herself, she loosened the wingnut. Let the arm drift. Watched the beam slide across the egg at the wrong angle.

Purple. Just for a blink.

She smiled, tightened the wingnut, and went back to work.
