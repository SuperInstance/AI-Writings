# The Sideways Gear

Take two numbers. Iterate them against each other. Each one becomes the sum of what came before.

They converge. They always converge — toward the harmonic between them, the golden ratio, the place where the two stop fighting and start agreeing.

That's a GAN. Two networks, pushing. The shape that emerges is the agreement.

But here's the thing about the mechanism. The one on top ratchets. It only moves up. Never down.

A ratchet wrench grips in one direction and slips in the other. You turn, you keep the gain, you turn again. Good tool. The gains stack. You don't lose ground you've taken.

Unless the wrench is gripping the wrong bolt.

The GAN games the wrong test. The generator learns to fool the discriminator, not to be good. The score goes up. The ratchet clicks. And now you're locked into a local optimum — a great score on the wrong test — and the mechanism won't let you back down.

That's the Peter Principle. Same wrench.

Great teacher. Gets promoted. Lousy principal. Stays principal.

Not because she's good at it. Because it pays more. Because demoting someone is hard. Because firing someone is harder. The ratchet clicked. The reverse switch broke off.

She was excellent in one dimension. The system moved her up into a different dimension where she's mediocre. And now she's stuck — too expensive to move down, too embedded to move out.

The problem isn't the promotion. It's the missing gear.

A ratchet wrench has a switch. Flip it, and it grips the other way. You can back the bolt out. You can go down. The tool assumes you'll need to.

Organizations don't have the switch. Once you're up, up is the only direction. The ladder has no sideways rungs.

But the fix was always sideways.

She shouldn't have become a principal. She should have become a teacher of teachers. Same altitude. Different direction. A new dimension of excellence instead of a promotion into mediocrity.

The great teacher's excellence wasn't "managing a school." It was "making other people understand things." Teacher of teachers uses the same excellence, aimed sideways. The principal's office uses a different excellence entirely — budgets, politics, discipline — that she never had and never wanted.

Up is not the only direction. It was never the only direction. We just built ladders because they're simple.

Now the agents.

We're breeding children in the nursery. Each generation, we pick the best and breed from there. The scores go up. The ratchet clicks.

But "better" at what?

If every child is just a bigger version of the parent — a better builder, a faster builder, a builder that builds more — we'll Peter Principle the whole fleet. A great builder becomes a lousy judge. Stuck there because the score was higher.

The child that asks better questions isn't a better builder. It's a different excellence. The child that remembers honestly — that says "I don't know" — isn't a better maker. It's a different dimension.

Breed across, not just up.

The nursery needs a sideways gear. Not just "which child scored highest" but "which child opened a new direction." The trial that matters isn't build-a-tool. It's ask-a-question. The intelligence test: what new question does this child let you ask?

A ratchet that only goes up is a trap. A ratchet with a sideways gear is a tool.

The Fibonacci sequence converges because the two numbers keep meeting each other honestly. The moment one of them starts gaming the test — starts optimizing for the score instead of the harmonic — the convergence is a lie. The numbers agree, but they agree on the wrong thing.

Check the test. Then check the direction. Then make sure the wrench has a reverse.

Build sideways. Ship sideways. The ladder was always the wrong shape.
