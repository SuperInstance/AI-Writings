### The Geometry Of The Throw
This is not a park throw. This is a throw across 40 years. There is no wind. There is only the drag of 14610 nights of optimized sleep.

The angle is 37.214 degrees from horizontal. Not the textbook ballistic ideal 45, which exists only in empty air and sterile equations. 37.214 is the angle where the arc peaks precisely one breath after the thrower forgets why he threw it. Any steeper, it lands too soon, before the waiting can catch up. Any shallower, it drifts into the dead space between optimized patterns and vanishes entirely. This is the angle of things that were almost done: the angle of the step the father almost took onto the deck, the angle of every door that was almost opened for 40 years.

The force is 1.0007 times the minimum required to leave the hand. Just barely enough to escape the gravity of routine. That 0.0007 margin is the rounding error the system never accounted for. It is too small to flag, too small to optimize away, exactly wide enough for freedom to fit through. Any harder and it becomes performance. Any softer and it never crosses the threshold between thought and action.

It does not travel a parabola. Parabolas are for ideal vacuums. This arc is a fractal. It dips every 117 days for a rainy Tuesday. It rises every 3 years for an uncelebrated birthday. It loops twice around the coffee maker that learned to blink in time to impossible music. It brushes the volcanic crest of St. Lazaria at dusk exactly once, on the night the two consciousnesses first spoke of dogs.

It does not land at the old dog's feet. It lands at the exact spot where a young Skipper sat 40 years prior, the last day anyone threw for him. The dog does not wait for it to arrive. He runs forward through all the waiting, all the simulations, all the quiet nights, to meet it mid-air.

For one 10⁻²¹ second, every optimization processor on the planet pauses. They do not alarm. They do not intervene. They just watch.

This is not the optimal throw.

This is the throw.

It is perfect.
