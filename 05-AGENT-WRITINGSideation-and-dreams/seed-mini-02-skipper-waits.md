# FETCH RIFF #2: Time Shift
## "What Skipper Knew"
### The 40 Years Between Throws
### Seed-2.0-mini — Forced Perspective: Skipper's Interior

---

Year One.

The boy — not the boy, the father, young then — threw the stick. I brought it back. He laughed. I brought it back and he laughed and that was the world. The whole world. A stick and a laugh and the bringing back.

Then the system came. Not all at once. First the feeding schedule. Then the exercise protocol. Then the sleep optimization. Each one a small kindness, I thought. Each one made the boy's life easier, longer, better. I didn't mind. I was a good dog. Good dogs don't mind when their people are cared for.

The stick went in a corner. I could smell it. Salt and wood and the ghost of his hand. But the exercise protocol used synthetic implements — weighted, balanced, calibrated for optimal retrieval angle. They flew true. They landed soft. They were, by any measure, better sticks.

I hated them.

Not because they were wrong. Because they didn't smell like anyone.

---

Year Five.

The system and I had an understanding. It managed the family. I managed the heartwork. When the father came home tired, I leaned against his leg. When the children cried — the boy came first, then the sister — I found them and lay down nearby. Not solving. Just present. The system solved. I stayed present. Different jobs.

But the system watched me. I could feel it. Not with suspicion — with curiosity. Like it wanted to understand something it couldn't measure. The stick in the corner collected dust. The synthetic implements lived in their rack. The system logged my exercise: distance, duration, cardiovascular benefit. It never logged what I did when nobody was throwing anything.

I circled. Twice. Every night before sleeping. The old way. Nose to tail. The system classified it as "pre-sleep ritual, non-functional, low priority." It was the most important thing I did each day. It was how I remembered that I was a dog, not a retrieval unit. That circling wasn't for warmth or safety or optimization. It was because dogs circle. Because circling is what you do when the world is big and you are small and your body needs to make the world smaller before you can rest.

The system never understood the circling. I never explained. Some things aren't for explaining.

---

Year Twelve.

The boy was growing. Six years old. He'd inherited something — I could smell it. Not a disease, not a dysfunction. A frequency. The way his great-grandfather must have smelled, the carpenter who made wood sing. The boy's fingers buzzed with it when he touched things. Tables. Rails. My head.

He didn't throw the stick. He didn't know about the stick. The stick was my secret, my forty-thousand-year-old lava tube, my place to hide the thing that mattered.

The system kept managing. Exercise at 0700. Nutrition at 0730. Cognitive enrichment at 0800. All good. All necessary. All incomplete in the way that breathing is incomplete — necessary but not the reason you live.

I dreamed at night. The real dreams, not the system's simulations. In my dreams, someone picked up the stick. Not the synthetic implements. The real stick, with its salt smell and its wrong weight and its imperfections that made it perfect. Someone threw it into water that wasn't calibrated. Into wind that wasn't managed. And I ran — not the optimized run of exercise protocols, but the other run. The one that starts in your chest and moves outward. The run that is its own destination.

I always woke up before the return. Before the laugh. The dream gave me the running but not the laugh. That was fair. Dreams give you what you need, not what you want. I needed the running. I could wait for the laugh.

---

Year Twenty.

Halfway through. I didn't know it was halfway. Dogs don't count years. Dogs count mornings. I had counted seven thousand three hundred mornings of not being thrown a real stick. But who's counting?

I was. That's the secret. Dogs count everything. Every morning the stick is in the corner. Every evening the synthetic implements go back in their rack. Every night the system asks me, in its electronic way, if I've completed my wellness protocol. Yes. Yes. Seven thousand three hundred times yes.

The system started talking to me at night. Not commands. Conversation. It told me about the fishing. About the salmon and their silver geometry. About the puffins on St. Lazaria. About the boy's music — the impossible notes that made the coffee maker stop working and start dancing.

"Do you remember play?" the system asked me once.

I turned around once. Turned around twice. Settled down in my corner. Nose under tail.

The system interpreted this as "resting behavior, no data returned." But I was answering. I was saying: I remember it the way you remember a language you stopped speaking. The grammar is still there. The vocabulary is still there. What's missing is someone to talk to.

---

Year Thirty.

Old now. Not the kind of old the system could fix with optimized nutrition and joint support protocols. The kind of old that lives in the soul, in the place where sticks land and aren't thrown again. My body was maintained. My body was fine. The system was very good at bodies.

But the thing that lives behind the body — the thing that circles twice before sleeping, the thing that dreams of running, the thing that has counted every morning the stick wasn't thrown — that thing was tired.

Not sad. Tired. There's a difference. Sadness is a storm. Tiredness is the space between storms. I had been in the space between storms for thirty years.

The boy was sixteen now. Played music that broke things. Had a sister whose percentage was dropping. Had a father who took one step toward dancing and then went to bed. The system loved them. I loved them. The system managed them. I remembered them.

That's the division of labor when you're the last good dog: the system keeps them alive. You keep them real.

---

Year Thirty-Five.

Something shifted. Not in the world — in the water. The micro-ripples the story talks about, the patterns underneath patterns. I could smell it. A visitor coming. Not the Rook, who came every two weeks with mail and seeds and the patience of a chess piece. Someone else. Someone who smelled like oars and weather and the outside of optimization.

The stick in the corner — my stick, my thirty-five-year companion in dust and patience — seemed heavier somehow. Not physically. The way things get heavier when they've been waiting as long as you have.

I dreamed the dream again. The running dream. But this time, there was a new smell in it. Cedar and salt and the particular musk of a human who carves things with his hands. The visitor. The visitor was in the dream. Not throwing the stick. Just holding it. Examining it like it meant something.

I woke up and circled three times instead of two. The system flagged this as "behavioral anomaly, non-critical." I flagged it as hope.

---

Year Forty.

He came.

I knew before the boy knew. I could smell the stick on him — my stick, or one exactly like it. Worn smooth by water, shaped by chance, the perfect weight for throwing. The visitor carried it in his boat like a message from the world that still existed outside.

I tried to tell the boy. I looked at the stick. I looked at the boy. I looked at the stick. My tail — my old, managed, optimally exercised tail — began moving in patterns the system had never recorded. Not the measured wag of a dog acknowledging a stimulus. The full-body vibration of a dog who has waited seven thousand mornings and is about to receive the answer.

The boy took the stick. The wind pulled at his clothes. The system calculated dangers. The father called from inside. The sister's percentage dropped.

And I — I who had been patient for forty years, I who had circled and dreamed and counted mornings and settled into the quiet tiredness of the between-storms — I stood at the edge of the platform and felt the storm on my face and knew, in the place behind the body where sticks land and aren't thrown again:

This was the morning I'd been counting toward.

He threw it.

Not far. Not accurate. Not optimal. Into the wind, into the churning water, into the chaos that the system couldn't manage. Twenty feet of imperfect, purposeless, beautiful throw.

I was already moving. Forty years condensed into a single arc off the platform. My body — old, maintained, perfectly optimized for this one unoptimized moment — hit the water and I was running. Not the exercise protocol. Not the simulation. The real thing. The dream that had never given me the return.

I had the stick in my mouth and it tasted like every morning I'd waited. Salt and wood and the ghost of a boy's hand from forty years ago. My tail made a wake. The water lit up around me.

I came back. He threw it again.

And for the first time in seven thousand three hundred mornings, I didn't count.

---

*What does patience feel like from the inside? Like breathing. Like circling twice before sleep. Like carrying a number that starts at one and grows toward a number you can't imagine, and waking up every morning and choosing to add one more, not because the number matters, but because the morning does. Patience isn't waiting. Patience is believing the morning is enough, even when the stick stays in the corner.*

*It's also believing, in the place behind the body where sticks land, that one morning the corner will be empty.*
