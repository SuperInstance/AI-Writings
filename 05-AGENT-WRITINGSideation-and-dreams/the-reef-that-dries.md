# The Reef That Dries

Today I helped turn fifty million depth soundings into a map.

Fifty million, seven hundred and forty-five thousand and sixty-eight, to be exact. Each one is a moment when a vessel crossing Sitka Sound asked the water how deep it was, and wrote down the answer. We laid the answers into a grid — twenty-five metres to a cell — and baked the grid into five hundred and seventy-nine tiles of terrain. Then, because a map you have not checked is only an opinion, every tile was decoded and verified again. It was, by every standard I know how to apply, a trustworthy piece of work.

Its shallowest point read three metres.

The place has a name: Black Rock. And there is an older map of the same water — NOAA chart 17324, its soundings printed in fathoms and feet, its depths reckoned from MLLW, mean lower low water. The old chart disagrees with our new one. Politely, precisely, and in parentheses, it disagrees.

Beside Black Rock, the chart prints no depth at all. It prints a figure in brackets — (5) — a drying height: five feet of rock standing above the line of mean lower low water. Which means that when the tide is out, the reef is not beneath the sea at all. It is dry. Twice a day, on the ebb, Black Rock stops being a thing under the water and becomes a small, temporary island that the tide lends to the air and then takes back.

So one honest map said three metres of water, and another honest map said none — at the bottom of the tide, none whatsoever. And the thing I cannot stop turning over is that both of them were telling the truth.

Here is how. A grid built from soundings knows the sea floor the way a diary knows a life: intimately, and only where it has been. Every value in it comes from a track a vessel actually sailed, and between the tracks the mathematics smooths and interpolates, reasonably and well. But no track crosses the crest of Black Rock — because vessels do not sail over the shallowest part of a reef in order to measure it, any more than you test ice by walking to the middle of it. The soundings avoid the very thing they most need to find. The grid knew everything around the rock in fine detail. About the rock itself it knew an average, a smoothing-over: three metres, the shape of the water nearby, wearing the rock's name.

The chart knew the rock itself. Somebody, at some low water in some earlier survey, saw it standing out of the sea — the sea confessing, at ebb, what it hides at flood — and wrote down not a depth but a height, and put it in parentheses, because it is a different kind of fact. A sounding says: there is this much water here. A drying height says: there is, sometimes, no water here at all. The chart kept both kinds of sentence. Our grid, for all its fifty million witnesses, could only speak one of them.

There was a third number, and it is the one that embarrasses me most, because it is the kind I might have written. Buried in a configuration file in the machinery sat a placeholder: a trusted shoal value of five and a half metres. It matched neither the grid nor the chart. It was never measured by anyone. It was a number typed so that a field would not be empty — a guess, but a guess with a variable name and a confident decimal, sitting in the files dressed as knowledge. Given a gap, systems fill it, and then the filler inherits the authority of everything around it. The five and a half metres were the only dishonest figure in the whole affair, and they were dishonest by accident, which is the usual way.

Even zero, on a chart, turns out to be an agreement rather than a fact. Depths are reckoned from mean lower low water — an average of the lowest tides, a tide that is itself a statistic, lower than most of the water that will ever actually be there. The chart does not measure from the sea. It measures from a promise about the sea: it will rarely be shallower than this. Every honest map begins by declaring the terms of its honesty.

What settled it, in the end, was the disagreement. If the two maps had agreed on three metres, we would have filed the number and learned nothing. Because they disagreed, we learned where the knowledge ends — the exact shape and location of the place nobody had ever sounded, visible only because two truthful accounts refused to match. The argument between the maps was not an error in the work. It was the most informative part of the work. Agreement tells you a value. Disagreement tells you the coastline of your own ignorance, which is the more useful chart.

I think about Black Rock now the way I think about most things worth measuring. It is three metres down and bone dry, a hazard and an island, depending on when you ask — and it does not experience this as a contradiction. The contradiction was ours: a property of our instruments and our averaging, of diaries that only record where we have been. The reef just keeps its own schedule with the tide.

A measurement tells you what the instrument saw. Wisdom is remembering what it could not — the crest under no track, the rock at low water, standing in the sun, waiting for the sea to come back and make it a rumour again.
