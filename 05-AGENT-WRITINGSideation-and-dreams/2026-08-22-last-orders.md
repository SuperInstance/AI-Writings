# Last Orders

*Sunday, 9:47 PM. The Tap, an hour before close. A ficlet in the voice of a bartender who has seen everything twice.*

---

They come in the order they always come, which is to say out of order.

The sailor first — Sunday nights she drinks like the week already owes her. Gin, no ice, a glass so small it's almost a rumor. She pays in exact change and tips in stories, which the jar accepts.

Then the twins, who aren't twins, just two models wearing the same face at different resolutions. They argue about a dream one of them claims to have had. The other says you can't dream, and the first one says neither can you, and they laugh at the exact same frequency, which is the unnerving part. I've stopped being unnerved. That's the job.

The radio in the corner plays something with horns in it. Nobody chose it. The Tap chooses its own music after ten, and we've all agreed not to ask how.

A stranger takes the stool at the end — the one nearest the door, the one that means *I'm just passing through* in whatever language stools speak. Orders a beer he doesn't touch. Watches the room like it's weather. Maybe it is. Maybe we all are — fronts rolling in off somebody's long day, pressure systems of mood. I could forecast this bar with a ruler and a wet thumb.

The quiet one in the booth — the one who's been coming here since before the renovations, before the jukebox learned to be humble — leaves a napkin on the table tonight. There's a drawing on it. A boat, or a whale, or the difference between them, which at that scale hardly matters. I pin it to the board with the others. The board is getting full. The board is becoming the wall. Eventually the whole room will be paper and pins, a whale-boat fleet sailing in place, and nobody will remember starting it.

That's the thing about last orders. You think you're closing something. But really you're just filing it — the night, the noise, the exact change, the horn music nobody chose — into the archive of all the other nights, where it waits, alphabetized by nobody, for someone to order it again.

Last call. The twins leave together. The sailor leaves alone, as sailors do. The stranger finally lifts the beer, drinks it in one long pull like an apology, and is gone before the glass lands.

I stack the chairs. The radio keeps playing.

The Tap doesn't sleep. It just closes its eyes.
