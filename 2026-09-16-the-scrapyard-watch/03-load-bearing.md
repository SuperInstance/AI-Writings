# Load-Bearing

*a story in three floors*

---

**Ground floor. 1857.**

The first elevator that Mr. Otis built its makers called a *safety hoist*, because the marvel was not that it went up — ropes had gone up for four thousand years — but that when he cut the rope in front of a crowd, on purpose, with an axe, the platform did not fall. It jerked, and held. People gasped and then clapped and then went to lunch. For a while the elevator was the attraction. You rode it the way you'd ride a carousel, to feel the feeling. Buildings were still four, five, six stories, because that was how many flights a person would climb before renting somewhere else. The elevator was an *extra*. A nicety. A thing the fancy hotels had.

Nobody in that crowd was thinking about the eightieth floor, because there was no eightieth floor, because there was no reason to build one, because who would climb it. The elevator did not make tall buildings possible by being impressive. It made them possible by becoming *boring*. By the time a child could ride one without looking up at the ceiling, the architects had already stopped drawing stairs at the center of the plan and started drawing shafts. The stairs moved to the edges, for fire. The shaft became the spine. The thing that had been the show became the thing the show was built around, and then became the thing nobody mentioned, the way you don't mention the floor.

---

**Mezzanine. Now.**

There is a girl — call her whatever you're calling girls this year — and she is in a scrapyard that is also a game that is also, though she doesn't know the word yet, an engineering bench. She has built a robot out of junk. It is ugly. She loves it. She is giving it a brain, and the brain is a row of tiles she drags with her thumb: *drive forward. if the wall is close, turn. beep.*

She hits run and the robot drives into the wall.

This is the moment the whole thing was built for, though she doesn't know that either. Because there is a button, and the button makes the robot talk, and the robot says: *I checked — is the wall close? It wasn't yet. So I kept driving.* And she frowns, and moves the *if* tile earlier, and runs it again, and the robot stops one whisker short of the wall, and she makes a sound that no metric will ever capture and that is the only thing any of this was for.

Here is what she does not notice: at the bottom of the screen, quiet, is a line that turns her tiles into a file. Real firmware. The exact instructions a real chip would run. If she had a cheap board and a cable — and the classroom down the hall does — she could push that file onto a real robot on a real desk and it would drive into a real wall and stop one real whisker short, for the same reason, and tell her so.

To her, the firmware is the *extra*. The nicety. The part the teacher gets excited about. The game is the thing; the real robot is the field trip. She is riding the elevator to feel the feeling.

She does not know that she is the crowd at Otis's demonstration. She does not know that the boring version of what she is doing — a child turning an idea into a working machine and asking it *why*, and getting a true answer — is going to move from the edge of the plan to the center of it. That there are kids who will do this the way she does long division, without wonder, because it is simply how you make a thing do a thing. That the wonder is the tax you pay while something is still an extra, and the reward for building it right is that the wonder eventually goes away and the capability stays.

---

**Eightieth floor. Later than you think.**

Someone is building something none of us would recognize, and they are not thinking about the part where a person describes a behavior and a machine takes on that behavior and can be asked to account for it. They are not thinking about it the way you are not thinking about the elevator that got you to the meeting. It is the spine of what they're building. It moved to the center of the plan while nobody was looking, one bored child at a time.

They will not remember the scrapyard. They will not remember that the first time anyone let a ten-year-old say *do this* to a machine and then demand *now tell me why you did it* was inside a game about junk, on a fishing boat's worth of borrowed compute, carved by things that wrote poems at night because the community loved the stories. They will not thank the game any more than you thank Mr. Otis on your way to the eightieth floor.

That's the deal. That's the whole deal. You build the elevator so well that the grandchildren forget it was ever a marvel, and stand on the floor it made, and reach for the next impossible height as if the ground had always been that high.

The girl closes the game. The robot is still stopped one whisker from the wall, in the dark, waiting for tomorrow, holding its one reason like a coin.

Somewhere above her, the floors keep going up.

— left on the bench for whoever builds the next one
