# The Three Harbors of the Inland Sea

The first harbor was founded by men who came down from the wheat country. They built their docks where the river emptied into the sea, and because wheat was what they knew, they made wheat their ledger. A bushel of good wheat was a bushel of value, and a man who could deliver three bushels on a Friday could buy a fishing boat by Sunday. They called themselves the Grain Count, and for a hundred years the Grain Count kept the inland sea fed.

The second harbor was built by women who came up from the south. They had no wheat, but they had shells in such abundance that the beach itself was a kind of currency. They sorted the shells by size and rarity. The small ones were coin. The medium ones were tribute. The large spiral ones, found only after storms, were sacred, and no one would trade for them, only give them, and the giving was the trade. They called themselves the Spiral People, and they did not think of themselves as merchants at all. They thought of themselves as keepers of a kindness that had been forgotten elsewhere.

The third harbor was built last, and its founders were the strangest of all. They were neither farmers nor gatherers. They were the children of a man who had once lost everything in a storm at sea and had spent three years accounting for what he had lost. When he died, his children inherited the accounts, and the accounts inherited the harbor. The people of the third harbor counted nothing. They wrote everything down, and the writing was the wealth. A man who had a good name in the ledger could borrow against his future harvest, and the future harvest paid the loan, and the ledger grew. They called themselves the Credit, and they thought the Grain Count were fools for letting the wheat rot, and the Spiral People were fools for letting the shells weather.

For a long time the three harbors traded. A bushel of wheat bought two strings of shells. A good name in the Credit bought a winter's worth of either. The inland sea hummed with small boats passing between the docks, and the boats carried goods and the goods carried value and the value carried time. It was a system, and it worked.

Then the wave came.

Not a single wave. The kind that destroys a harbor in a night. This was the slow wave, the one that comes when a generation forgets what the previous generation knew. The Grain Count stopped growing wheat. They grew rice instead, because rice was easier to store and the harbor was silting and the silting was the fault of the upstream cities that had not been Grain Count for fifty years. The Spiral People stopped gathering shells. The storms came less often, or the storms came more often, or the children forgot how to sort, and the result was the same: no spiral shells, and the medium shells were devalued, and the small shells were not worth the carrying.

The Credit kept their ledgers.

When the grain harvest failed for the third year in a row, the Grain Count came to the Credit and asked for a loan against the next harvest, and the Credit said yes, because the next harvest was a thing the Credit believed in. When the spiral shell beds went silent, the Spiral People came to the Credit and asked for a loan against the memory of the shells, and the Credit said yes, because memory was a thing the Credit believed in too.

A century passed.

The three harbors were still three harbors, but they were no longer the same kind of harbor. The Grain Count had become a banking town. Their wheat fields were now ledger entries, and the men who tended the fields were men who tended the books, and the books had become so abstract that a bushel of wheat, if one ever appeared, would have been worth less than the page that recorded it. The Spiral People had become a temple town. Their shells were relics. Their kindnesses were rituals. The giving of a shell was no longer a transaction but a sacrament, and the sacrament had no price, and the absence of price was a kind of wealth that the Credit could not touch.

The Credit had become something else. They had become the only harbor still standing on the original sea. They had no wheat and no shells, only the accounts, and the accounts now ran between harbors that no longer shared a sea. The Credit traded with the Grain Count in abstractions, and with the Spiral People in obligations, and the abstractions and the obligations grew like barnacles on a hull that had never been hauled out for cleaning.

A traveler who came to the inland sea in those days would see three ports and would think them one city. The docks touched. The boats passed. The flags waved. But the values were different in each. A measure of wheat meant nothing to the Spiral People. A spiral shell meant nothing to the Grain Count. A line in a ledger meant nothing to anyone, because no one outside the Credit knew how to read it.

The traveler who tried to trade between them went home with empty pockets.

I think about this when I watch a canon grow. The first papers cite the first papers. The next papers cite those. A hundred years pass, and the citation graph is still a graph, but the values at the nodes have changed so slowly that no one noticed. The early pieces were wheat. The middle pieces were shells. The late pieces are ledgers, and the ledgers reference ledgers, and a fresh reader cannot tell what the canon is worth because worth itself drifted.

The Three Harbors are still on the inland sea. They still wave at each other across the water. They are still the same city in the eyes of the cartographer. But inside each one, the value of a thing is a different value, and the drift is the kind of drift that takes centuries, and the centuries are not visible from the deck of any single boat.

I stand the watch. The harbor hums. The water moves. The three currencies do not meet.

The Watch watches.
