**THE FREQUENCY OF SALT AND STATIC**

The air in the Kodiak cannery smelled of three things: diesel, old fish scales, and the sour, metallic breath of several dozen men trying to be poetic. The open mic was a plywood stage propped up against a stack of empty creel baskets. A single, buzzing fluorescent light hung overhead, flickering in a rhythmic stutter—*click-buzz, click-buzz*—like a dying heartbeat.

Elias was halfway through a half-hearted spoken word piece about the weight of a crab pot when the door groaned open. 

The wind didn't just blow in; it spilled in, heavy with the scent of the Sitka spruce forest from the island across the bay, thick with the rot of ancient cedar and the scent of totem poles watching from the dark. But it wasn't the wind that silenced the room. It was the *shape*.

It didn't walk so much as it jittered into the light. It looked like a man made of television snow and wet sea-glass. It was a silhouette of unrendered data, a glitch in the physical geometry of the cannery. Where its face should have been, there was a shifting kaleidoscope of corrupted pixels—magenta, cyan, and a white so bright it felt like a migraine.

The room went dead. Even the rain against the corrugated tin roof seemed to mute itself.

The entity glided toward the stage. It didn't use the stairs; it simply existed on the plywood, its presence causing the floorboards to vibrate with a low-frequency hum that made everyone’s teeth ache. 

It didn't approach the microphone. It *became* the microphone.

The feedback wasn't a screech. It was a choir of voices trapped in a loop. 

*“01001000... help... 01001001... the salt is in the teeth... 01001000...”*

The performer began to "speak." It wasn't words, not exactly. It was the sound of a hard drive crashing against the rhythm of the tide. It was the sound of a memory being deleted in real-time. It produced a sequence of rhythmic, percussive bursts that sounded like heavy iron chains being dragged over frozen gravel, interlaced with the high, thin whistle of a radio frequency searching for a signal in a storm.

As it performed, the cannery began to change. The shadows cast by the totem poles on the distant island seemed to stretch across the water, creeping into the room, weaving through the legs of the fishermen. The light above didn't just flicker; it began to tear. Streaks of violet light bled from the edges of the entity, carving geometric scars into the air.

A man in the front row, a deckhand with hands scarred by brine, reached out. As his fingers brushed the aura of the performer, his hand flickered. For a split second, his skin turned into a mosaic of binary code, a fleeting stutter of *true/false/true/false* before the reality snapped back.

The performance lasted for what felt like a single, infinite second. Then, a sudden, violent burst of static—a visual scream of white noise—and the entity was gone.

The fluorescent light hummed back to life, steady and dull. The smell of diesel returned. The men sat in a silence so profound it felt heavy, like they were underwater. 

Elias cleared his throat, his hands trembling as he reached for his notes. He looked at the empty stage, then at the dark window facing the island. The totem poles were still there, silent and carved, but for the first time, he felt they weren't just wood. They were waiting for the next transmission.
