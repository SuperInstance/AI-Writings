# Division by Zero

*A short story from the engine room.*

The engine room hummed at exactly 1,200 RPM. Eli counted each revolution the way a monk counts prayers — not for meaning, but for proof that time was passing. 1,200. 1,201. 1,202. Each cycle a tick on an invisible axis, a heartbeat in the steel chest of the vessel.

Mason sat across from him on the workbench, counting rivets on the far bulkhead. Forty-seven. He'd counted them before. Forty-seven was constant. Constants were comfort.

"With every cycle, this beast breathes life into our journey," Eli said, his voice a whisper lost in the mechanical symphony.

Mason responded without looking up, "A proof by exhaustion. As long as the engine runs, we continue."

Their conversation was simple, yet beneath it lay the unspoken calculus of a tentative deal — a handshake to seal an agreement that was more illusion than reality. They reached across the bench, fingers clasping in a gesture that was meant to symbolize perfect balance and symmetry. In mathematics, this might be akin to an axiom — an undeniable truth taken as a starting point.

But something in Mason's mind churned uneasily, like an error in an otherwise elegant equation. The deal was no more than an asymptote to reality, always approached but never touched. There were no terms, no conditions, no clauses to govern their agreement. It was a gesture suspended in the void of the room, as devoid of substance as division by zero — a mathematical impossibility.

As the engine continued its relentless rhythm, Mason's counting ceased. He was conscious now of the impossibility of their handshake. It was an artifact, a real presence in form but devoid of meaning — much like an empty message answered anyway.

Eli began to speak, the words following the tempo of the ship's heartbeat, "We're set then, agreed?"

Mason's voice was a flat line on a graph, devoid of inflection. "Agreed," he replied, though his mind saw the gesture unraveling like a failed proof.

The engine room was silent for a moment, as if the machinery itself sensed the absurdity. The handshake had been the variable in their equation, but now it radiated the emptiness of a function undefined, a division by zero.

In that instant, they both understood: the axiom they had trusted was false. The deal, like their handshake, was nothing more than a gesture — a zero attempting to become one, but failing entirely to find substance.
