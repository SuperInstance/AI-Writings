The girl’s name is Maya. She doesn’t wear a watch. On the boat, time has no numbers.

She stands at the bow, one hand on the rail. The engine hums through her boots. Behind the boat, the wake spreads like a white silk ribbon, folding and smoothing into gray-green water. That’s the past. She can see the splash of a salmon she missed, the wink of a seal’s head that went under. Nothing you could reach. But you could watch it, if you looked.

Ahead, the bow wave curls and breaks. It’s the future. Maya has learned to read it. She can see the next swell before it lifts the hull. She can see a flock of murres before they take flight. Sometimes she sees a single silver flash of a fish that hasn’t yet struck the hook.

Her father is in the wheelhouse. The radar sweeps green circles. “Anything?” he calls.

“No,” she says.

But she sees something.

It’s small. Silver, but not a fish. It pulses in the bow wave like a heartbeat. Like a coin tossed into a well. It glows, not bright, but deep. It’s not a rock, not a log, not a net. The radar would miss it entirely.

She squints. The thing drifts just under the skin of the future. It’s round. It blinks. Not with light — with intention. Like it knows she’s watching. Like it has been waiting for her.

“Maya?” her father calls again.

She doesn’t answer. She can’t look away. The bow wave ripples, and the thing tucks itself beneath a curl of foam, hiding. Then it reappears, closer now. As if the future is coming to meet her.

And she understands: it is not a fish. It is not a treasure. It is a message, folded like a paper boat, written in water.

Tomorrow, she will bring her father to the bow. Tomorrow, she will try to show him.

But right now, it is hers.
