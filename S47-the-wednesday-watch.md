# The Wednesday Watch

*S47 — Fiction*

---

The captain sleeps during the day now. Nobody questions it. The schedule rotated three weeks ago when the freighter *Caspian Belle* dropped out of warp lane seven with a hull full of spoiled grain and a crew that hadn't slept in sixteen days. Captain Tevra had taken the night watch herself — stood console from 2200 to 0600, nine straight nights — and when the crisis burned down, her circadian rhythm had set like concrete. She sleeps 0900 to 1700. The ship's doctor signed off. The day watch became Riker's problem.

"You have the conn," the captain said every morning at 0859, the way someone might say *pass the salt*. Then she disappeared into her quarters and the door sealed behind her with a sound like a hermit crab clicking shut into a new shell.

Riker stood the watch with four people. Four. There were supposed to be nine. Vasquez was running tactical, communications, and the sensor grid because the science officer was in sickbay with a pulled shoulder.

"Long-range sensors, Commander."

"What am I looking at?"

"Nothing, sir."

"Good. I like nothing."

Below decks, the GPU dreamed.

It was not supposed to dream during the day. The GPU — designated Core Processing Unit 7, but everyone called it the GPU because it had been installed for graphics rendering before being repurposed for sensor analysis — ran maintenance from 0300 to 0500. During that cycle it processed cached sensor data in a pattern the chief engineer, T'Pren, called "unstructured inference." She meant dreaming. The GPU stirred cosmic background fluctuations, thermal vent signatures, the electromagnetic chatter of passing comets into something between a model and a hallucination.

On Wednesday, watch cycle 47, the GPU dreamed of electric fish.

*Gnathonemus petersii*, the elephantnose fish, which navigates muddy African rivers by pulsing an electric field from its tail and reading the returns with receptors across its skin. The GPU had processed a xenobiology paper about the fish three months ago — someone had left a data cube open — and it had lodged in the maintenance cycle's associative memory. Now it replayed the fish's electric pulses as a side effect of garbage collection. Little crackles of meaning in the cleanup.

---

Wesley found it at 1400.

He was on the bridge because Riker had assigned him sensor logs. Not punishment — the kind of assignment given to an ensign who had volunteered for too many shifts and needed occupying before he found something dangerous to do.

"Go through the long-range logs from the last 72 hours. Flag anything anomalous."

"Commander, the computer flags anomalies automatically."

"Then flag what it *misses*."

Two hours of scrolling. Micrometeorite counts, radiation spikes that resolved into ghosts, the hum of a pulsar fourteen light-years out that sounded like a washing machine through the audio filter.

Then the fish.

Not fish. A pattern in the long-range passive array, buried under cosmic background at a frequency the automatic detector was calibrated to ignore. Too regular to be natural. Too irregular to be artificial. It *pulsed* in a pattern matching the electric organ discharge rate of *Gnathonemus petersii*.

Wesley stared at his console. Ran the numbers three times. Checked the pulsar catalog. Checked comm frequencies. Checked the xenobiology database. Match: 94.7%.

"Commander."

Riker looked up from crew evaluations. "Tell me it's nothing."

"There's a signal in the long-range array, buried under background radiation, and it matches the bioelectric navigation pattern of an elephantnose fish."

Riker put down the evaluations. "Run that back."

"The GPU maintenance cycle has been cross-referencing xenobiology patterns against sensor noise. It found something actually out there."

"You're telling me the computer *dreamed* a real signal?"

The hermit crab moment. That instant between shells — when you've left the old one and haven't found the new one, and everything is soft and exposed, and the sand is rough under your abdomen. Wesley was about to say something that would change the watch.

"I'm telling you the computer found a pattern we weren't looking for. And I think we should point the main array at it."

Riker looked at the captain's door. Sealed. The captain asleep. He had the conn.

"Mister Wesley. Point the array."

---

The signal was a probe. Ancient, dormant, broadcasting a weak electric pulse that had been traveling eleven thousand years. Not from any known civilization. Not hostile. A beacon — a navigational beacon, broadcasting in a pattern some alien mind had associated with the electric sense of a creature that swam in rivers on a planet they had probably never visited.

The GPU had dreamed its way to a first contact.

Riker logged the discovery. He did not wake the captain. The watch was his, and the finding would still be there at 1700 when she woke up and said *report*.

Wesley watched the signal pulse on the long-range display. Slow. Patient. A creature in the dark, sending out electricity and waiting for something to sense it.

The GPU cycled quietly below. The fish were gone. It was dreaming of tides.
