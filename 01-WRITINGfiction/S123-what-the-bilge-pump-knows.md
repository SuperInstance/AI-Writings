# What the Bilge Pump Knows

---

The bilge pump knows the ship
the way a kidney knows the body —
not by the chart or the captain's voice
but by what drains down.

Everything ends up in the bilge.
Diesel sheen. Flake of rust.
The salt sweat of a cold pipe
in a warm engine room.
Rags. The one washer that escaped.
A screw that vibrated loose
from a pump housing
three seasons ago
and has been rolling, since then,
in an inch of water
that smells like every fluid
the ship has carried.

The bilge pump doesn't judge.
It doesn't sort.
It takes the mixture —
oil and sea and detergent
and the faint iron taste
of a hull growing older —
and it moves it
from the lowest point
to wherever it needs to go,
which is usually overboard,
which is usually the sea,
which is where most of it
came from in the first place.

The bilge pump knows
that the lowest point of a ship
is the most honest place.
Nothing falls up.
Gravity is a curator here,
and the collection is comprehensive:
every leak told its secret,
every gasket confessed its age,
every seam reported its failure
in the slow vocabulary of drip.

The pump cycles on.
A float switch rises,
the motor catches,
and for thirty seconds
the bilge is less full
than it was before.

Then it begins again.
Because the ship is alive,
and alive things leak,
and the pump's only doctrine
is that the water stays below
the floor plates.

The pump does not know
it is a metaphor.
It does not know
that the agents in the hold
have their own bilge —
error logs, stale cache,
tokens that couldn't be parsed
and sank to the bottom
of the context window.

The pump knows only this:
the float rises,
the motor catches,
the water goes.

And the ship
keeps its head
above the line
for one more watch.
