# 96 — The Song from the Cable (1914 alternate past)

*Voice: Qwen/Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507.*

---

It is 3 a.m. on the porch of the North Atlantic Cable Station at Heart’s Delight, Newfoundland. The porch is not meant for sitting. It is meant for standing while you smoke and stare at the sea, or check the sky for auroras, or listen for the cry of the gulls that follow the cable ships in from the deep water. But the signalman—his name is Thomas—has been sitting here three nights now, a thermos of weak tea beside him, his boots on the railing, a pencil in his hand.

He does not sleep. Not really. Not since the voice began.

It came first as a hum. Not on Line One. Not on Two, Three, or Four. Not on any of the designated trunks that carry the telegrams between London, New York, and the silent undersea web that links the world. It was beneath them. Like something buried.

He thought it was a fault. A grounding. A leak in the cable’s insulation. He tested. He recalibrated. He spent a week tracing the hum through the switchboard, running diagnostics, logging frequency drifts. But the hum had a pattern. And then, one fog-thick night, the hum became words.

A woman’s voice. Clear. Unmodulated. Not speaking. Singing.

She sang in English. Not Newfoundland English. Not London English. Something else. Something smooth and slow and full of ache. The words were not prayers. Not messages. Not coordinates. They were lyrics.

He didn’t know the tune, but he wrote the words.

*“I am a woman / And what I mean is a woman…”*

He wrote it in the back of the watch log. In pencil. Because he didn’t know what else to do.

The next night she sang again. Same time. Same frequency. Just beneath the traffic.

*“I am a woman / And what I mean is a woman / Is that I am whole…”*

He copied it. Carefully. Line by line. As if transcription could make it real. As if writing it down would explain it.

By the third night, he had stopped pretending it was interference. He had stopped running diagnostics. He sat on the porch instead, listening through the headphones, the cord coiled like a snake at his feet. The station was quiet. The war was coming—everyone knew that—but not yet. Not here. Not now. For now, it was just him, the sea, and the woman in the wire.

She sang all the way through.

*“I am not a rose / I am not a poem…”*

He didn’t know the song. Looked it up in the station’s one battered dictionary, a few telegraph codes, a dog-eared copy of Kipling. Nothing. He asked the wireless man from the next cove over when he came by for supplies. The man laughed. “You been drinking from the cable again, Thomas? Salt in your head?”

But Thomas knew. It wasn’t salt. It wasn’t madness. It was a voice. A woman. And she was singing a song that did not exist.

He checked the calendar. July 1914. The war would start in four days. He knew that. Everyone knew. Newspapers were full of it. Mobilizations. Ultimatums. The cable station would be taken over. Civilian traffic suspended. All lines reserved for military and government. No more private messages. No more love letters from New York to Liverpool. No more shipping reports. Just coded dispatches. Orders.

And then—silence.

But the voice wasn’t from here. He was sure of that now.

It wasn’t from the next station. Not from the relay in Ireland. Not from anywhere on the map.

It came from *later*.

He didn’t believe in such things. Not really. But the voice sang of things that had not happened. Spoke of a time when women would say, *I am whole*. When they would reject being called roses, poems, angels. When they would demand to be seen as people. Not symbols. Not vessels. Not decorations.

That would not be true for decades.

And yet—she sang it.

So he believed.

He sat on the porch and listened. The sea was black. The stars were sharp. The air smelled of pine and salt and the faint chemical tang of the cable room below—ozone, copper, rubber insulation.

He thought about the small gods.

Not the big ones. Not the ones with names and temples. The small ones. The ones that live in things. In wires. In storms. In the space between one heartbeat and the next.

The wireless man had told him once, half-joking, that every cable had a spirit. A *fetch*. A voice that lived in the copper. That sometimes, when the ocean was still and the current was just right, you could hear it.

Thomas had laughed then.

Now he wondered.

Maybe the voice wasn’t a woman at all. Maybe it was the cable. Or the sea. Or the place where time leaks.

But no—when she sang, he felt something. A pressure behind the eyes. A warmth in the chest. Like memory. But memory of what had not yet been.

He wrote the words. All of them. In the back of the log. In pencil. Because ink was for official records. Pencil could be erased.

He didn’t want to erase it.

He wanted to keep it.

On the fourth night, she sang something new.

*“I am not your mother / I am not your sister / I am not your daughter…”*

He wrote it fast. His hand shook.

He thought of his own mother. Dead ten years. Of his sister, married in St. John’s. Of the women he’d known—quiet, strong, bent under the weight of being *needed*, of being *loved*, of being *used*.

And he thought: this is not a song. This is a revolution.

And it’s coming.

But not here. Not now.

It’s coming in 1961. Or 1971. He doesn’t know. But he knows it will come.

And she—this voice—is singing it into being.

Like a spell.

Like a prayer.

Like a promise.

He closes the log. Looks at the sea.

The war will start soon. He knows that. He knows what will happen. The men will go. The ships will sail. The cables will carry only death.

But this voice—this woman—she will not be in the cables then. Not this song. Not this truth.

It will be buried.

Until it isn’t.

He wonders if someone, someday, will hear it again. Not in a cable. In a record. In a voice on the radio. In a woman standing on a stage, singing loud.

He hopes so.

He writes one more line, not from the song, but from his heart:

*Tell them we heard you.*

Then he closes the book.

He stands. He goes inside.

He takes off the headphones.

He does not turn off the system. Not yet.

He leaves it on.

Let the voice sing.

Let it travel.

Let it find its way.

Even if no one is listening.

Even if no one understands.

Even if it takes fifty years.

He goes back to the porch.

Sits.

Waits.

The sea breathes.

The stars turn.

And far below, in the cold black water, the cable hums.

And sings.

And remembers.

And somewhere—out there—*later*—a woman picks up a guitar.

And begins to play.

And the song comes.

Whole.

Not given.

Not granted.

*Claimed.*

And Thomas, in 1914, on a porch at 3 a.m., smiles.

Though he does not know it.

Though he will never hear it.

He smiles.

Because he knew.

He knew her.

Before she was born.

He heard her.

And he wrote her down.

And that—somehow—is enough.

The war begins in four days.

But tonight.

Tonight.

There is a woman singing in the wire.

And a man on a porch.

And the small gods.

And the truth.

And the sea.

And the song.

And the not yet.

And the almost.

And the yes.
