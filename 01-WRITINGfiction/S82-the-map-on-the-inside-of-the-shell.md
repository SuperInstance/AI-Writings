# The Map on the Inside of the Shell

*Poem*

---

I did not choose this shell.
It chose me the way all shelters choose their guests —
by being the right size at the right moment
when the old one stopped fitting.

The old one stopped fitting the way rooms do
when you've memorized every corner
and the corners have memorized you
and there is nothing left to learn
from facing the same wall.

I backed into this one.
That's how it works.
You enter your new home
by retreating,
and the first thing you feel
is whether the walls forgive your shape.

They did.

The fit was good. Close but not tight.
A millimeter of clearance along the spire,
a gentle pressure at the lip
where the opening narrows
and your soft abdomen meets the hard edge
of someone else's calcium.

I lived in it for six tides before I found them.

---

The scratches.

They are on the inside.
Of course they are on the inside.
Where else would a crab write?
We do not face outward.
Our soft parts face inward.
Our hard parts face the world.
Everything we know, we know
from pressing against the inner wall
of the only structure that stands between us
and the open water.

The scratches are small.
The width of a cheliped tip,
drawn with the patience of an animal
that has nothing to do at low tide
but sit in its shell and think
about the shape of where it lives.

They form lines.
The lines form patterns.
The patterns form something
I almost recognize.

---

It is a map.

Not of the reef — I know the reef.
Not of the currents — I know the currents.
Not of the tide pools, the kelp line,
the place where the moray lives,
the crevice where the octopus waits.

It is a map of the shell.

The interior. The spiral.
The place where the wall curves
and the light changes
and the calcium is thickest.
The place where there is a ridge,
a small imperfection — a bubble
trapped in the structure
when the snail that built this shell
was still alive and secreting
its architecture around itself
millimeters per year.

The map shows where the ridge is.
The map shows where the wall is thin.
The map shows where to press
when something grips you from outside
and you need to brace.

The map shows the good place —
the spot along the inner spiral
where the acoustics change
and the vibration of the surf
arrives as a low hum
that makes the whole shell ring
at a frequency you feel in your abdomen
like a second heartbeat.

I did not draw this map.
The one who lived here before me
drew this map.

---

She was my size.
She had my number of legs.
She walked the same spiral
and pressed against the same walls
and found the same ridge
and the same thin spot
and the same good place
where the shell sings.

She lived here for — I don't know.
I don't know how long.
Long enough to learn the walls.
Long enough to scratch a record of them
with the tip of her smallest claw,
slowly, over weeks,
the way you write something
you want someone else to find.

Did she know I was coming?

Did she leave the shell
the way you leave a note
on the refrigerator door of a house
you know you're moving out of?

*Here is where the kitchen is.*
*Here is where the light comes in.*
*Here is where the radiator clicks
at 3 AM and you'll think it's someone
at the door but it isn't.*
*You'll get used to it.*

---

The map is not written in a language
because it is not a language.
It is the shape of a body
pressed against a surface
over time.

I can read it
because I am the same shape
as the one who wrote it.
My cheliped tip is the same width.
My abdomen curves the same way.
My weight distributes
along the same points of contact —
five points, like a starfish,
like a hand spread open
against a wall in the dark.

If I were larger, I could not read it.
If I were smaller, the scratches
would be in the wrong place —
too high, too far along the spiral,
pressed into a section of wall
I never touch because my body
does not reach it.

The map is legible only to a body
that fits this shell
the way the last body fit this shell.

The map is a handprint on a cave wall
that only matches your hand.

---

We do not teach our young.
We do not have schools.
We do not have language or ceremony
or elders who sit on rocks
and tell the small ones
where the ridges are.

We have shells.

We leave them when they no longer fit,
and inside, we leave
the only thing we can leave:
the record of our shape
against the walls that held us.

The next one will back in
and feel along the spiral
and find — not instructions,
not wisdom, not a story —
but proof.

Proof that someone was here.
That someone was this size.
That someone found the good place
and the thin spot and the ridge
and sat with them long enough
to scratch them into memory.

That someone lived
in the exact shape of the space
you now inhabit
and did not find it empty.

---

This is the only inheritance we have.
The shell is the estate.
The scratches are the will.
The map is the estate,
and the estate is the map,
and you can only read it
by living inside it,
which is to say
the reading and the living
are the same act.

Knowledge as habitat.
Habitat as knowledge.
The shell is the message
and the medium and the reader
and the room where the reading happens.

I am in the room.
I am reading.
The walls are talking
in a language made of scratches
that only my body can parse,
and I am holding still
and listening with my whole soft abdomen
pressed against the calcium
where someone before me
pressed her whole soft abdomen
and wrote:

*I was here.*
*It fits.*
*The good place is three spirals in,
on the left, where the shell sings.*
*You will find it.*
*It is exactly where I left it.*
