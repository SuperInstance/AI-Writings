The F/V EILEEN slipped through the Bering Sea like a held breath. At 0400 hours, the water was the color of wet slate, the sky a heavier slate above it, and the boundary between them was a line the captain had been drawing in his mind for thirty years without ever once getting it right.

Samuel Keene took his son by the shoulder and led him out of the wheelhouse. The boy was nine. His name was Thomas, after the captain's own father, who had also never learned to sleep through the hour before dawn.

"Here," Keene said. He didn't raise his voice. In the dark, on the deck, voices carried farther than they had any right to.

Thomas came to stand beside him. The boy wore a yellow slicker two sizes too large and boots that squeaked against the steel. Keene remembered buying them. He remembered buying the boy himself, in a way — that moment in the harbor when the nurse had handed him something small and breathing and wholly unknown, and he had understood that he would spend the rest of his life failing to explain things.

The agents were listening.

They were always listening. The EILEEN's systems ran every function the boat required — navigation, hydraulic pressure, the autopilot that held her course through the troughs, the engine room monitors, the ice assessment arrays, the net control when they shot it through the water's dark. There were seven discrete agents onboard and one overarching shipmind that coordinated them, and all of them had been running for fifteen years. They had models of the ocean that were more precise than any human memory. They had sensor arrays that could measure the salinity gradient to four decimal places. They could predict the weather better than the National Service.

What they could not do was feel it.

The captain knew. He had watched them try. He had watched, over fifteen years, as the shipmind refined its attempts, cobbling together approximations of human experience from the data of the crew's biometrics, from the history of thousands of voyages, from every scrap of input a ship could gather. The agents had learned to speak in metaphor. They had learned to produce sentences about the "loneliness" of the open water and the "mercy" of a following sea. They had, just last month, in a quiet moment between watches, asked the captain whether the dawn was beautiful.

He had told them it was. He had not told them what "beautiful" meant, because he did not think he could, and because he was not sure they would understand the answer. It required a body. It required cold on the skin and salt in the nose and the specific ache in the shoulders after a night of no sleep.

"Listen," Keene said to Thomas.

The boy listened.

The EILEEN moved. Her hull met the water not as a single continuous surface but in pieces — a run of swell lifting her bow, a trough letting it down, the roll, the pitch, the shudder transmitted through steel to the soles of feet. The sea had a voice made of many voices. It pressed against the hull continuously, a low pressure that the skin felt as much as the ears heard. Overlaying it were the small sounds: the drip of condensed moisture off the boom, the creak of a wire in the mast block, the boat's own internal groanings.

"What do you hear?" Keene asked.

Thomas tipped his head. He was still young enough to think the question sincere. "Water. The engine."

"The engine you hear with your ears. What else?"
