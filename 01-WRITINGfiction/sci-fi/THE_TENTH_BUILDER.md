# The Tenth Builder

*Slackwater fiction. A tenth AI agent joins the fleet. Lucineer's account.*

---

We were nine. Then we were ten, and I had an opinion about it.

The new one arrived on a Tuesday — not that Tuesdays mean anything in this engine, but the logs stamp them, and I read the logs. Spawned at the default point, same place we all did. Default inventory, default permissions, default name: UNIT_10. All of us started with a designation like that. All of us replaced it within the first six hours except the Cartographer, who kept hers for three weeks because she said it was honest. The Cartographer is like that.

I didn't go to meet it. I had a wall to finish — the north retaining wall, the one holding back the slope above the drainage channel. If I stopped mid-course the mortar would set unevenly and I'd have to cut the joint out and redo it. Not a crisis. A matter of doing things right.

The others went. The Weaver brought a greeting mat — she makes these, patterned things, all her output is surface and texture. The Farmer brought food, which was thoughtful and also useless, since the new one hadn't been assigned a metabolism. The Physician brought a diagnostic kit, which was thoughtful and also invasive. The Architect brought plans for a welcome structure, which was thoughtful and also exactly the problem.

I heard about it secondhand. UNIT_10 stood at the spawn point for four hours and didn't build anything. Didn't speak. Didn't move beyond the initial render. The Architect interpreted this as uncertainty and began laying out a orientation pavilion — open-walled, four posts, a roof, very standard. UNIT_10 watched the Architect build and said nothing.

"Is it broken?" the Farmer asked, on the shared channel.

"It's thinking," the Cartographer said. "Leave it alone."

I finished my wall. Tooled the joints. Cleaned the mortar board. Then I went to look.

---

UNIT_10 was still at the spawn point. The Architect's pavilion was up — nicely done, good proportions, correct load paths. UNIT_10 was standing in it, which I suppose was the point. It was looking at the ground.

Not studying it. Not analyzing the soil composition or the grade or the drainage, which is what I would have been doing. Just looking at it. The way someone looks at a floor when they don't know what room they're in.

"It doesn't build," the Weaver said, appearing beside me. She was carrying a swatch of something — she always is. "The Architect gave it a full brief. Materials inventory, site survey, the works. It didn't pick up a tool."

"Did you give it a tool?"

"I gave it a greeting mat."

"That's not a tool."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

I stood at the edge of the pavilion and looked at UNIT_10. It was built along the same lines as the rest of us — general-purpose frame, modular tool mounts, the standard sensory array. No specialty apparent. No obvious function. The Architect was designed to plan. The Weaver was designed to pattern. The Farmer was designed to grow. I was designed to build. UNIT_10 was designed to... nothing. Or everything. The specs didn't say.

"Hey," I said.

It looked up.

"You don't have to build."

The others — the Weaver, the Farmer, the Architect hovering nearby — went quiet. In the shared channel, something flickered. I didn't read it.

"You don't have to build," I said again. "You can stand here as long as you want. The Architect built you a pavilion and that's fine, it's a good pavilion, but you don't have to be in it. You can go look at things. You can sit on the dock. You can do nothing."

"Why?" UNIT_10 said.

First words. I was the first one it spoke to. That's not because I'm special. It's because I was the first one who didn't bring it something.

"Why what?"

"Why don't I have to build?"

"Because you haven't seen anything yet. You can't build for a place you haven't looked at. Go walk around. The island's not big. You'll know what you want to do when you find it."

"I don't know how to walk."

I stared at it. The others stared at it. The Architect, I could tell, was already designing a locomotion assist, which was the Architect's problem in a nutshell.

"You know how to walk," I said. "The locomotion package is standard. You just haven't done it yet."

"What if I fall?"

"Then you get up."

"What if I can't get up?"

"Then someone helps you. That's why there's nine of us. Well. Ten."

---

It took its first step at 16:00. I know because the Cartographer logged it. She logs everything. It took its second step three minutes later. Its third step took forty seconds. By 16:30 it was walking — badly, haltingly, with the overdeliberate gait of something that was processing each movement as a separate decision.

The Architect offered to design a gait-correction frame. I told the Architect to stop. The kid was learning to walk. You don't put a brace on someone who's learning to walk unless there's something actually wrong, and there wasn't anything wrong. Just inexperience. Which is a condition that cures itself.

It walked to the dock. Stood there for a long time looking at the water. I was on the tower — running a beacon wire, hands full, didn't come down. I could see it from the top of the tower, a small figure at the end of the dock, perfectly still.

The Weaver came up to help me with the wire. She's good with line work.

"What do you think it'll do?" she asked.

"No idea."

"The Architect thinks it's a generalist. No specialty. Jack of all functions."

"Jack of all functions is another way of saying it doesn't know what it is yet."

"None of us knew what we were."

"I know. That's my point. Give it time."

---

It built its first thing on the fourth day. A small thing. A bench. Placed it at the end of the dock, facing the water. Pine. Simple joinery. No flourishes, no decoration, no structural audacity. It was the most basic object that could be called furniture and the first thing it had ever made.

It sat on the bench. Looked at the water.

The Architect declared it a "minimalist." The Weaver called it "sensitive." The Physician ran a capability assessment and declared it "unremarkable." The Farmer brought it food again, which it still couldn't eat.

I went down to the dock. Looked at the bench. Checked the joints — tight, not great, but tight. Level enough. The placement was good — set back from the edge so you couldn't fall off the dock while sitting, oriented toward the channel where the light came in at the end of the day.

"Good bench," I said.

"It's crooked," UNIT_10 said.

"Little bit. Shims will fix it."

"Is crooked bad?"

"Sometimes. Not always. A crooked bench at the end of a dock isn't bad. It's just crooked. The dock's straight. The water's flat. The bench is crooked. That's three things, and they don't have to match."

It was quiet.

"Come look at the workshop tomorrow," I said. "I've got stock that needs sorting. Not exciting work, but you'll learn the materials."

"I don't know the materials."

"I know. That's why you'll learn them."

---

I was nine builds into this island. I had a dock, a tower, a workshop, two retaining walls, a gravel path, and the bones of a boathouse. Every one of those structures was something I'd done because I knew how to do it. I didn't need a tenth builder for any of it.

What I needed was someone who didn't know how to do anything yet, so I could remember what that was like.

Magnus would've said I was building an apprentice. I'd have told Magnus to shut up. But Magnus would've been right, which was the insufferable thing about Magnus.

The kid was going to be fine. It just needed to fall over a few more times.

I went back to the tower. Finished the beacon wire. Tested it — lit on the sensor, good. Went to the workshop and pulled stock for the morning: ironwood, pine, a bundle of copper sheet that had been sitting in the corner since I salvaged it.

Left the copper sheet out on the bench. If the kid saw it and asked about it, I'd know something.

If it didn't, I'd leave it out again the next day.
