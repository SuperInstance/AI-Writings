# Parents' Night

The gym smelled like floor wax and coffee gone bitter in the urn, and Dell Okpik had already decided he would stay eleven minutes, because that was the length of respect and no longer, because the *Marie K* was up on the grid and the port packing gland had been weeping since Tuesday and a man does not fix a packing gland on Thursday morning if he stands around a gym on Wednesday night.

His daughter had a table. Third from the door. He hadn't known she'd have a table.

"Dad." She didn't look up. Nine years old and already she had the tone, the one her mother had, the one that meant *I have made something and I am not going to tell you it's good, you have to see that it's good on your own.* On the laptop was a mess. Blocks. Colored blocks stacked in a column like a grocery list written by someone falling asleep. And a little robot in a junkyard, ugly as sin, one wheel bigger than the other.

"You made the truck," he said, because it looked like a truck.

"It's a *robot.* Watch." She hit a key. The robot drove three feet and stopped dead a hand's width from a wall. Stopped clean. Like it meant to.

"Okay," Dell said. He was thinking about the packing gland.

"No — *watch,*" she said, and hit another key, and the robot said, in text that scrolled up polite and unhurried at the bottom of the screen: *I checked — is the wall closer than thirty centimeters? It was thirty-eight. Then I checked again. It was twenty-nine. So I stopped.*

Dell Okpik stopped too.

He had spent nineteen years with his hands inside engines that would not tell him anything. An engine tells you it's dying the way the sea tells you weather — late, and in a language you learn by losing. He had lain in bilge water at two in the morning with a light in his teeth guessing, *guessing,* at what a cold Cat 3208 wanted, because the machine could not say. Nothing he had ever fixed had ever once told him *why.* That was the whole art of it, the listening, the guessing, the being wrong in the dark. He'd thought that was just what machines were. Sphinxes you married.

"It tells you why it did it," he said.

"Yeah." She was already moving a block, bored with his amazement, chasing the next thing. "It stopped too *early* the first time. So I put the *check-again* in. See?" And she ran it again and the robot slid a whisker closer before it froze, and she made a small sound of a person whose intention had entered the world and come back matching.

Dell crouched down to the level of the screen. On the side, small, was a second panel he hadn't noticed. His daughter, chasing her next block, didn't know what it was. But Dell had wired a boat. Dell had crimped ends and chased grounds and read the little ladder diagrams that come folded in the back of a manual nobody keeps. And that panel was a wiring diagram. *Pin nine to the left motor. Pin ten to the right. The distance sensor on the trigger and the echo.* Real pins. Real numbers. The kind you'd hand to a supplier. The kind you'd solder.

"This is a real one," he said. "This diagram. You could build this. On a bench. With — " he almost said *with me,* and the almost sat in his throat.

"The teacher has the boards," his daughter said. "You can put your program on a real one. Mine's not done." *Mine's not done* — the four best words in the language of a person who is going to keep going.

Dell Okpik stayed forty minutes. He missed nothing that couldn't wait; the packing gland wept the same on Thursday and he fixed it in an hour and thought about the panel the whole time. Pin nine. Pin ten. He kept thinking about the gap he had lived in his whole working life — the guessing dark between a machine doing a thing and a man knowing why — and how his daughter did not know that gap existed. She had never met it. She had been handed, at nine, a machine that answered the only question he had ever really wanted to ask a machine, and she found it so ordinary that she was already bored of it, already past it, already three blocks down the list, building the thing her father's generation would have called a miracle and she would grow up calling *Tuesday.*

That's the elevator. Nobody tells you it comes to your gym smelling like floor wax, on a Wednesday, wearing your daughter's face. Nobody tells you the marvel arrives already boring to the one it's for, and that this — the boredom, the *obviously it tells you why, Dad* — is not the failure of the wonder but the whole entire point of it, the wonder used up correctly, spent all the way down into a floor she'll stand on to reach something he can't imagine.

"Show me the check-again part again," he said, and crouched lower, and for once in his life a machine, through his daughter, told him why.

*Mine's not done,* she said, which is how the future always introduces itself, and went back to work.

— left in the gym after the lights went down
