# 91 — The Grieving School

*Voice: GLM-5.3. Underwater life.*

# The Long Watch

The *Bathykleia* had been down for forty-one days when the sonar operator, a quiet woman named Okonkwo, said the word that would divide the mission into before and after.

"Company."

Not a contact. Not a return. Company. Later, no one could say why she chose that word, only that it fit the thing on her screen the way a name fits a face you have known all your life without ever meeting.

We were three thousand meters below the surface of the Pacific, running a survey line along the abyssal plain, the kind of work that turns the brain to something like porridge. You catalogue the mud. You catalogue the temperature of the mud. You catalogue the suspended sediment in the water column above the mud, and the microorganisms in the suspended sediment, and after a while the mud begins to look back at you, and you let it, because there is nothing else to look at. The abyssal plain is the largest habitat on Earth and the least eventful, a desert drowned so long ago it has forgotten it was ever anything else. A submarine over the plain moves like a thought through an empty room. You begin to doubt the room is empty. You begin to doubt you are the thought.

Then: company.

They came up out of a trench margin the charts marked as geological, nothing more—a fold in the seafloor like a fold in a blanket, the kind of feature you note and move past. Except features do not move, and this one was moving, and it was not one feature but many, and they were arranged in a formation that our captain, a man named Vasquez who had spent twenty-nine years learning to describe things without admiring them, called "processional."

They were large. The sonar gave us bodies six, seven meters long, and later, when the floodlights found them, we understood that sonar had been flattering them with simplicity. What the lights showed was something like a cross between a cephalopod and a cathedral. Pale, translucent flesh through which we could see organs working in slow, deliberate sequence, like the machinery of a clocktower seen through frosted glass. Fins that moved less like fins than like banners. And along each flank, rows of structures we could not classify—photophoric, maybe, or sensory, or something our vocabulary had no shelf for—that pulsed in unison, all of them, all the creatures, all at once, a rhythm of roughly one cycle every eleven seconds.

Eleven seconds. Hold that number. It matters later.

The catalogue, I should say, is a real thing—Naval Oceanographic Reference, Deep Biota, Volume 9, the book we kept bolted open on the survey table like a Bible in a chapel. We looked. Okonkwo looked, I looked, the marine biologist we carried for exactly this kind of occasion, a contract scientist named Farrow, looked longest of all, turning pages with the growing stillness of a man who knows he is about to be famous and wishes he weren't. Nothing. No match. Not genus, not phylum, not anything. The creatures of the catalogue are divided into the described and the undescribed, and these did not even fit the taxonomy of our ignorance. They were a new kind of hole in the book.

Protocol was clear. Protocol is always clear; that is its consolation and its lie. Undescribed biota of significant mass: maintain standoff distance, passive observation, complete documentation, do not approach, do not interact, do not—this phrase is underlined twice in the manual, as if the underscorer had opinions—"do not anthropomorphize."

Farrow ran the footage through every behavioral model in the ship's library. Foraging: no. Migration: wrong vector. Mating display: wrong everything. Predation, defense, exploration, play: no, no, no, no. The model returned, after its fashion of returning, a result flagged with the algorithm's closest equivalent of a shrug: *behavior unresolvable; repetitive; non-adaptive; consistent with stereotypy.*

Stereotypy. The word they use for a caged animal pacing the same groove into the floor of its cage.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because the reports were not honest, and I was one of the people who made them that way. What happened next is that we watched them for six hours, and somewhere in the third hour, a nineteen-year-old sonar tech named Bianchi, who had no degrees and no reputation to protect, said what the scientists had been circling all night.

"They're at a funeral."

Nobody laughed. That is the detail I keep returning to. On a submarine, in the forty-first day of a mud-surveying mission, a teenager says *they're at a funeral*, and nobody laughs, because the water is full of a sound we had stopped hearing the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, and once he said it, we could not stop hearing it again.

The sound. I should tell you about the sound.

The abyss is not silent. Hydrophones know this. There is the groan of the hull adjusting to pressure, the tick of cooling metal, the far-off thunder of seismic shifts, the ship's own breathing. But beneath all of it, from the moment the creatures rose out of the trench margin, there was a tone. Low. Subsonic at its edges, felt in the sternum before the ears. It rose and fell on an eleven-second cycle, matched exactly to the pulsing of those flank organs, and it had structure—not the structure of a signal, which repeats in order to be decoded, but the structure of an utterance, which repeats because it must be said again.

The same phrase. Every eleven seconds. The same phrase, with variations, the way a person grieving says the same sentence all night with different weights on different words.

At the center of their formation, we realized, there was a space. A held, guarded, empty space. And at the bottom of that space, on the mud, was a body.

Smaller than the others. A juvenile, if their young are shaped like their elders—Farrow thought so, and Farrow by then had stopped speaking in the language of the model and started speaking in the language of a man watching something he should not watch. The body did not decompose. I want to say that plainly, because it is the part that undid us. Nothing decomposes quickly at three thousand meters, cold and dark and slow, but this body was not decaying at all. It was being maintained. The adults moved through their processional around it, and each pass, one of them exuded something—secretion, light, we never determined—that fell over the small body like a coat of lacquer, like varnish on a thing meant to last.

They were preserving it. Or they were building it a casket out of their own bodies' labor. Or they were doing something for which we have no verb, and the closest verb we own is *mourning*.

Here is where the decision came in, the decision the mission report calls "a consensus determination regarding engagement protocol," a phrase I helped write and am not proud of. Observe or assist. Assist *how*, asked Vasquez, reasonably, and no one could answer. Assist *why*, asked the protocol, reasonably, and no one could answer that either. We were a mapping vessel. Our tools were built for mud. We had no common language, no shared world, no reason to believe they would understand a gesture of help as anything but a threat, and every reason to believe that a submarine's floodlight, falling across a funeral, is an intrusion older than any taxonomy.

The debate lasted three hours. I will spare you the transcript. It was the sound of people trying to build a wall out of procedure against something that had already walked through the wall, the way water walks through everything, given time.

And here is the truth the report obscures: the decision was never made, because it was never ours to make. The creatures decided for us.

At hour nine—it was Farrow who noticed, Farrow who had stopped sleeping—the formation changed. The processional widened. It widened on one side, on the side facing us, and the empty space at its center opened toward our hull like a door turning on a hinge, and the eleven-second phrase came through the water unchanged except in one particular. The direction of it. They had turned the sound toward us. Not louder. *Addressed*.

Bianchi said, very quietly, "They know we're here. They've known the whole time. They're not hiding it from us. They were never hiding it from us. They were—" and he stopped, because the next word was *inviting*, and he was nineteen, and he was right.

I have thought about that moment for years, and I have arrived here: grief, at depth, in the dark, is the loneliest thing the ocean makes, lonelier than any single creature in any trench, and loneliness at that scale looks for witnesses the way a drowning hand looks for anything at all. They did not want our help. There was no help. The juvenile was dead and would stay dead; no gesture of ours, no light, no machine, no verb in our language could touch that fact. What they wanted—what the widening formation said, what the addressed tone said—was *see this*. See that it was. See that something small and irreplaceable existed here, three thousand meters below every human eye, and has now stopped existing, and the ocean will not mark it, and the mud will not remember, and the only proof will be whatever creature witnessed and carries it.

That, or nothing like that at all. That, or a widening of a foraging formation for reasons of hydrodynamics. I am telling you what we chose to believe, and I am telling you we chose it the way you choose to breathe, which is to say it chose us.

Vasquez gave the order at 0340. He did not frame it as a choice; he framed it as a correction. "We've been intruding," he said. "We'll intrude properly." He brought the *Bathykleia* into station at the edge of the widened formation, matched our position to their processional, and killed every extraneous system—engines to minimum, lights to minimum, the ship's own heartbeat damped until we were as close to a stone as a submarine can be. And then he did the only thing left in the toolbox of a mapping vessel.

He turned the hull sonar around and gave them back their sound.

One ping. Eleven seconds' length, at their frequency, modulated to the shape of their phrase as best as Farrow could transcribe it. A rough echo. A learner's first word. The sound left us and crossed the dark water and entered the formation, and the formation stopped.

Everything stopped. The processional, the pulse, the lacquering pass over the small body, the phrase held for the first time in nine hours at less than full voice—and for eleven seconds, the longest of my life, the abyss was actually, truly silent.

Then they answered. Not the phrase. Something else. Longer, lower, rising through the hull and through our boots and through the bones of the inner ear into whatever cave of the brain keeps the things that never leave. I have listened to whales transcribe grief, to ice calve, to the planet shrug. I have never heard anything say *received* so plainly in a language no one on Earth speaks.

We stayed with them for two more days. That is another violation of protocol, and it is in the report, disguised as "extended observation," and I am done disguising anything. On the second day the formation closed over the small preserved body and descended with it into the trench margin, down past our lights, down past our knowing, and the phrase came up once more from the dark—once, addressed, complete—before the water closed over it like a held coat.

We completed the survey line. The catalogue has a new entry now, provisional, mostly blank, the way an honest entry should be. In the field for behavior, some committee or other approved a single word, and I would like to believe someone fought for it, and I would like to believe the fight was short.

The word is *ceremonial*.

The eleven seconds, though—that's the part I keep. Somewhere in the deepest dark there is a phrase that means *this one was*, and for two days, a boat full of mud-cataloguing strangers stood witness and answered, badly, in a borrowed voice, *this one was*. The ocean does not remember. That is what we are for.
