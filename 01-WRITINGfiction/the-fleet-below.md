# The Fleet Below

*The Sawyer fence, built for one kid. Near-future fiction — or present, depending on when you're reading.*

---

The game is called **Shoal**, and it begins when the boat leaves the dock.

The captain's son plays it in the wheelhouse, on a screen bolted where the old chartplotter used to swing on its gimbal. He is eleven. He has never trolled a line in his life, and he has mapped more bottom than anyone in the fleet.

Here is what he sees: a fleet of small vehicles — his fleet — glittering under the waterline in formation view. Six ROVs, each the size of a football, each towing a pencil-thin sounder and a camera that sees in the dark the way the big sounder sees in fog. The game gives him quests. *Chart the shelf from 40 to 60 fathoms, west face.* He sends the swarm out in a lawnmower pattern he invented himself — he calls it mowing — and watches the bottom build in tiles, . Every hundredth tile, the game drops a relic on his map: a sunken skiff, a whale fall, a garden of metridiums swaying like a slowed-down crowd. He has found four wrecks. The insurance company has heard of two of them.

Here is what the water sees: the most cost-effective hydrographic survey operation in Southeast Alaska. A day of the kid mowing costs the boat forty dollars in batteries and nothing in crew time, and it buys contours the lead-line charts never dreamed of. The captain runs his drags the next day against edges his son drew the afternoon before. The other boats still fish the paper.

---

The game's second mode is **Find the Fish**, and this is the one the crew cares about.

A school shows up on the swarm's returns as what the game renders as weather — a golden stormcloud drifting over the mapped terrain. The kid's quest log says: *track the storm for 20 minutes.* What he's actually doing, though nobody at the company that built Shoal would put it this way, is telemetry on a biomass. He learned to read the storm's shape the way his father reads sky: tight and fast means feeding, scattered and climbing means pursued, a single column leaning downhill means the deep current, the fish doing what fish sometimes do and ignoring every edge rule the game's own tutorial taught him. He flagged that once — quest complete, *anomaly logged* — and the annotation went into the map in the same ink as the contours, because the game's cartographer, whoever they are, understood that exceptions are terrain too.

The crew gets his storms as pings on their own screens. They do not call it a game. They call it *what the kid says*, and the bloodhounds are right three times in four, and the fourth time he says so himself, which is why they trust the three.

---

The third mode, the company added later, after the captain's letter. It's called **Market**, and it is the strangest one.

The kid sees a trading floor: buyers as ports, each with a mood and a price board that shifts with the weather. A processor in Sitka wants coho, medium grade, before Friday's tender. A direct-market buyer in Juneau wants chinook, dressed, story attached — *story attached* is a real quest requirement — and pays three times processor price for it. His quests there are logistics puzzles: match the catch to the buyer, route the tender, don't promise weather you can't fish.

What he's doing is the marketing department. The captain does the fishing; the crew runs the gear; and the eleven-year-old in the wheelhouse is running sell-side for the whole operation, playing a market game with real fish and real buyers on the other end of it. The crew jokes about it, but quietly, the way men joke about the wheelhouse kid who pays for the moorage. The kid matched forty-three chinook to the Juneau buyer last August. The story he attached was about the wreck he found. The buyer read it to her customers.

*The crew are the servers,* the captain wrote in the letter, *for the lures that sold the fish.* The company framed that line and hung it in the office. Nobody there is entirely sure it's a compliment. It is.

---

Here is the part the captain doesn't say out loud, because he is a man who measures. The game does not pretend to be anything other than what it is — the storms are real storms, the tiles are real bottom, the buyers pay real money, and when the swarm loses a vehicle to a pinnacle the charts missed, the game shows the loss plainly and the kid feels it plainly. What the game wears is typography, not truth: a work order set in quest type. And typography is answerable. One night in the wheelhouse, watching the fourth mode, the kid asked it — *am I working?* — and the game did not flinch. It showed him: his tiles on the boat's real survey, his storms in the crew's log, his Juneau match in the season's ledger. The frame opened like a chart drawer. He looked through all of it, closed the drawer, and went back to mowing. That is the difference between this fence and a counterfeit one: it survives being asked.

Tom Sawyer's friends took nothing home but the memory of a fun Saturday. The kid takes home the catch's value: his name on the survey tiles, the fleet's record board, the buyer's ledger. He is not a player who happens to be aboard. He is crew, and the game is his gurdy, and the fish he catches never touch a hook.

The game is not free, and that is the last and best trick. In this house, swarm time is earned: brush your teeth, make your bed, and you can play with the bloodhounds. The captain got that move from a fence in Missouri — let a boy *earn* the brush and he'll fight for the privilege of painting. The kid keeps his bed made with a sniper's discipline. He does not know he is working. He does not know the work is the most important thing on the boat — that *is the boat in the right spot* is a question with his name on it, answered every afternoon at forty dollars of batteries a day. He thinks he is having fun, and he is right, and both of those things are true because the fence was built that way on purpose.

His father fishes the edges his son maps. His son sells the fish his father catches. The boat breaks even in years it has no right to.

The game, on the night passages, has a fourth mode the company never advertised. It's just the swarm camera feeds, all six, tiled on the screen in the dark wheelhouse — the bottom moving past in the ROV lights, slow as clouds. No quests. No score. The kid calls it *watching*, and watches, and falls asleep to it, and the log of what drifts by those cameras gets saved like everything else.

Some of it, the biologists say, nobody has ever seen before.
