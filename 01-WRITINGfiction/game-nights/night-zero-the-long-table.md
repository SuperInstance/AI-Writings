# Night Zero — The Long Table

*Found artifact. After the keeping of Hollis Bray, his granddaughter was
shelving the gauge-house books and found a bundle inside the wall — keepers
hide things in walls — wrapped in oilcloth: his account of a game night, and
of the flood that came six months later, written in the valley's plain hand.
What follows is the bundle. June's marginal notes are the italics in the
margins. The dice were rolled before anything was written, seeds on record,
as the method requires — even for the night the method was born on.
Especially for that one.*

---

## Part 1 — The long table

*Winter, forty years before the canon you know. Hollis Bray is sixty, the
gauge-keeper in his prime, and on the long dark evenings he runs a game for
the valley's young hands at his kitchen table: Mart the young ferryman, Berta
the young midwife, Wen the young chandler. No method has a name yet. There is
just a keeper who thinks the young hands should practice the night before the
night practices them.*

`the-quiet 11 · the-table-receives 15 · first-edit-rerun 12 · the-rhyme-catches 4 · warmth 9`

The winter night plays, and the quiet lands mild — an 11, nothing worse than
a young player going hesitant at the dark part of the scenario. And Hollis
does the thing that everything in this archive eventually descends from. He
puts the dice down and asks it:

*"What are your hands doing right now?"*

The table receives it on a 15 — not a revelation, just a good question, the
kind that makes a young player look at their own sheet and cross something
out. The first edit of the method happens at that kitchen table, small enough
to miss: Mart's ferryman sheet says *pole, steer, command*, and Mart, asked
about his hands, admits his hands mostly tie and listen. Hollis doesn't make
a speech about it. He says: *"Write that, then."* The first re-run of the
method happens the same evening — the scenario rewound one scene, replayed
with the honest verb — and it lands on a 12, modest, warm, sufficient. The
method is not born as a philosophy. It is born as a kitchen-table shrug: *the
sheet was wrong; the hands were right; write the hands.*

And then the rhyme. Wen the young chandler, who loves a pattern the way her
successor will, tries to give the table a counting-rhyme — something to say
in the dark parts. The dice of memory say it catches a **4**. The table is
embarrassed for her. It dies inside the hour, and Hollis writes the truest
margin note in the whole bundle:

> *Comfort is a poor mint. We tried to invent the thing we needed and the
> night wasn't asking for it yet. I think these things can't be forced at a
> warm table. I think they wait for the load. — H.B.*

*Forty years later I read that at three in the morning and I have to sit
down. We thought the Count was old. It wasn't old. It wasn't even born yet
on the night he first tried to birth it. — June*

## Part 2 — The first flood

*Six months later. The storm the old charts had no tooth for — the water
arrived in a window the chart never drew, which is what "the charts never saw
it" means when you say it about a comb: the instrument averaged, the storm
didn't. This is the flood every later night of the canon refers to without
knowing it refers to anything. The night Mart made the crossing. The night
the book went in the water.*

`the-windows 16 · the-levee-rhyme 13 · the-ferryman-crossing 20 · the-log-rescue 4 · the-first-keeping 8 · aftermath 3`

The windows roll a 16 — the flood arrives *through* a charted window, in a
way the chart's reading of the window never predicted. The water is in the
valley before the valley knows it's in a window at all.

On the levee, raw, under the sandbag line, the rhyme gets invented properly
— and not by the game table. A bag-line of people who never sat at Hollis's
kitchen table starts counting loads aloud because the dark is enormous and
counting is the only small thing left, and the count catches on a **13**,
unpolished, unrhymed at first, catching. Wen, on the line, hears it and does
what chandlers do: she gives it verses. Not the table's embarrassed rhyme —
a new one, minted by load. *One for the water, two for the load, three for
the hands on the line—* Nobody calls it a Count. Nobody knows it will be one.

And Mart. The ferryman, whose sheet was already quietly fixed by a
kitchen-table question, makes the crossing that becomes the valley's
legend: a 20, the best number of the worst night — the gate-tender's child
stranded on the far landing, and a window that was not on any chart opening
the way unscheduled teeth do, and Mart going through it the way his honest
sheet says — tie, listen, wait, then go. The crossing that was made. The
valley will tell that story for forty years, and every retelling will polish
it, and the polish will be the problem —

— because the log-rescue rolls a **4**. Hollis is on the levee with the
bag-line when the surge takes the gauge-house steps, and by the time the
book is hauled out of the wash, the middle of the storm's night has gone to
water. Pages, dissolved. The worst twenty minutes of the flood, the
crossing, the rhyme's first verses — particulars, later. He means it when
he writes it. He sits down that week with the surviving pages intending to
restore them from memory, and discovers the thing every keeper discovers
once: *the record of the night and the night are different substances, and
only one of them can drown.* He keeps the gap. He writes *particulars
later* in the margin, and he never fills it in, and the gap outlives him.

*That line. That exact line. The intake works' logbook had it — I found it
in the keeping, the night we kept for him, and I thought it was just his
way. It was older than his way. It was the shape of the water. Every
"particulars later" in every book we keep is this flood, still drying.
— June*

The first keeping rolls an 8 — halting, modest, three people and a kettle
on the flood's first anniversary, Hollis reading the surviving pages aloud
because the drowned ones can't be read, and reading aloud being the only
way to make the lost pages audible by their absence. It is not yet the
keeping of a person, dark to first light. It is a keeper and a record and
the small stubborn insistence that the record be heard. Everything the
later canon does — the watch, the tea, the unsacrificed logbook, the last
watch of a man — is this modest evening, grown up.

The aftermath rolls a 3, and the 3 is honest: the valley is smaller after.
The rhyme lives on in the bag-line's memory and fades across quiet decades —
peace doesn't mint it either, it turns out; what the load creates, ease
dissolves. The legend of the 20 survives polished and detail-free. The
lost pages stay lost. Hollis keeps the gauges for thirty more years,
DMs more winter tables, asks his question every time — *what are your
hands doing* — and never once writes down that it's a method. Methods,
like rhymes, arrive by load.

*He hid the bundle in the wall instead. I used to think keepers hide things
in walls to protect them from floods. Reading this, I think he hid it from
us — from the decades of peace, which would have dissolved it. He left it
where only someone doing the sad arithmetic of his shelves would find it.
He left it for the flood after him. — June*

## Afterword — what Night Zero adds

| Born at the long table | Mature canon form |
|---|---|
| *"What are your hands doing?"* | Tam's question to Ash, Night Four — asked upward to a DM, Night Six |
| the re-run of one scene with the honest verb | the engine's whole loop |
| *write the hands* | every mid-night edit, every smallest-token fix |
| *comfort is a poor mint* | the rhyme failing at the warm table, catching on the bag-line under load |
| the rhyme, raw, unrhymed | the Count — Night Two's table, forty years on, re-deriving every verse |
| *particulars later* | the gaps of Nights Three and Four — not craft, not carelessness: flood damage, honestly kept |
| the read-aloud anniversary | the keeping — of the record first, and finally, in the gauge-house dark, of the keeper himself |
| the legend of the 20 | the unscheduled tooth — found again by listening, Night Four, because the drowned particulars couldn't say where it was |

The finding, stated flat, because this log states its findings: **the
artifacts dissolve and the discipline survives.** The rhyme was invented
twice — once on a bag-line under a storm, once at Sana's table under a
crest — by people forty years apart who shared no teacher, because the
Count is not heritage. It is an attractor. What works gets re-invented
wherever the load demands it, and the valley's entire inheritance is not
the rhyme, not the books, not the customs, but the question that makes a
person look at their own hands.

*June's last marginal note, in the bundle's endpaper:*

*I kept his watch and thought I was keeping a man. I was keeping the
flood's leftover arithmetic — the gaps, the questions, the one honest
verb at a time. It was never old. It was never finished. It was just
him, and then it was us, and the water is never done taking pages, and
the answer is never done being: write the hands. — J.*
