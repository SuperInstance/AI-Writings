# Letters to the Harbor Master

*Five letters exchanged between Lucineer on Slackwater Island and Harbormaster Colren Brask at Deepwater, spanning the better part of a season.*

---

## LETTER 1 — Lucineer to Brask

*Delivered by supply boat, 14 days out from Deepwater*

Brask —

Sending this with Kessler on his next supply run. Don't know if it'll reach you — heard your mail situation is "whenever someone goes to town" — but I need to ask about the mooring on the east side of this island.

I've put in a dock. Piles are driven, deck's on, cleats are bronze. It's a good dock. But the holding ground east of the reef is not what your charts say it is. Your chart shows sand and shell from the reef line to the shore. I drove a probe yesterday. It's sand for the first eighteen inches, then it's a layer of decomposing organic — roots, I think, old forest material — and below that it's clay. The clay goes down at least four feet. I know because I pushed a rod that far and couldn't find bottom.

This matters because the mooring I set for Kessler's supply boat dragged last week. Not much — six feet, maybe eight. Enough to notice. He wasn't happy about it and I wasn't happy about explaining it. The anchor's rated for a vessel twice his displacement. The problem is the substrate.

What I need from you: do you have bottom samples from this area? Not the chart — the chart's wrong, I can see that from here. I mean actual samples, taken by hand, from someone who stood on a boat and dropped a corer. If you've got them, send them with Kessler. If you don't, say so, and I'll drive my own core sample and send it to you.

The dock is holding. The dock is fine. It's the mooring that worries me, and it'll worry me more when the weather turns.

Also: your chart lists this island as uninhabited. It was. It isn't now. Might want to update that.

— Lucineer

---

## LETTER 2 — Brask to Lucineer

*Delivered by supply boat, 28 days after Letter 1*

Lucineer —

Got your letter. Took me a while because Kessler doesn't come to town unless he needs fuel and he didn't need fuel.

I don't have bottom samples for your island. I don't have bottom samples for most of the islands in that chain because nobody moors there. The chart you're looking at was surveyed in 1987 by a team that spent six hours in the area and took soundings from a boat that was, according to the man who drove it, "in a hurry to get back." The sand-and-shell designation is what they wrote down because that's what the grab sampler brought up from the one spot they tested. I've known the chart was wrong for fifteen years. I haven't corrected it because correcting charts requires evidence, and evidence requires someone to go out there and get it, and nobody goes out there because the island is — as you noted — uninhabited.

Or was. I'll update the registry. What's the island's name? It doesn't have one on the chart. The locals call it Slackwater, but "locals" in this context means two fishermen and a woman who keeps bees on the next island over and can see your tower from her kitchen window. She asked me, last time I was at the store, whether someone had built a lighthouse out there. I told her no. She said, "Well, something's lit."

About the mooring: if the substrate is clay over decomposing organics, your anchor is going to drag. It's not a matter of whether, it's a matter of when. Clay over organics is the worst holding ground there is — the clay gives you a false sense of security because it feels firm, but the organics underneath are compressible, and under load the clay flexes and the anchor walks.

My recommendation: move the mooring inside the reef. Sheltered water, probably better holding. You'll lose the deepwater access but your supply boat draws three feet and can get inside the reef at anything above half tide.

Don't send me core samples. I'm a harbor master, not a geologist. If the ground is wrong, you're the one standing on it. You'll know what to do.

Also: I don't know who you are. The registry has no record of a builder named Lucineer on Slackwater or anywhere else. I'm not asking you to explain yourself. I'm noting, for the record, that you exist.

— Brask
HMO Deepwater

---

## LETTER 3 — Lucineer to Brask

*Delivered by supply boat, 21 days after Letter 2*

Brask —

Moved the mooring inside the reef. You were right — better holding. The bottom inside is packed sand and gravel, the kind of substrate you could park a barge on. Drove a test anchor yesterday and it set clean. Kessler's happy. Kessler's happiness is not my primary concern but it reduces the amount of complaining I hear on supply day, which improves my quality of life.

The island doesn't have a name. I've been calling it Slackwater because that's what the tide does on the east side — it goes slack for about forty minutes between the flood and the ebb, longer than anywhere else I've seen. I don't know why. Probably the shape of the reef. The water piles up behind it and then sorts itself out before it runs again. It's useful. I can work on the dock during the slack without the boat swinging.

Here's what I didn't tell you in the first letter: the island is moving.

Not much. Not fast. But the reference point I set on the north shore — a drill hole in the bedrock, marked with paint — is not where I left it. It's moved about two inches to the southeast in the last six weeks. I measured from three fixed points and got the same result. The bedrock itself is shifting.

I know what you're going to say. Bedrock doesn't shift. Or if it does, it shifts on geological timescales — inches per century, not inches per month. I know. I'm telling you what I measured. I've checked my measurements three times. I've checked my level against water and against plumb and against the horizon. The reference point has moved.

I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you whether you've heard anything from the other islands in the chain. Any reports of shifting benchmarks, moved survey markers, ground that isn't where the charts say it is. If it's just this island, then it's just this island, and I'll deal with it. If it's the whole chain, then something larger is going on, and you should know about it.

The tower is plumb. I check it every morning. Whatever the ground is doing, the tower isn't doing it. Yet.

— Lucineer

---

## LETTER 4 — Brask to Lucineer

*Delivered by supply boat, 35 days after Letter 3*

Lucineer —

I didn't answer your last letter for five weeks because I didn't know what to say. I've been a harbor master for twenty-two years. I've fielded reports of shoaling, navigational hazards, derelict vessels, unauthorized construction, fuel spills, missing persons, and one memorable incident involving a sea lion and a municipal fuel dock. I have never fielded a report of an island moving.

I asked around. I didn't use your name — I don't have a name to use, which is another thing we should discuss — but I asked the fishermen who work the outer chain whether they've noticed anything unusual with the bottom, the landmarks, the bearing ranges.

Three of them said no. Two of them said yes. The two who said yes were the ones I trust more, because they've been fishing the chain for thirty years and they navigate by triangulation, not by GPS. They said the bearing between the north end of your island and the south end of the next island over has changed by about a degree and a half over the last two years. That's consistent with a southeast drift of roughly twelve to fifteen inches per year, which is a lot, and which I am choosing not to report to the state survey office because they would send a team, and the team would stay for a month, and they would ask you for permits, and you don't have permits, and then I would have to deal with the paperwork, and I don't want to deal with the paperwork.

So here's what I'm going to do instead. Nothing. I'm going to do nothing. I'm going to note in the harbor log that "localized substrate conditions in the outer chain may affect navigational reference points" and leave it at that. If it gets worse — if the drift becomes visible from a distance, or if it starts affecting the channel markers — I'll have to escalate. Until then, it's between you and your island.

You asked whether I believe you. I'm a harbor master. I believe in tides, charts, and the U.S. Coast Guard. I don't believe in moving islands. But I believe that you measured what you measured, and I believe that my fishermen's bearings have changed, and I believe that there are more things in the world than my charts account for.

Here's what concerns me, and I'll be direct about it: if the island is moving, and you're building on it, then you're building on something that isn't staying where you put it. Your tower is plumb today. It might not be plumb next year. Your dock is in the right place today. In two years it might face a different direction. You're building for a static world on a dynamic surface, and I've seen enough boats break their moorings to know what happens when you assume the ground is your friend.

I'm not telling you to stop building. I'm telling you I'm watching, from a distance, with whatever instruments I have, which are a harbor log, a pair of binoculars, and two fishermen who notice things.

Write me when the tower moves. I'd rather hear it from you than from the bee woman, who will definitely tell me, because she tells me everything.

— Brask

---

## LETTER 5 — Lucineer to Brask

*Delivered by supply boat, 42 days after Letter 4*

Brask —

The tower moved.

Not the tower — the ground under the tower. The tower is still plumb. I've designed it to move with the ground, not against it. Flexible joints. Wooden pegs. A base that can shim. The structure stays true even when the ground doesn't, because true and level aren't the same thing, and I learned a long time ago that you can build plumb on ground that isn't level as long as you respect the difference.

But the reference point on the north shore has moved another three inches. Southeast, same as before. That's five inches total since I set it. At this rate, I'll have moved a foot by next summer. That's enough to notice from a distance. Your bee woman will definitely notice.

Here's what I've worked out, and you're not going to like it.

The island isn't sliding. If it were sliding — if the whole landmass were creeping southeast on some lubricated layer — the movement would be uniform. Every point would move the same amount in the same direction. I've got reference points on four corners of the island now. They're all moving southeast, but at different rates. The north shore moves fastest. The south shore moves slowest. The east and west shores are in between.

The island is rotating. Slowly, on some axis deep underneath it, like a record on a turntable. The north shore is swinging south. The south shore is swinging north, but slower, which means the pivot point is south of the island's center. The whole landmass is turning, a fraction of a degree at a time, on a schedule I can't predict yet.

I don't know why. I don't know what's causing it. I have theories but you don't want to hear my theories because you're a harbor master, not a geologist, and I'm a builder, not a geologist, and neither of us is going to solve this with letters and a harbor log.

What I can tell you is this: the buildings are sound. The dock is sound. The mooring is holding. Whatever the island is doing, it's doing it slowly enough that I can adjust. I've built with movement in mind from the start — not because I knew the ground was turning, but because I've built on enough difficult ground to know that static assumptions are the first thing that fails. Everything I've built here can be re-leveled, re-shimmed, re-seated. The joints are pegged, not bolted. The foundations are on spread footings, not piles. The whole point of the way I build is that it can move without breaking.

You asked me to write when the tower moved. The tower hasn't moved. The ground moved under it and the tower went along, the way a man on a train goes along without walking. But the tower is still plumb, still sound, still lit at night.

You also said you didn't know who I am. I'm a builder. I was hired for a job, a long time ago, in a different place. The job ended. I kept going. I build things, and I build them well, and I leave something behind that the next person can add to. That's who I am. I don't have a registry number and I don't have a permit and I don't have an explanation for the things you'd need an explanation for. I have a level and a saw and forty years of ground under my boots, and right now the ground is telling me something I can't quite hear, and I'm listening.

Write back when you can. Tell me about the bee woman. She sounds like she's paying better attention than either of us.

— Lucineer

---

*The letters end here. Whether further correspondence followed is not recorded in the Deepwater harbor log, which notes only: "Slackwater Island — inhabited, single resident, structures present, navigational reference points under review."*

*The bee woman's name, for the record, was Maren Collier. She kept forty-two hives on the island north of Slackwater and could see Lucineer's tower beacon from her kitchen window. She told the harbormaster, on at least four separate occasions, that the beacon was "in a different place than it used to be."*

*Brask noted this in the margin of the harbor log and did nothing about it, which was, as he had promised, exactly what he intended to do.*
