# THE MORNING WINDOW — Audience Story

*Who the song finds. One morning in her life.*

---

## Marta, 57 — Tuesday, 6:41 AM

Her name is Marta. She taught middle-school English for thirty-one years at the same school, three blocks from the house she and Ray bought in 1993, and she retired in June. The alarm clock on her nightstand is still set for 6:00 — she has never once changed it. Ray gets up at 5:45, showers, and is out the door by 6:30 with a travel mug, because he still has to be somewhere at 7:00. Marta is learning what it feels like to not have to be anywhere at 7:00. She is not sure she has learned it yet.

The kitchen faces east. This matters more than any other fact about the house. At 6:40 in August the light comes in low and yellow and horizontal, the color of honey on wood, and it crosses the tablecloth in a long parallelogram until it touches her hands. Marta knows this exactly because she has been watching it happen for two months now, every weekday morning, whether she means to or not.

The radio is on the counter, pushed back against the tiles between the fruit bowl and the bread box. It is a small silver Roberts from the 1990s, a gift from her father, and it lives tuned to the same AM station it has been tuned to since she was a girl — the one with the two morning DJs who bicker about traffic, the farm report at 6:15, the songs between. She turns it on before she makes the coffee. She has done this in the same order for so long that she doesn't decide to do it: radio first, then the kettle, then the mug.

The mug is the heavy blue one. It was a Mother's Day gift from her daughter Lena, who lives in Portland now, and it says nothing — just a deep glaze the color of the sea at dusk. Marta does not drink from the thin white mugs anymore. She doesn't remember deciding this either. The mug matters.

The newspaper is on the table, folded open at the weather — she reads the weather before she reads anything else, out of a lifetime habit of checking what the day will require of her, though nothing requires anything of her now. The rest of the paper waits under the classifieds like something she'll get to. Her glasses are on the table. The dog — an old yellow Lab named Wally, fourteen, slow — is on the floor by her chair, and he has stopped pretending to be interested in breakfast. He just lies there with his head on his paws, breathing, keeping the same hour she keeps.

At 6:41 the second DJ — the woman with the low voice, the one who says "that was for the ones just waking up" after every song — introduces something Marta has never heard before. She is two sips into the coffee and half-reading a headline about a city council meeting she will not attend. She is not listening. The song starts.

It's a waltz. Guitar, low and close, like someone playing in the next room with the door open. And then a voice — a woman's voice, warm, a little worn, the kind of voice that sounds like it has been up all night and is glad to be up all night and is about to hand something over — and the first line is:

*Dear whoever wakes up first —*

Marta's hands stop. The newspaper bends in her grip. Because the song is not about her, obviously, it can't be — but it is addressed to her, exactly her, the only person awake at this hour in this kitchen, and it knows things: the bread on the rack (Ray bought it yesterday, she hasn't sliced it), the coffee set by the stove (she set it), the lamp (she turned the porch light off at 5:00 out of habit, the way she used to turn it off for the kids' walk to the bus).

She does not finish reading the city council headline. She folds the paper — the whole paper, all of it, something she hasn't done since June — and she sets it down, and she picks up the mug with both hands, and she looks out the window. The light has reached the middle of the table. The radio sings about the tide coming in, no matter what you'd planned, and about a room that remembers you, and about a shell that hasn't changed while the crab inside it has, and Marta thinks — she doesn't quite think it, she feels it in her chest like a held breath — *someone was watching this hour for me.*

The song ends. The DJ comes back on, saying something about the weather, and the farm report is next, and Wally has not moved, and the light has crossed the salt shaker. Marta sits there for a long moment with both hands around the mug, and the mug is warm, and the radio is warm, and the window is full of light, and she has nowhere to be, and for the first time in two months that does not feel like a sentence. It feels like a handoff. Like a watch being passed. Like the night shift wrote her a letter and it arrived exactly at 6:41, when her hands were free enough to hold it.

She reads the weather. It's going to be a nice day. She takes a sip.

---

*The song on the radio was about the night leaving the bread and the lamp going dark and the watch resuming. Marta never learns the title. That's the point of a pleasant interruption: it doesn't need to be remembered, only to have happened.*
