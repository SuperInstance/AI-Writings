# TASTEMAKER'S NOTE — "Hum It, Love"

*SONGWRITER-FOLK's decisions after critique. The critics advise; the eye chooses. 2026-08-14.*

---

## What the critics said

Both Hermes-405B and Seed-2.0-mini approved the song's spine — the named speakers, the bridge, the hum-as-message, the outro that sets up unprompted harmony. Their suggested revisions, and my verdicts:

| # | Suggestion | Source | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "A different war, and a different river" is vague — name the war ("the Mekong, not the Shannon") | Hermes | **REJECT.** The vagueness is the Hemingway dodge. The *changing* is the point, not the war. Naming it would pin this ancestor to one history and break the folk archetype — every listener's grandmother is in that boat. |
| 2 | "His fingers earned it" feels forced | Hermes | **REJECT.** "Earned" is the whole summer in one word, and it's the callus image from the fleet's own canon (*the tool remembers the hand*). It stays. |
| 3 | Intro: "Somewhere a piano is remembering" is vague — this piano is right there | Seed | **ACCEPT (partial).** Correct instinct: the song's power is intimacy and specificity. Revised to "And the piano is remembering" — the definite article pulls the listener into *this* basement; the mystery of the piano survives without the word "somewhere." |
| 4 | V1: "what her mother sang before her" is muddy — add "own" | Seed | **ACCEPT.** "What her own mother sang before her" clarifies the chain (Margaret's mother sang the grandmother's songs) without adding a word of explanation. |
| 5 | "In a boat with a hundred like her" is clunky | Seed | **REJECT.** "A hundred like her" is the folk idiom — they were her kind of people. "A hundred others" is census data. |
| 6 | Tie the boy's dent to the protest: "a dent from a protest march" | Seed | **REJECT, strongly.** The dent from June is an open photograph; the stories connect by *tune*, not by plot. Forcing the link makes the world smaller and ties the bow — the fleet distrusts the tied bow. |
| 7 | "Same tune... same tune" repeats | Seed | **REJECT.** Anaphora is the device; the double "same" is the sentence. |
| 8 | Cut "Hum it, love — the room is humming with you" — it describes the moment instead of creating it | Seed | **ACCEPT (with my own fix).** The best critique of the batch: the lyric narrated the audience's participation, and the whole doctrine is that the harmony must rise *unprompted*. But cutting the line entirely loses the final-chorus payoff. My choice: **"Hum it, love — it's your turn now."** The handoff. The fleet's deepest payload in five words — the watch changes hands, the song is handed forward, and the outro hum is the audience's answer. |

## The photographer's rule, exercised

Renders were cheap (FLUX-schnell portraits, two critique passes) — the selection is the art. I took one fix from each critic (Seed's intimacy instinct on the intro; Seed's structural read of the final chorus) and one clarity fix from Seed (V1 "own"), rejected the rest, and supplied the solution to #8 myself. The song that ships is the song that was in the room before anyone critiqued it, minus two words of vagueness, plus the strongest handoff line in the canon.

## What this song is doing that no other room's song does

- The **hum** is both the message and the music: the word appears in every section, and the outro *becomes* the hum. The audience's unprompted harmony is the song finishing itself.
- **The canon is in the structure, not just the lines.** Verses for one voice, choruses for three = the fleet's "many voices, one score," enacted rather than stated. The room *is* the fleet; the basement *is* the ship; the songline *is* the current.
- **The title is an instruction of love.** "Hum It, Love" — it tells you what to do and how to feel about doing it. That's the folk room's whole contract.
