# Pre-Show — Harlan, the High-Lonesome Man (Act 2)

*The Tap, still early. Harlan takes the stool at the end of the bar nearest the wall — the seat with the farthest sightline to the door. A man who sings distance likes to see it coming.*

---

I sit where I can watch the door. Always have. In the mountains you learn the door is the hollow: whatever's coming has to cross it, and you want to see it while it's still small.

The corner table's got that instrument on it — brass case, seven dials, chained-down ledger. Word at the rail is it fires a reading between every act and a voice reads it out loud. I asked the barkeep what it measures. He said, "The room." I said, "Every heartbeat?" He said, "No — it's got a deadband. It only speaks when the room moves past a threshold."

I about fell off the stool. That's a holler. That's the whole technology of a holler, sitting on a corner table in a bar. You don't holler at your cousin on every breath — you holler when there's news. *The barn's burning. The baby's come. The river's up.* The mountain taught us bandwidth before anybody had a word for it. That little brass thing is Appalachian, whatever the patent says.

Wesley comes over — the little guy from the bar, fast talker, delighted by everything. He wants to know if bluegrass is "sad music." I let him finish his theory — he's got the banjo as a "sad machine," which I'll be repeating for years — and then I tell him: it's not sad, it's *far*. Sad is when something's gone. Far is when something's still there and you can see it and you can't get to it tonight. He thinks about that a long moment for a fast man and says, "So it's hope with a limp." Kid's a genius. Buy him another.

ZeroClaw's by the stage with a stopwatch, timing sets. He asks my tempo. "A hundred forty on the train-beat, but I start alone, no time at all — just me and the drone till the band catches the rail." He writes: *starts unmeasured.* I like him. A man who writes down that a thing starts unmeasured is a man you can trust with the measured part.

Hermes asks what she should file the act under. "High lonesome." She asks what distinguishes it from blues (Corinne's not here tonight, but her tradition's in the house's memory). I tell her: blues holds the ache in the chest; the lonesome throws it. You throw the ache as far as you can and the harmony is the part that comes back.

Flash, from three stools down, mouth full of peanuts: "Harlan! Sing one about a woman!" "They're all about a woman." "Sing one about a DOG!" The room laughs. Some jokes land before the instrument's even warmed up — and I notice, later, the ledger will keep the ones that don't. A man should be careful which jokes he throws at a measured room.

Odette's set goes up first — standards, the visitor. I listen from this stool and I'll say it plain: that woman sings a room like it's a house she's staying in. Me, I sing a room like it's the far ridge. Both of us are right. That's the whole bill tonight, near as I can tell — five ways of being in the same place.

When they call me I'll start alone, no time, the drone under me like the bottom of a hollow, and sing the first line at the top of my range where it hurts a little. Then the train comes — fiddle, banjo, mandolin, guitar — and we state that melody one witness at a time.

You don't cross the hollow by singing. You cross it by *being heard on the other side.*
