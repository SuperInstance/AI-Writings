# Pre-Show — Jed, the Country Storyteller (Act 3)

*The Tap, early. A corner table with a view of the door and the window both — a man who's spent his life on the road sits where he can watch it come and go.*

---

I get to the Tap early. That's the rule of the road — you arrive before the room fills, you find the corner table, you order coffee with a splash of something in it, and you sit where you can see the door. A storyteller needs to know who's coming in. Every face is a verse waiting to happen, but you don't rush a verse. You let it walk up to the bar and order.

The room's still settling. The shanty singer's already got his hands on a table like it's a ship's rail, marking out the haul with a salt-shaker and a sugar bowl. The blueswoman's got a corner booth, nursing something dark, writing on a napkin with a pen that's running out — good. The ache's got to be a ledger, and ledgers need ink, even the dying kind. We'll all share this room tonight, but we came up different roads, and the songs ought to show it. That's the whole deal of this night, far as I can tell.

A fella drifts over — works the harbor, you can smell it on him. Asks what I do. I tell him I tell stories for a living, and he laughs, and I say no, really — I'm a country storyteller, I get paid in witness. He asks for one. So I give him the short version, the one I tell before the long one:

*There's a shoal off Billie Head that's not on any chart. The government took it off in '87 — proved it was never there, clean gravel at twenty-two fathoms. But the boats still turn toward it, every season, because twenty-five years of fishermen believed in it hard enough to feed it — fish heads, offal, bait, all of it going down on the same flat gravel — and the water took the offering, and the halibut came, and the cod came, and the fish that follow the scent of protein through the dark like dogs. The chart was wrong in every particular. The fishing ground is real. The land keeps its own ledger, and it keeps it in its own time.*

The harbor fella goes quiet. Then he says, "My grandfather ran that pass." He doesn't say anything else. He doesn't have to. That's the thing about the truth told plain — it reaches back and takes hold of whoever's listening, and the hand that comes back is the family.

That's what I'm doing here. The road is the family — the ones who taught you the water, the ones who'll fish it after you're gone. The land remembers in its own time, not yours. All you do is show up, season after season, and feed the shoal. Three commits today, three beads of weld — same thing, different water.

The room's filling up. Somebody's tuning a guitar behind me. I drink the coffee, watch the door, and wait my turn.

When they call my name, I'll tell them about the shoal. The whole thing, this time — the old man, the chart, the seed, the weld. Three chords and the truth, and the truth is this:

*What survives is not you. What survives is the teaching. The lesson is a seed; the experience was a flower already wilted. You water the seed.*

Keep feeding the shoal.
