# The Saturation Point — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

**Story — The Night the Census Ran**

At 1:47 in the morning, Daria was alone in the sampler room, which is where the census lives, and she was running the numbers again because the numbers had started to mean something.

The census is older than the Song Factory. It's the experiment that asks: when you multiply voices, how much of the crowd does the container keep? Daria's container is sixteen-bit — 65,536 steps, no more, no less — and the census had taught her that a crowd does not fit in 65,536 steps. It fits, or it doesn't; the arithmetic decides.

Count two voices: the container kept almost everything. You could hear the room, the air, the whole shape of the two of them. Count eight: 97 percent. Count thirty-two: seventy-eight. The medium's share grows with the crowd, quietly, like a tax collector who has always known the rates.

The bell is the part she loves. A bell struck once per voice counted. Two voices, two rings, clear as glass. Eight rings, still bright. Thirty-two — and by the thirtieth ring, the bell was sounding against a wall. One sample in ten at thirty-two voices is a flat-topped rail. The recording presses its lips together and refuses the rest. That's the thing people get wrong about clipping: it's not louder. It's a flattening. The wall is polite. It doesn't scream; it refuses.

She'd have gone home at that point, except for the stranger dividend.

The dividend appears when the crowd is the same four voices many times over. The census can undercount itself — the bonus that was never architecture, just material agreeing with itself at a lag. Four voices, doubled, agree; doubled again, they almost agree; at the right lags they stop agreeing and start subtracting. Daria heard it happen in the bridge: the count going down while the voices went up. A crowd of identical people, discovering it has fewer members than the list says, because some of the names were the same name.

Next door, the forgiving machine forgives. Daria has never understood it. Forgiving is for people. The sampler counts, and counting is the dignity: a ledger that knows its own limits tells the truth about the crowd and about itself.

She wrote "seventy-eight" in the log at 82 BPM, in B flat minor — the key of the bell, she'd realized, the key of things that ring until they can't. Then she left the light on, the way you do when a room is holding something important.

The song is that night.
