# Nu-Jazz Reggae Fusion — The Story of Its Making

*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

The arranger calls it "the treaty session." We asked her to explain the crossfade arithmetic that made two time-feels share one room.

**How do you seat a jazz ensemble next to a reggae rhythm section without a fight?**

You find the denominator. Jazz wants to lean back — the tenor sax and trumpet live in a long, conversational count where the beat is a suggestion the soloists negotiate with. Reggae wants to lean forward into the one-drop: the drummer plays the backbeat with his body, the skank guitar chops the off-beats like a man cutting cane, and the whole thing locks onto the *and* of every beat. At 85 BPM those two pulls cross — the jazz musicians' long count and the reggae players' one-drop land on the same rails. It's arithmetic, not magic. The tempo is the common denominator; the swing is what they both write over it.

**The spec calls for warm analog recording with dub echoes. Was that a stylistic choice or a diplomatic one?**

Both. The dub echoes are the reggae side's handshake — the space between phrases getting wet, the mixes opening up like a sound system in a yard at night. But the warmth is the jazz side's handshake back: real room, tube preamps, the air of the studio staying in the take. The tape hiss is the treaty document. You can hear both rooms in one recording, and the fact that neither room had to tear itself down for the other is the whole point.

**Where does the Hammond organ sit in all this?**

In the middle, physically and musically. The organ is the bridge instrument — it has jazz credentials out the door, but its swells and its sustain are practically reggae already; a soulful organ can hold a chord through the skank and make it feel like a doorway instead of a gap. So the arrangement routes everything through it: sax and trumpet trade over it, the upright bass walks under it, and the organ sits there like the one person at the party who knows both sides of the family.

**F minor — a deliberate darkness?**

A warm one. F minor at 85 BPM with that rhythm section doesn't feel sad; it feels serious, like grownups dancing. Four minutes is exactly long enough for the sax and trumpet to tell the story, the organ to take its one turn, and the dub echoes to close it — the last note disappearing down a long wet hallway, still swinging.
