# Grammar A — Round — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

**Story — The Slow Voice**

The prompt that made her was a telegram: an a cappella vocal round about four voices singing the same line; one voice is slower; the round drifts; and only that voice finishes.

Mara was the fourth voice in. She had volunteered for the slow part — the study needed one voice noticeably slower, and she'd been trailing her whole life, so it was the only tempo she trusted. The others started the line one after another, each a beat behind the last, chasing the melody around the circle like it was something you could catch. Mara started her beat behind them, and stayed there.

At 72 BPM in F major, the round is gentle on purpose. Gentle is what a round sounds like before it knows what's going to happen. But Mara knew. That's the thing about being the slow one: you see the whole shape from the first bar. The others hear the line; she heard the geometry.

It took three passes for the drift to become audible. Pass one, she was a hair behind — the kind of lag you'd fix with a metronome, and the study had banned metronomes. Pass two, a little more, and the harmony began to stretch, the parts separating like cloth pulling at a seam. Pass three, and the stretch was a distance — you could hear the round becoming two songs that happened to be about the same thing.

One by one, the others finished. That was the part the telegram had promised and the part nobody in the study believed until they heard it: voices drop out one by one, and the round keeps going, thinner and thinner, until there's one voice left carrying the whole line. The soprano finished her pass and stopped, listening. The second voice finished and stopped. The third — the one who had been nearest to Mara's tempo, the one who almost kept her company — finished, and the silence where the harmony had been was enormous.

Mara sang the last phrase alone. Not sadly. Inevitably. The telegram had told her her ending before she sang her first note, and she had sung toward it the whole time — the way a person walks toward a door they were shown in a photograph. Being slow had never been the defect. In a round, being slow is the arrangement.

She held the final note — the single sustained tone, the one the spec calls for — and she held it longer than the bar asked, because the round had taught her that the ending belongs to the last voice, and the last voice gets to decide when the silence starts.

The others stood at the edge of the room, listening. Somewhere down the hall, her sister loop — grammar A's other child — was playing its 144th repetition, learning that it was a loop.

Mara already knew what it was. She was the part of the loop that didn't repeat.
