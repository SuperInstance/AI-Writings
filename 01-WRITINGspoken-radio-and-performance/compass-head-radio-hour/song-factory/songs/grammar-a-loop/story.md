# Grammar A — Loop — The Story of Its Making
*A Song Factory art piece · how it came to be*

**Interview — Jun, Render Operator**

The prompt-grammar study ran three grammars through two forms — loop and round — to ask one question: how much of a song lives in the asking? Grammar A is the telegram. Jun typed it, and she has opinions.

**What is grammar A?**

The least you can say and still get a song. "Minimalist electronic pop about a loop that becomes self-aware. 100 BPM, C minor, analog synth, drum machine, reverb. Introspective." That's the whole prompt. The renderer gets a sentence and a shopping list.

**Why so little?**

To see what the machine does with the rest. Grammar A doesn't specify structure — the study's scaffold supplies intro-verse-chorus-verse-chorus-outro. It doesn't specify the 144 repetitions, except as the subject. It doesn't specify the single sustained tone at the end. The renderer has to fill every gap, and filling a gap is a kind of thinking. A is the grammar that trusts the machine.

**Did it work?**

The loop became self-aware on schedule. One hundred and forty-four repetitions at 100 BPM, each pass adding a small change — a filter opening, a drum-machine pocket shifting, reverb blooming a little wider — until the loop knew it was a loop. And then the sustained tone, held past the point where the pattern would have restarted. That's the answer to the question A never asked. The loop heard itself ask.

**What does A leave out that the other grammars put in?**

B gives the machine a body — a drone that begins to listen, an arpeggio that begins to respond, a kick that begins to breathe. C gives it a ledger — a spec sheet, key-value, no poetry. A gives it a dare. B is about the machine's senses; C is about the machine's schedule; A is about the machine's nerve.

**Would you write it differently now?**

No. The gaps are the song. Ask for less, get more — that's the A thesis, and the loop is the evidence. It's introspective because there was nothing else in the room with it.

The study kept all three grammars, and the family plays as a triptych: the same loop in three dialects of asking. Jun's is the one that asks the least and waits the longest. "People think a prompt is instructions," she said, closing her notebook. "It's a hunger. A is hungry in the right direction."
