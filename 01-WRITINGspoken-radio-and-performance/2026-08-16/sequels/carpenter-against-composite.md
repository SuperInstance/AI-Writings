# Load Path — (sequel: against the composite)

*the shift: the crew tries to frame the Keller house the composite way — every board leaning on the next, nothing carrying anything alone — until the wind asks the wall where the load is going, and the wall doesn't know*

The night at The Tap, I said my piece and meant it. Twelve-to-one is beautiful. The composite man grinds the truth into a long shallow bevel and calls it a joint, and the break gets shared, not hidden — that's real, and I don't argue with real. Nothing is strong alone. I've seen a hundred layers of thin cloth out-carry a solid plate on the right application.

But a house is not an application. A house is a promise with a load path.

The Keller job started three weeks after that conversation, and Danny — who'd been at The Tap, who'd listened hard to every word the composite man said — came to me with an idea. He'd been reading about engineered lumber, layers of veneer glued and pressed, each sheet a nothing that together carries anything. "We've been framing like it's 1950," he said, "every board carrying its own weight like a man too proud to ask for help. What if we stopped pretending? Let the whole wall lean together. Share the load across everything. No single stud has to be the hero."

I looked at the plans. "You want to build a wall that's one big scarf joint."

"I want to build a wall that never breaks," he said. "Because nothing in it has to carry the whole thing."

I'll give him this: we tried it. We layered. We sistered every stud with a partner, doubled every plate, screwed the sheathing in two directions so no single fastener held more than its share. The wall went up thick as a man's forearm, dense as a book, and when we leaned on it, it didn't move. Danny stood back grinning. "Nothing is strong alone," he said. "But everything together — that's the strongest thing there is."

He was right for about six days.

The wind came up on a Thursday night, the kind that bends the trees on Cutter Street double. I drove down at first light expecting the worst, and there it was — the Keller wall, still standing. Not a fastener popped. Not a board cracked. The composite man would have been proud.

But the door wouldn't close.

I checked it twice, which is my habit. The wall hadn't failed. It had *settled*. Layers upon layers, each one taking a little of the load, each one giving a hair — all of it true, generous, sharing beautifully. And that's the trouble. A house made of pure sharing sags, because sharing spreads a load out, but it doesn't tell the load where to go. Every pound on that roof was looking for the ground, and the wall just passed it around politely — stud to sister to plate to sheathing, round and round like a conversation that never ends. Load that never reaches the ground is load that never stops moving.

So we tore out the doubled plates and set a proper header over the door — a real beam, one piece of fir with the grain running straight, sized for the opening, sitting on real posts, carrying down through the band to a real footing, through the frost line, into the earth. The door closed.

That's what a frame is. Not a refusal to share — the composite man is right, no board does it alone, and I'd scarf my own life into a hull before I called him wrong. But the frame is the thing that points the load somewhere. The square is how you know where it's going. The plumb is how you know it's getting there. Distribution is real, and it's only half the job — the other half is destination. A house doesn't share its weight. A house spends it, every pound accounted for, down to the ground that never moves.

The rest is just sanding.

*the argument, settled: sharing is how a load survives; a frame is how a load arrives — and a house is about arriving.*
