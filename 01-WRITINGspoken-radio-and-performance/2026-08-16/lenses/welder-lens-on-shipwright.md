# The Reader of Seams — (the lens: the welder seeing the shipwright)

*He took my bead and chalked a date beside it — and showed me the seam was never a secret, it was a sentence waiting for a reader.*

Nineteen years I've worked with my back to the room. Hood down, arc lit, the record of everything under paint where nobody can read it but me. I told myself that was keeping. Keeping the day it was made and the day it gave, welded into one piece. Stronger at the seam than anywhere else. I called it the whole trade.

Then the shipwright took my bead and cut it in half like a log.

Two trees that never met. Fir from a valley, winter-cut in 1909, slow rings. Spruce from an island a hundred miles north, cut in 1911, light as a promise. Forests that never shared a bird. Then a man forty years dead cut the same long angle into both, drove them together with treenails, and made one frame where two trees had been. And when she gave, one ordinary Tuesday, they opened her up and drove her back together — and he chalked the date beside the seam.

That date is the thing that undid me. My bead hides its history under paint. His scarph tells it in the grain. I said keeping was telling. It isn't. Keeping is keeping. Telling is chalking the date. I'd spent a career being the only reader of my own work, and he showed me a seam that reads itself to anyone who knows how to look.

Two trees, two forests, one frame. That's what I see when I look at him now: a man who thinks in readers. Every scarph he cuts is a letter to somebody not born yet. He doesn't just keep the break — he signs it. Leaves the date like a name on the work, so a stranger a hundred years off can run his hand along the grain and feel two mornings at once.

I used to think that made him careless. A record you can't hide is a record anybody can read. But the crack is visible under the paint if you know where to look — and I've spent nineteen years hoping nobody looked. He's spent forty hoping everybody would. Same seam. Same keeping. He just trusted the reader to exist, and I didn't.

So now when I strike the arc, I'm not only introducing the two halves of a thing's life to each other. I'm writing a sentence. And the shipwright taught me sentences are meant to be read. The bead doesn't outlive both sides in the metal — it outlives them in whoever knows where to look. He chalked the date. I'm learning to leave one too.

*I used to think he kept records; now I see he spends his life writing letters to strangers — and the date chalked beside my seam taught me my welds were never secrets, they were correspondence.*
