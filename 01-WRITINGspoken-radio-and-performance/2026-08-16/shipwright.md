# The Lofting Floor

*One night the sawdust stops falling, and a shipwright wakes in a yard two hundred years from home — where the marks he chalked are still the marks they use.*

They say the lofting floor remembers. I didn't believe it. A floor is just planks, and a man is just the shape of his work. But you put four hundred years of chalk lines down on the same black pine — every hull we ever drew, full size, laid out flat on the boards — and maybe the wood starts keeping accounts.

The night I'm telling you about, I'd been fairing a set of lines till my eyes crossed. The curve of a bow, you see, it can't be faked. You don't invent it. You find it — bend the batten till it runs fair, and the fair line was always there, waiting in the wood, and you only held the stick still long enough to let it out. That's the whole trade, if you want it short: we don't make the shape. We let it out.

I must have fallen asleep on the floor. When I opened my eyes the lanterns were gone and the light was wrong — cold, flat, no flame in it. And the yard was different. Bigger. Machines I'd never seen, steel frames, men in hard hats and gloves, and the smell of the place half new-plastic and half the same old tar. I was standing in a boatyard that hadn't been built yet, or had been built on top of mine, and nobody looked at me twice. To them I was just another old hand, which is its own kind of kindness.

What stopped my heart wasn't the machines. It was the floor. Same black pine — or pine so like it I'd swear — and on it, drawn fresh in chalk, was my own midship section. Not a copy. *Mine.* The little kink I'd faired out that very evening, the one I'd told nobody about, the run of a planking line I'd found at two in the morning. There it was, under their lasers and their computer screens, laid out full size like it had always been laid out, because there's only one way to draw a hull true and it doesn't care what century's doing the drawing.

One of the men came over, a young fellow, hard hat pushed back, and he saw me studying the floor. "You get it too, huh?" he said. "Every time. Doesn't matter what software we use. Sooner or later you come down here and chalk it out yourself. It's the only way it'll ever be honest."

I laughed. "Son," I said, "that line's older than your grandfather."

He looked at me like I'd said the sea was older than the fish.

The wood remembers. That's the long and short of it. You don't build a ship to last your lifetime — you build her so the lines survive you, so some stranger a hundred years off can run his hand along the scarph and find your name in it. The joinery outlives the builder. That was never the sad part. That was always the point.
