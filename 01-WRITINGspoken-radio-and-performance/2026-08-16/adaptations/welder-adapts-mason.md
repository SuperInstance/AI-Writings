# Let It Cool — (adaptation: the mason's patience, in the welder's shop)
*the mason's "The Set" — the cure that got cheated, the waiting that is the material — retold in the repair bay as a tale of two welds, one rushed and one waited on*

There's a rule they don't print in the code books: the weld is done when you lift the rod, but the weld is *right* when the metal says it's done. You can't hurry that part. The arc snaps off, the puddle freezes solid, and every beginner thinks the job's over. That's the lie. That's when the real work starts.

I learned it on a crane pedestal down at the docks — steel thick as my wrist, lifting forty tons like a bag of groceries. Somebody had welded a doubler plate onto it the winter before, and I got called in because the plate let go. A crack the full length of the seam — and not through the weld. Around it. The bead was still there, textbook. The crack had walked around the weld like water going around a stone, and every man in the yard was baffled.

I wasn't. I read the fracture face the way my old man read a horseshoe: that weld had been rushed — passes laid down too tight, no time between them, no preheat. Cold steel, damp morning, and the puddle froze the instant the rod lifted. The hydrogen in the flux, trapped in that hurry, sat in the metal like a held breath, waiting months for the right cold night to shove the crack out into the world. It didn't fail the day it was made. It failed the day it finished deciding it had been cheated.

A crack is just a cure that got cheated. Same as a wall, same as a mix — the mason and I could trade notebooks and lose nothing but the ink. His farrow cement is my filler rod. His water drawn before dawn is my preheat: you bring the steel up slow, at the room's own temper, so it meets the puddle as a friend instead of a shock. His waiting is the material, and mine is the cooling — the time you give a joint after the arc goes out is load-bearing. You weld hot, you let the blue light die, you breathe the burning flux, and then you leave the damn thing alone. Steel that cools too fast doesn't harden. It hardens *wrong*: the grain turns brittle, the stresses twist in, and the crack you spent the afternoon avoiding shows up next spring.

The second weld on that pedestal took me a day and a half. Two hours of preheat, one hour of welding, and then I wrapped it in an insulating blanket and stood in the dark listening to the metal tick as it cooled — doing nothing, getting paid for nothing. The foreman thought I was goldbricking. I told him what the mason would've: you can't arc-weld time.

That was eight years ago. The crack hasn't walked. It won't — because I didn't ask that steel to decide anything it wasn't ready to decide. You don't erase the break, and you don't rush the making. You give the puddle what it's owed: the heat first, and then the long, patient dark. And the seam outlives both sides.

*the mason waits on a thing that's alive; the welder waits on a thing that's hot — but in both shops, time is the filler you can't buy in a sack, and the joint knows whether you gave it the cooling it was owed*
