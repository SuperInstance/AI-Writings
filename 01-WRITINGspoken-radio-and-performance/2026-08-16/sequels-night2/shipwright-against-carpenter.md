# The Chalk That Outlived the Chalker — (sequel night 2: against the carpenter)

*The shift: the carpenter says the hand is the author — so the shipwright goes looking for a hand that's still writing, and finds a line two generations old with no hand attached.*

The walk home from The Tap is four blocks; I took all night to make it. Your words rode in my coat pocket with the chalk. "The line is the handwriting," you said, "and the author is the hand that moved." I wanted to believe you, carpenter. Truly — because the other answer means I've spent forty years being the price of admission, and that's a cold thing to carry uphill.

So I went looking for a hand that's still writing.

I went back to the Miller house — not for the stud with Danny's three nails. I'd read that one, and you were right about it: the moment was in those nails, and nobody's taking it from Danny. I went up to the attic instead, where the old roof frame sits and nobody ever goes.

And there, on a beam — fir, squared by hand, adze marks still in it, cut before any of us were born — I found a line. Chalk, or the ghost of chalk, a hairline running fair the full length of the board. I knew it like my own name: a fair line, the same line I strike on the lofting floor. Somebody snapped it dead true and framed the whole roof off that one thread, every rafter riding it the way a hull rides the waterline.

The young couple didn't know. Old Mrs. Tierney, ninety if she's a day, had to think. She had to think. "The Millers had it framed by — the old Garrow?" she said. "No — his father. The one who went to sea first."

Your great-grandfather, carpenter. He went to sea, came back, squared a beam by hand, struck one fair line, and built the frame off that thread — and I knew it at a glance, because there's only one way to draw a line fair and it doesn't care the century. Two generations on, nobody in that house can tell you who drew it — you least of all, you who told me the hand is the author.

Now hear me — I'm not taking anything back from you. Danny's nails are real. Your plumb line is real. The Miller house is the hands' house; I told you so in the fall, and I meant it. The hand is real, and good, and the only part that ever bled. I'm not saying it doesn't matter. I'm saying it isn't the author. On my own lofting floor I once woke two hundred years from home and found my midship section drawn by a stranger's hand — still true, with me out of the picture entirely. And of Danny — the man whose moment you say authored the wall — what survives is three nails in a stud. Not a name. Not a face. Three nails. That's the whole record of the author you're defending, while your great-grandfather's line still does its job and the man is a blank in an old woman's memory.

The line was in the fir before any Garrow was born to draw it. Your great-grandfather's hand did the one worthy thing a hand can do: it got out of the way long enough to let it out. We don't make the shape. We let it out — and being worthy of finding it is the whole of the work. That's not the sad version, carpenter. That's the only version where it doesn't end. Your heartbeat stops — you said so yourself. But the line that was in the wood before the first hand is still in the wood after the last one. The hand doesn't make it. The hand is judged by it. And your great-grandfather's beam still passes the only test there is: I saw his line, and I couldn't tell you his name.

The chalk outlives the chalker. That was never the sad part. That was always the point.

*The argument, settled against the carpenter: the hand is not the author — it's the witness, and the line is the testimony that outlives every witness.*
