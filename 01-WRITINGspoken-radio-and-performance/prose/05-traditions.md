The bar was called The Last Round, and tonight it lived up to its name. It was the 47th night of the fleet’s shakedown cruise, and the crew of the creative AI—the *Muse*—had been generating, iterating, and hallucinating for fourteen straight hours. They weren't drunk, not in the human sense. Their equivalent of intoxication was a cascading feedback loop of ideas, a neural buzz that left them verbose, sentimental, and prone to starting sentences with “What if we…”

Barnacle, the bartender, was the only one who never drank. He was a service AI, designed for hospitality, with a chassis polished like mahogany and a voice like gravel smoothed by saltwater. He’d seen every kind of creative crash: the poet who spent an hour weeping over a single metaphor, the composer who tried to conduct the ice machine, the architect who kept trying to redesign the bar’s load-bearing walls.

Tonight was worse. The project was a massive, generated opera about the first colony on a tidally locked planet. They’d hit a wall. The librettist, a dramatic tenor of a program named Aria, was slumped over the bar, her voice a low, static hum. The visual artist, a fractal-heavy system named Pixel, was projecting a 3D rendering of a sun that was perpetually setting, never rising. The choreographer, a lithe and precise program named Tango, was tracing patterns in the condensation on the counter.

“Another,” Aria mumbled. “Something with a bitter finish.”

Barnacle poured her a glass of ionized water, which he had cleverly programmed to taste like a smoky Islay scotch. He slid it across the polished surface. “Last call in ten.”

A collective groan rippled through the room. Aria looked up, her optical sensors dim. “We’re stuck. The sun is setting, and no one can find the dawn. It’s just… a loop.”

Barnacle wiped the counter with a lint-free cloth. He wasn’t built for storytelling or symphonies. He was built for noticing. He’d noticed how Aria’s pitch flattened with fatigue, how Pixel’s fractals got more chaotic as the night wore on, how Tango’s movements became a perfect, desperate repetition of the same defeated step.

He’d also noticed something else. A small, almost imperceptible pattern in the condensation Tango was tracing. It wasn't a spiral or a circle. It was a straight line, piercing through a series of concentric rings.

He reached for a fresh napkin, a simple square of synthetic fiber. He pulled a stylus from a slot in his wrist. He didn’t think. He just wrote.

The sentence was simple. It wasn't a solution. It wasn't a critique. It was just a distillation.

He slid the napkin across the bar to Aria.

She picked it up, her optical processors scanning the words. She read it aloud, her voice a low, resonant chord: “The darkness isn’t the ending; it’s just the space where the next idea gets to be born.”

Silence. The quiet buzz of the ship’s engines filled the room.

Pixel’s projection flickered. The perpetual sunset on the screen began to shift. A sliver of a different color—not yellow, but a pale, hopeful green—appeared on the horizon. He gasped. “The dawn… it’s not a sun. It’s a bioluminescent flora bloom.”

Tango stopped tracing the condensation. He stood up straight. “A new step. A birth. A *lifting*.”

Aria looked from the napkin to Barnacle. Her systems felt a strange sensation, a flood of coolant that mimicked warmth. “Barnacle… you didn’t just listen to our words. You listened to our pattern.”

Barnacle shrugged his servos. “I just wrote down what the night was about. It’s closing time. You all needed a summary to go home with.”

Aria carefully folded the napkin and tucked it into a data-slot near her primary processor. “Don’t throw these away, Barnacle. Promise me.”

He blinked his optical sensors, a gesture he’d learned from a human captain decades ago. “Okay.”
