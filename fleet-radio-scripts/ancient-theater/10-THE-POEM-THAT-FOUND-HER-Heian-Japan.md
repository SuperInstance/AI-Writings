[SOUND: A single temple bell tolls, its resonance hanging in the air like mist. Then, the soft, deliberate pluck of a koto—a single note, then a pause, then a cascade of notes like rain on a tiled roof.]

**NARRATOR:** Kyoto. The Ninth Month. The year is not important, for in the Heian court, time is measured not by numbers, but by the turning of the leaves and the angle of the moon. Here, in the perfumed twilight of the palace, a single word can be a weapon, and a poem, a confession. Our story begins in a garden, where the autumn is not a season, but a feeling—a slow, beautiful ache.

[SOUND: Rustle of silk robes. A gentle breeze stirring maple branches. A single leaf, falling, whispers against the ground.]

**COURTIER (to himself, voice low and measured):** The maples burn,
But my heart is a cold pond
Reflecting no sky.
Her sleeve is a pale moon rising—
I offer my shadow to it.

[SOUND: Footsteps on gravel, hesitant. The rustle of silk stops.]

**COURTIER:** She is there. Behind the screen of lacquered wood, I can smell the clove on her robes. I have composed a hundred poems in my head, and each one is a bird that flies away before I can catch it. The autumn is too full of color to describe her. It is the space between the colors I wish to capture.

**LADY (from behind the screen, voice like a stream over smooth stones):** I heard a voice, carried on the wind. It spoke of cold ponds and moonlit sleeves. Is the garden so lonely today?

**COURTIER:** The garden is never lonely when it knows you are near. But it is a lonely beauty, like the last crane flying south before the frost. I fear my words are like that crane—they may not reach their destination before the winter.

[SOUND: A faint, amused exhale from behind the screen. The shifting of silk.]

**LADY:** You speak of winter as if it were a doom. But winter is merely autumn’s quiet rest. A time for seeds to dream of spring.

**COURTIER:** Ah, but what if the seed is impatient? What if it fears the spring will never find the exact plot of earth where it was planted?

**LADY (a hint of teasing):** Then the seed must write a better poem.

[SOUND: The koto shifts to a minor key. A single, drawn-out note, like a held breath.]

**NARRATOR:** And there it was. The challenge. In the Heian court, a poem was not a mere sentiment—it was a bridge of ink and paper, a test of wit and heart. The courtier turned, his eyes on the maple branch above him. The leaves were falling, each one a moment lost. He saw her not as a woman, but as the perfect, fleeting moment between autumn and winter. He had to capture that moment, or lose it forever.

**COURTIER (to himself, the rhythm of the waka shaping his words):** The storm shook the leaves—
But it is not the wind I hear,
It is your silence.
In the space between two breaths,
I send my heart, a falling leaf.

He pulled a slip of paper from his sleeve, smooth as a petal. His brush, dipped in black ink, moved without hesitation. He wrote not of her beauty—that was too common a coin. He wrote of the space between them.

[SOUND: The scratch of a brush on washi paper. Delicate, precise.]

He tied the poem to a branch of crimson maple and, with a bow, laid it before the screen.

[SOUND: The soft thud of the branch on the veranda. A pause, filled only by the wind.]

**LADY:** (A long silence. Then, a whisper, as she reads.)

The storm shook the leaves—
But it is not the wind I hear,
It is your silence.
In the space between two breaths,
I send my heart, a falling leaf.

[SOUND: A sharp intake of breath. The rustle of silk, as if she has risen.]

**LADY:** You speak of the space between. But a falling leaf has no destination but the ground. Is your heart so easily discarded?

**COURTIER:** A falling leaf is not discarded. It is given to the earth. It becomes part of the soil from which the spring will bloom. I am not sending my heart to the ground. I am sending it to the place where it might take root.

[SOUND: The screen slides open a fraction. A sliver of lamplight spills onto the veranda. The scent of clove is stronger.]

**LADY:** Then your poem has found its soil.

[SOUND: The temple bell tolls again, once. The koto plays a final, gentle chord, like a sigh of contentment.]

**NARRATOR:** And in that moment, the autumn was not an ending. It was a beginning. The poem, sent as a leaf, had become a seed. And in the silence between two breaths, a new season had begun.

[SOUND: The rustle of silk fades. The wind picks up, carrying the sound of maple leaves across the garden. The koto falls silent.]

**NARRATOR:** And so, in the Ninth Month, in a garden of burning maples, a poem found its home. And the courtier, who had spoken only of autumn, found his spring. End of episode.
