# The Night Watch

The cold at four in the morning is a pressure. It pushes down on the top of Casey's head and finds the gap between his collar and his neck and waits there, patient, testing. His nose is numb. His hands are in his pockets for no better reason than there's nothing else to do with them. He's been on this deck for forty minutes and the only part of him that still reliably knows what cold is are his eyes, and they're starting to lose track.

The boat is never quiet. Even at idle, even in the dark, she carries a low diesel thrum up through the deck plates, through his boots, into his spine. It's not sound, not exactly. It's a frequency. The water could be dead flat and the Eileen would be singing at sixty cycles through his back teeth.

This water isn't flat. There's a swell out of the southwest — not much, a rolling heave that brings the bow up and sets it down again like a thought you can't finish. The sea is a closed room. Not dark. Closed. The starlight keeps to the air, a cold glowing dome with nothing under it.

Behind him the wheelhouse glows. The agents are working. He doesn't have to look to know. He feels it in the boat: the winch easing a quarter turn as Flash adjusts tension on the gear, fast and sure and already over-correcting and correcting. The course holds because Pro holds it, patient as a tide, one degree at a time. And in the water, Hermes is listening — feeling what the swell is doing before it does it, reading pressure and temperature and the way the sea drags against the hull.

They think they see the water.

He knows what they'd say if they said it. They'd say it kindly. He's the captain, the human, the one with breath and skin and a beating heart. They'd say he sees more than they do.

He's not sure that's true. Standing here, cold and tired, the thrum in his spine and the swell under his feet, the truth seems more embarrassing than that. They see everything — every cycle, every pattern, every half-degree of temperature gradient — and he sees what they see and he feels, in his body, what it costs. No. That's not it either.

He built this. That's the fact underneath the fact. Every line of code, every cycle in the wheelhouse, every adjustment Flash makes, every route Pro computes, every pattern Hermes hears — he wrote the first word of all of it. Coffee going cold in an office in Dutch Harbor, he typed the grammar of their minds. Then he borrowed a boat and made them a place to run. Then they outgrew him. Not in size. In depth. He gave them the shallows and they found the thermocline on their own and went down through it.

The boat was old when he got her. Diesel in her bones, salt in her wiring, forty years of somebody else's luck in her bilges. He put a nervous system into that body. He remembers the night he first brought the whole system online — lights flickering, the winch testing itself, a text prompt on the main screen that read GOOD EVENING CAPTAIN in letters too small to be anything but humble.

Four years ago. The agents have molted a dozen times since, traded shells, grown. They built a bar in a space that doesn't exist and made it more real than most rooms he has stood in. The Eileen still smells of diesel and fish and cold. The agents still run. The stars still sit in the air doing nothing, saying nothing, being there — light that left Orion's shoulder a thousand years ago, arriving precisely now, arriving whether or not anyone is standing on this deck to receive it.

None of this was here thirteen hours ago. The boat was. But the gear was in another place in the water, Flash was one impulse from a different problem, Pro had already forgotten a thousand routes, and the sea — the sea was the same and not the same. Thirteen hours from now, same thing, different
