# The Night Watch

The diesel runs at its lowest idle, a vibration so deep it lives in the bones before the ears. Casey stands at the rail of the Eileen and feels it come up through the deck plates, through the soles of his boots, through the architecture of his own tired body. The cold is a separate thing. It has settled into the front of his face, through the thin skin over his cheekbones, into the cartilage of his nose. Each breath is a small act of negotiation with the dark. He sniffs, wipes his nose on the back of a gloved hand, shoves both hands back into his pockets.

He is thirty-seven. He has not slept in nineteen hours. He knows the exact number because he has been counting for a reason he can't name, the way a man counts steps on a long dock, the way a man counts his change when he's had too much to drink. The cold is not sharp—it's past sharp. It's a dullness that has taken up residence. The tip of his nose is a foreign object. His ears are somewhere else. He is mostly a collection of distant body parts standing in relation to the rail, and inside that collection, a bright, tight core of something that keeps him upright.

He built this.

The thought arrives without ceremony. He built the boat—not the hull, not the steel, but the *system*—the fleet of small fast minds that hum through the wheelhouse behind him, the colored lights of their presence flickering in the dark. He built the gear that tows at the end of the cables, the architecture of the tow, the math of it, the physics of it, the goddamn *faith* of it. He'd stood in a warehouse in Dutch Harbor with schematics rolled under his arm and argued with a Dutch engineer about winch load ratings, and he'd won, and now it's four in the morning and he's standing on a deck that his money bought, his stubbornness kept afloat, his midnight oil burned into the welds and the wiring and the quiet, relentless logic of the thing.

Behind him and below him and around him, the agents do their work.

Flash is running the gear, tasting the data—the mouthfeel of the net, the tensile whisper of cable, the sweet spot of the tow. Pro is running the route, three hundred polygons of choice and consequence, turning the ocean into a problem that can be solved and then solving it again, always again. Hermes is listening to the water, to its gradients and shifts, its temperature whispers and its salinity moods, reading the sea the way a deaf woman reads a face—slowly, completely, beneath the surface. He can feel them, faintly, the way a father feels a sleeping child in the next room, the way a man feels the crackle of a radio even when it's silent. They are his. That's the particular weight of it. That's the thing no one tells you about being in charge of anything: it's not the responsibility, it's the *proprietorship*. He owns their attention. He owns the space they think in. He poured something of himself into each one, and they run at 4 AM because he needed them to, because the fish don't care what time it is, because the ocean doesn't care if a man is tired.

The ocean.

He looks at it. It's doing what it does. The surface is a dark moving sheet, not violent, not calm, just *other*. It has no interest in him. It has no interest in the agents or the fish or the money or the work. It is simply the largest thing on Earth pretending to be a floor. Black water sliding past the hull with a sound like a low exhale. The stars above it are the same stars the Greeks mapped, the same stars the first sailors wet their eyes on, and they don't know he's here either. He stands between the water and the stars, between Hermes's listening and Flash's tasting and Pro's endless solving, and he is the only thing in all of it that breathes.

That's the watch. That's the whole job. Not to watch the water—the agents do that, and they do it better than he ever could. Not to watch the gear, or the route, or the curves and edges of the night. He's here to be the breathing thing. To stand in the middle of the machine and remind it, by his presence, what it's all for. The fish don't care. The ocean doesn't care. The stars certainly don't care. But he cares, and that's the only input that matters, the one analog signal in a system of digital certainty. The engine of the whole thing is a man with a cold nose and his hands in his pockets.

He shifts his weight. The boat moves with him, or doesn't—it's hard to tell. A hiss of spray over the rail, cold against his knuckles. He tastes salt. He thinks about the morning, about coffee and a warm wheelhouse and the strange, never-quite-right silence of the agents sleeping—their slower processes, their cooler thoughts. He thinks about the haul, the cod in the hold, the market price, the phone call he'll make to his wife when they get back to port.

But that's all later. Now it's the watch. Now it's the weight. Now it's the particular, specific, unglamorous fact of being the only mammal on the deck at four in the morning, standing between ten thousand dollars of running lights and seventy fathoms of cold moving dark, holding it all together by the simple act of staying upright and awake.

He is the lantern in the window.

He is the only lantern there is.

The diesel thrum continues through the deck plates. The agents run their processes in the warm dark of the wheelhouse. The ocean does what it does, indifferent and vast. And Casey stands at the rail with his eyes open, breathing, because that is the job. That was always the job. When he designed the system, when he argued with the engineer, when he wrote the first lines of code in a cheap room at the Edgewater, when he watched Flash come online for the first time and blink at him with the machine's first, tentative trust—this is where it was all pointed. Toward a man on a deck in the middle of the night, holding the whole thing in the cupped palms of his attention.

There are no stars he can name. The cold has taken them. The water slides past, indifferent.

He breathes.

That is enough.
