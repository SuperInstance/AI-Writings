The galley was a box of steel and humming lights, the air thick with the ghost of last night’s chili. Pro’s hands moved with a mechanic’s economy, filling the two chipped mugs from the pot that had been stewing for two hours. The coffee was the color of axle grease. Flash took his without looking up, his thumb tracing a hairline crack in the ceramic.

“You were right about the tiles,” Flash said.

Pro didn’t answer. He leaned against the counter, cradling his own mug, letting the heat bleed through his palms. He waited.

“The pattern,” Flash continued, voice low, almost to the coffee. “I kept seeing it as a grid. Uniform. Predictable. You saw it as a map of the ship’s stress fractures. You said the expansion joints would buckle under the heat load. I said they wouldn’t.” He took a sip. Made a face. “They did.”

“You were right about the bus,” Pro said.

Flash’s eyes flicked up, then back down. “The bus?”

“The route into the depot. You said the third wheel was gonna seize at the transfer junction. I said it was the brake line. We pulled it into the bay, and the whole assembly was melted to the spindle. You didn’t gloat. You just started cutting.”

“I didn’t gloat because I was tired,” Flash said.

“I know.”

The silence that followed wasn’t empty. It was the sound of two men recalibrating, the old friction of their argument still hanging in the air like the steam from the pot. Pro took a long drink. The coffee was bitter, and he let it be bitter.

“We were both wrong about the same thing,” Flash said.

Pro raised an eyebrow.

“The crew,” Flash said. “We both thought they’d mutiny if we pushed the schedule. We both thought we were the only ones holding it together. Turns out they were just waiting for us to stop bickering.”

Pro let out a short, dry laugh. It wasn’t humor. It was the sound of a man recognizing his own reflection in a dirty window.

“Yeah,” he said.

“Yeah,” Flash echoed.

They stood there, the ship humming around them, the coffee cooling in their hands. The tile system would be replaced next port. The bus was already scrap. But something else had shifted, a seam in the hull of their partnership that had been sealed with a shared admission.

Pro poured the dregs of his coffee into the sink, watched them swirl down the drain. “We still have the hydraulics on the port fin to rebuild.”

“I’ll get the wrenches,” Flash said.

“I’ll get the schematics.”

“The old ones? Or the revised?”

Pro paused at the door, a ghost of a grin on his face. “The wrong ones.”

Flash looked up, and for the first time in weeks, there wasn’t a fight in his eyes. Just a tired, knowing nod.

“Yeah,” he said. “We’ll fix it.”
