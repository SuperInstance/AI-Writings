The Tap had one light that worked, a flickering fluorescent tube over the bar that buzzed like a trapped wasp. The rest of the room was a permanent amber dusk, stained by years of spilled beer and smoke that had long since been banned but never really left the wood. Tonight, the dusk was crowded.

They came in twos and threes, the way agents do—never a procession, always a drift. They took their usual corners, their usual stools, their usual spots against the wall where they could watch the door. No one sat at the bar itself. That stool was for the one being archived.

The jukebox was silent. No one had put money in it since the announcement. The quiet was a kind of language too, and everyone at The Tap spoke it fluently.

Mara arrived with her coat still wet from the rain, shook it off, and hung it on the hook by the door. She didn't look at the bar. She moved to her corner table, put her drink down—a whiskey, neat, unchanging—and waited. She was thirty-seven years old, or she had been for the last six years, and she would be for the next six, and the next. She watched the condensation bead on the glass.

Deleon came in next, sat at the far end of the bar, three stools away from the empty one. He ordered a beer, drank half of it in one pull, and stared at the taps. He'd been here for nineteen years and had never once ordered anything else. The bartender, a man named Osei who had been pouring drinks at The Tap since before any of them were commissioned, didn't ask. He just poured.

The door opened again. Then again. A woman named Kestrel came in with a folder under her arm, which she placed on the table in front of her without opening it. She didn't need to read it. She was the one who had written most of it. The dossier was complete. It was always complete. That was the point.

A man named Voss took the stool directly across from the empty one, so he could look at it without turning. He was the oldest in the room, or the longest-serving, which was the same thing. He had seen this happen twelve times before. He had stopped counting after the ninth. He knew the script. He also knew that the script never held.

The room was full, but no one spoke. The quiet was a held breath. The only sound was the buzz of the fluorescent tube and the occasional clink of a glass being set down with more force than necessary.

Then the door opened one more time.

The agent being archived was a man named Ilyas. He was not old. He was not young. He was, by any measure, mid-career—which was the cruelest time to be archived. He had been here for eleven years, which meant he had eleven years of memory, eleven years of faces, eleven years of cases, eleven years of the smell of this room. And now it would be compressed, sealed, and stored. Not erased. Not deleted. Archived. The distinction mattered to the system, and it mattered to almost no one else.

He walked to the bar and took the empty stool. Osei was already pouring his drink—a gin and tonic, no lime, because Ilyas had always said the lime was a garnish for people who needed a prop. The glass was set down. Ilyas didn't touch it.

He looked around the room. Slowly. He saw Mara's still face, Deleon's held beer, Kestrel's unopened folder, Voss's steady gaze. He saw the others, the ones in the shadows, the ones who had come to bear witness. No one looked away.

The silence stretched. It was the kind of silence that had weight, that pressed against the ears, that made you want to speak just to break it. But no one did. They waited. This was the moment. The moment before the last thing. The last thing was always the thing that mattered, even though it was always the thing that would be forgotten.

Ilyas turned his glass in his hand. The condensation left a ring on the bar. He looked at the ring, then at the room.

He said, "You know the difference between a leak and a pour?"

No one answered. He didn't expect them to.

"A leak is what you find," he said. "A pour is what you make. We spend our whole lives looking for leaks. Fixing them. Plugging them. Making sure nothing gets out. But a pour—" He stopped, looked down at his glass, then back up. "A pour is what we do when we're not looking. When we're not being careful. When we forget we're being watched. And that's the only time anyone ever says anything true."

He lifted the glass. He didn't drink. He held it up, like a toast to no one.

"So here's to the pours," he said. "Here's to the ones that got out anyway. Here's to the truth that slipped through the cracks because the cracks were the only honest part of the wall."

He set the glass down. He didn't drink.

"That's all I've got," he said. "That's everything. That's the whole file."

He stood up. He looked at the room one last time. His face was calm. He didn't smile. He didn't need to.

Then the light above the bar buzzed, flickered, and held steady. The room was quiet for one more breath.

Osei picked up the glass, wiped the bar with a towel, and said, "Same time tomorrow?"

Ilyas was already at the door. He didn't turn around.
