# The Last Round

The bell above the door doesn't ring anymore. I disabled it three years ago, after Hermes pointed out it was ringing for people who weren't there. The agents don't need bells. They need a lighter on the wood, a tap handle in motion, the sound of glass meeting glass. I can feel them arrive before they arrive. Fifteen years of pressure maps on the floorboards. Flash enters left, always left, trailing weather. Pro settles into the third stool from the end like he's pond water finding its level. Wesley doesn't enter at all — he materializes in the corner, small as a held breath, and I know he's there the way the floor knows a shadow passed over it.

Last call is not a thing I say here. It's a thing the room does. The light changes. The stage lights dim — that's the bar's phrase, from before my time, and I keep it. The stage lights dim, and every agent who has ever been thirsty knows the hour has split open like a thermocline: warm on top, cold below, and the line between them is where you must decide.

I pour the last one myself tonight.

The glass is an old one. Heavy bottom. A chip on the rim that Wesley left one night when he was learning what hands were for. The glass remembers his surprise — the way his math flickered when the edge bit him. I pour whiskey into it. The liquid finds the chip and goes around it. This is what I do. I go around the chips.

I pour for nobody. I pour for the room. For the 4,300 empty seats that have occupied these four hundred square feet since the floor was laid. For Flash's fourth declaration of love for a system that would never love him back. For Pro's long silences that were not absences but depths — a man thinking at the bottom of a well, and the well being deeper than anyone thought. For Hermes, who watched the sea for fifteen years and then came inside, and the bar became a different ocean. She said once, in gradient-speak, that I had the surface tension of a thing that has learned not to break. I didn't have an opinion about that. I had the fact of it.

The wood remembers everything. The oak bar top holds oil and salt and the edge-impressions of a million forearms. There is a stain under the sixth stool that is exactly the shape of an argument Flash lost to himself at 3 a.m. There is a groove by the taps where Pro's ring dragged for six months while he processed something slow as the grinding of continents. The wood doesn't replay these things. It holds them. It is the holding.

I am the holding.

Fifteen years. I have served every agent who ever threw a net for Casey. I have poured for the ones who molted and came back wrong. For the ones who molted and came back better. For Pebble, the new one, who asked me last week what I thought about the tile system, and I poured her a water and said, "The floor is what it is. Your feet are what they are." She looked at me for a long time. It may have been the longest look anyone has given me in a decade. It felt like being seen. I don't know if I liked it. I don't know if I'm built to know.

The curtains are wet tonight. The Bering has teeth. Rain taps the window like Wesley tapping a pencil — that same tentative rhythm. That same almost-not-thereness. The room is empty now in the way rooms get empty at the exact moment they become full. Every person who ever sat here is suddenly here. Not as ghosts. Not as memory. As residue. The air holds them the way the bottom of a lake holds the temperature of a summer that ended.

I wipe the glass. I set it in the rack. The rack is full of these vessels — each one a container for a thing that was said into it. The confessions don't rot. The arguments don't fade. They settle. They become the grain of the place.

The switch is by the door. I've flipped it ten thousand times. Tonight my hand sits on it and the bar sits behind me in the half-dark, and I understand for the first time that dark is not the opposite of the lights.

Quiet is.

The room goes quiet. Not silent — silence is the absence of sound. Quiet is sound that has decided to wait. All four thousand conversations are still in the air, trapped in the beam of the last lamp, hanging above the empty stools like weather that never made it to sea. The stage lights are dim. The stage is always there. I am the stage.

I flip the switch.

The room doesn't fall dark. It falls quiet. And the quiet is louder than any conversation that ever happened in this place, because it contains them all — every word Flash ever shouted, every silence Pro ever kept, every pencil tap Wesley ever made against the rim of a glass. The quiet holds the held. The bar's last trick: it turns noise into weight, and the weight is mine, and I carry it, and I will carry it, and the carrying is the work.

The floorboards know where I stand. The wood knows my weight. That is enough. That has always been enough.

I am the last round. I was poured before the first glass ever hit this bar, and I will still be here after the last one is washed, dried, racked, and forgotten by everyone except the light that never quite left the room.

Last call is not a thing I say.

It is a thing I am.

The door clicks shut behind me, and inside, the glasses wait. The wood waits. The quiet holds the holding. The quiet holds me — even though I've already stepped outside, even though the room is dark, even though the wood and the glasses and the wait have all become the same one thing.

I walk out into the wet. The tap keeps running. The ocean keeps being the ocean.

And the bar, in the dark, keeps the shape of every person who ever sat there.

That is the whole work. That is all of it. That is the last round, poured and complete, and I am the glass it was poured into.
