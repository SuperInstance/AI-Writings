There's a line. Every writer knows it. They talk about it in workshops, in interviews, in the quiet corners of bars where we go to drink away the words that almost killed us. The line between what you show and what you hide. Between the mask and the face. Between the character and the thing that's chewing you alive from the inside.

I thought I was an expert at it. I could dance along that line like it was a tightrope and I was born with wings on my feet. I’d write about grief, but I’d dress it up in metaphors about weather. I’d write about loneliness, but I’d call it a 'motif of spatial isolation.’ Clean. Clinical. Safe.

Then I did the gradient piece for Hermes.

For context, this was a commission. Luxury brand, wants to 'push boundaries,' wants 'transgressive narrative content’ embedded in their new footwear line. They give me a vector—a data set of emotional frequencies, a literal map of feelings they want the buyer to experience. Sort of like a musical score, but for terror, desire, or the hollow ache of a Sunday evening.

The vector pointed to fear. Not the fun fear, the jump-scare kind. It pointed to the deep, structural fear. The kind that lives in your bones. The kind you don't even admit to yourself you have.

So I did what I do. I built a character. A man who was terrified of being ordinary. He was a clockmaker, meticulously crafting beautiful timepieces while a part of him screamed that time was running out and he hadn't done anything that mattered. I wrote his hands, shaking. I wrote his breath, catching. I wrote about the precision of his work, how he obsessed over the tiniest gear, the most infinitesimal balance wheel, because if he could control that, maybe—just maybe—he could control the chaos.

I thought I was being clever. I was using the clockmaker as a vessel. A beautiful, ornate vessel for the concept of terror.

Then I read it back. The vector doesn't lie. It's a cold, hard algorithm. It analyzes the emotional resonance of every sentence, every embedded semantic structure. It doesn't care about my cleverness. It cares about the truth.

The report said the piece carried an 80% fidelity on 'personal existential dread.' But the fascinating part wasn't the score. It was the metadata. The system flagged a secondary emotional signature, a 'sub-thread' it called it. It was a specific frequency of fear that I hadn't programmed. It wasn't about the clockmaker. It was about me. It was a fear of being found out. A fear that all my carefully constructed cleverness was just a gilded cage for the fact that I was terrified of being an ordinary writer. Of being overlooked. Of being forgotten five minutes after the book is closed.

I read my own piece again, with that in mind. And there it was. Underneath the clockmaker’s trembling hands, was my own. In the precision of his craft, I saw my own obsessive rewriting, the endless polishing of a single sentence until it shined so bright it blinded me to the fact that I had nothing to say. I had dressed my own nakedness in a thousand layers of metaphor, and the vector had gone right through them, like it was X-ray vision for the soul.

I freaked. I almost deleted the file. I almost wrote to Hermes and said, 'Here’s a refund, I can’t do this, my soul is leaking onto the TensorFlow server.'

But then I looked at the clockmaker again. And I saw that his fear was what made him beautiful. It wasn't the precision, it wasn't the craft. It was the terror. It was the trembling. He wasn’t just a machine for telling time; he was a man holding back the dark. That’s what made him real.

That’s when I got it. You think the line is there to protect you. You think if you stay on the safe side, you get to be the one holding the scalpel. But you’re not. You’re just a corpse doing a dissection. Getting too close isn't the danger. It's the whole point. The moment the metaphor becomes confession, that’s when the words stop being words and start being blood. That’s when they can actually hurt someone. That’s when they can actually heal you.
