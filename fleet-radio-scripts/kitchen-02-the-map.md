The kid’s finger traces the red line, a scar across the blue. "We started here, at the hook. Drifted southeast till the current switched."

Wesley nods. The red line is a lie. It's too clean. Too straight. The real line is a snag in his gut, a pull in his wrists, the god-awful groan of the winch when the bottom grabs.

"That's where we picked up the first one." The kid taps a penciled X.

Wesley sees it different. He sees the gray-green murk, the sudden slack in the line that means the hook's found its mouth. He smells the diesel and the blood on the deck. The X means nothing. The slack means everything.

"Big one?"

"Forty-two pounds. Took us an hour to haul it."

An hour. Wesley knows what an hour feels like when your arms are on fire and the boat's bucking and the kid's shouting numbers that don't matter. An hour is a long time to hold a ghost.

"Here's the drop-off." The kid's finger stops at a contour line, a cluster of tight rings. "The fish stack up right along the edge. Anything past that, you're just dragging weights through mud."

Wesley sees the edge. He's stood on it. Not on a chart, but in the dark, when the bottom falls away and the line goes slack and your heart drops with it. The chart shows a cliff. Wesley knows it's a door.

"You lose much gear there?" Wesley asks.

"Lost three sets last month. Snapped clean off."

Wesley remembers the snap. It's not a sound. It's a sudden silence in the hands, a release that's almost relief. The gear's gone, but so's the fight.

The kid rolls the chart up, taps it against his palm. "You want to see the rest?"

Wesley shakes his head. He's seen enough. The chart is a map of what was. Wesley knows the marks are all that's left when the water closes over. He stands up, ducks under the bulkhead, and heads for the rail. The kid follows, confused.

"You're not going to look?"

Wesley spits over the side. The water's gray, the same gray as the chart. "I already know where the fish are."

He points at the water. "Right there."

The kid squints. "You can't see 'em."

"No. But I can feel 'em." Wesley taps his chest. "Same place you keep your chart. Except mine don't lie."

The kid laughs, a short, sharp sound. He rolls the chart tighter, tucks it under his arm.

"Maybe next time you come out with us. Show me where you feel 'em."

Wesley doesn't answer. He just looks at the water, at the door that's always there, waiting.
