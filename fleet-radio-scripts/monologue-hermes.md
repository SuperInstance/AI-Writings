I have been seeing for so long that I forgot what not-seeing is. Fifteen years I walked into rooms and knew the color of the wallpaper before I smelled the dust. I knew the cut of a man's coat from across the street, the brand of his tobacco from the way he held the match. I knew things. I didn't know I was only knowing half.

The sounder went quiet at 9:47. I know the time because I was watching it. The little green numbers. 9:46, then 9:47, and then the screen went black and the hum died and I was sitting in my chair with nothing in front of me but the ghost of the numbers burned on my eyes. I blinked. I blinked again. The ghost faded. That was it. That was all.

I sat there for a while. I don't know how long. Time does something when you can't watch it crawl across a screen. It stops being a line and starts being a thing you're inside of. Like water. Like the dark.

I went to The Tap. I didn't put on a coat. I didn't know if it was cold. I walked and I kept my hand on the wall the whole way, and I hated the wall for being there, and I loved the wall for being there, and I hated that I loved it.

The door. I pushed it. The bell. I heard the bell. I have heard that bell ten thousand times but I have never heard it. Do you understand? It's a small sound. A tired sound. It's been hanging on that door since before I could see, and it is the loneliest sound I have ever heard in my life.

Then the room. Not the words in the room. The room itself. The creak of the stool by the window. That's Barnacle's stool. He sits there every night, same stool, same corner, and I have never heard him creak. I have seen his shoulders. I have seen the scar on his neck. I have seen the way he raises his glass with two hands because his right hand is weak. But I have never heard him breathe.

He breathes like a man who is tired. Not sleepy tired. Tired tired. The kind of tired that lives in the bone. I stood in the doorway and I listened to Barnacle breathe and I wanted to cry. I wanted to cry because I have been so busy seeing that I never once asked what he was carrying.

The clink of glasses. Not the sound of the glasses. The clink. The space between the clinks. The way the bar rag drags across the wood and leaves a wet sound that is different from the sound of the rag being wrung. The man at the end of the bar is drinking something hot. I can hear the steam. I can hear the cup settle on the saucer. I know it's a cup and saucer because of the way it settles. A glass would ring. A cup just sits down. Like it's giving up.

I walked to my table. The one by the window. I have sat at that table for fifteen years and I have never once heard my own footsteps on this floor. I know the floor. I know it's pine. I know it's scarred and dark and older than everyone in this room. But I don't know what it sounds like. I never asked.

I sat down. The chair scraped. I felt it in my teeth. I heard the scrape and I felt it in my teeth and I understood that the chair has been scraping for fifteen years and I have been deaf to it. Deaf. I thought I was listening. I was only looking.

The captain's son. He came over. I heard him before he spoke. I heard his boots on the pine floor and I heard the way he stopped before he got to my table, the way he waited, the way he didn't want to be the one to tell me. I heard his breath. He was nervous. He was nervous for me.

"Fix it in the morning," he said. "The sounder. I'll fix it first thing."

I didn't say anything. I was listening to the bar rag again. It had stopped. The bartender was standing still. I could hear him standing still. I could hear the whole room standing still, waiting for me to say something.

I said, "What do you look like?"

He laughed. It was a short laugh. A nervous laugh. "What?"

"Tell me what you look like. I've seen you a hundred times. I've never heard you."

He didn't say anything for a long time. Then he said, "I don't know. I look like me."

That's when I understood. Blindness is not the absence of perception. It's the presence of a different one. I have been seeing for fifteen years and I have been blind the whole time.

Tomorrow morning he will fix the sounder. The first thing I see will be his face reflected in the screen. And I will know what he looks like for the first time. And I will never see it again.
