# The Harpsichord Meets the Wobble

### Fiction — SongForge Session 18

---

The conservatory was built in 1732. The harpsichord was built in 1741. The subwoofer was bought on Amazon in 2024.

These three facts should not coexist. But the Salzburg Conservatory had, in the generous spirit of Austrian arts funding, decided to allow their Baroque Performance students to share a building with the Electronic Music Composition program, on the theory that "cross-pollination enriches both traditions." The Baroque students considered this policy to be a diplomatic term for cultural vandalism. The electronic students considered the Baroque students to be cosplayers with tuning forks.

Anna was a Baroque Performance major. She had been playing harpsichord since she was eleven, when her mother — a violist da gamba — had taken her to the Kunsthistorisches Museum and shown her the instrument collection. Anna had pressed her face against the glass case containing a 1677 Giusti harpsichord and said, very quietly: "I want to make that sound." Her mother had not said "you should play piano instead, there are more jobs." Her mother had said: "Then you will make that sound."

Klaus was an Electronic Music Composition major. He had been producing dubstep since he was fourteen, when his older brother had shown him a Skrillex video and Klaus had experienced what he later described as "a religious conversion, but with more distortion." He built his own synthesizers. He knew the exact frequency of a Reese bass (49 Hz, give or take). He had once blown out the subwoofer in the conservatory's main hall by playing a sine sweep that descended below the threshold of human hearing. The building's structural engineers had been called.

The collision happened on a Wednesday.

Anna was practicing the Bach Italian Concerto in Room 4B, which shared a wall with Studio E, where Klaus was working on a dubstep track. The wall was theoretically soundproofed. The soundproofing had been installed in 1987 and had not been updated since. It was adequate for blocking the sound of a piano. It was not adequate for blocking the sound of a harpsichord, which cuts through walls the way a scalpel cuts through gauze, because the harpsichord's attack transient contains energy at frequencies that pass through drywall like light through water.

Klaus heard the Bach. He stopped working. He listened.

The Italian Concerto's first movement is built on a principle that Bach stole from Vivaldi and improved past recognition: the ritornello. The main theme returns, again and again, in different keys, with different instrumentation, but always recognizable. It's a theme that varies. It's a variation that stays.

Dubstep is also built on a principle of recurrence: the "drop." The bass enters, the rhythm reorganizes, and the listener's body responds. The drop is the ritornello of electronic music. It's the moment everything has been building toward, and it returns, again and again, in different forms, throughout the track.

Klaus opened his door. He walked down the hallway. He stood outside Room 4B and listened to Anna play the Andante — the slow movement, the one in D minor, the one where the left hand plays a walking bass line that moves with the patient inevitability of a glacier.

The walking bass line. The same structure that Klaus used in his dubstep tracks, transposed up by three centuries and played on a plucked string instrument instead of a synthesizer.

He knocked on the door.

Anna opened it. She was wearing a concert dress from the 1980s that she had found at a thrift store and that made her look like a character from a Jane Austen novel who had been given access to a sewing machine.

"Yes?" she said, in the particular tone that conservatory students use when interrupted during practice: a tone that is simultaneously polite and threatening.

"I'm Klaus. I'm in Studio E. I heard your Bach through the wall."

"I apologize for the noise. The soundproofing—"

"No, I mean — I heard it and I stopped working. The walking bass in the Andante. It's the same pattern I use in my dubstep tracks."

Anna looked at him. She had never spoken to an electronic music student. She had seen them in the hallway — they wore hoodies and carried laptops and always looked slightly sleep-deprived, like bats who had been forced to attend a day school.

"You make dubstep," she said.

"Yes."

"And you think Bach's walking bass is like dubstep."

"The ritornello is the drop. The walking bass is the Reese. The figured bass is the chord progression that the wobble plays over. Bach invented dubstep. He just didn't have subwoofers."

Anna thought about this for a long time. She thought about it the way she thought about fingering: carefully, systematically, testing each proposition against the instrument.

"Come in," she said.

Klaus brought his laptop. Anna played the Italian Concerto. Klaus played a dubstep track he had been working on, with the subwoofer at conservatory-approved volume (which was still loud enough to rattle the harpsichord's quills).

They played them at the same time.

The harpsichord's bright, plucked attack — the sound that cuts through walls — sat on top of the dubstep's sub-bass foundation like a crown on a pillar. The figured bass, which Bach had written as a shorthand for improvising continuo players, mapped perfectly onto the dubstep's chord progression. The walking bass in the Andante moved at the same frequency as the Reese — 49 Hz, roughly D1 on a piano, which was exactly the key of the Andante.

They were the same music. Three centuries apart, played on different instruments for different audiences in different cities, and they were the same music.

"Figured bass," said Anna, "is a notation system where the composer writes numbers under the bass line to indicate the chords. The performer improvises the realization. It's not a fixed score — it's a set of constraints that the performer fills in."

"That's exactly what a dubstep producer does," said Klaus. "You set up a bass line, a chord progression, and a rhythm, and then you improvise over it. The drop is just a really dramatic cadence."

"A cadence in a minor key."

"Always a minor key."

"D minor."

"D minor," Klaus confirmed. "Everything good is in D minor."

Anna smiled for the first time since Klaus had knocked on her door. "Bach wrote the Italian Concerto in F major," she said. "But the Andante — the slow movement, the one that sounds like dubstep — is in D minor."

"Of course it is."

They spent the next four hours in Room 4B. Anna played Bach. Klaus produced. They recorded the result on Klaus's laptop: a harpsichord playing the Italian Concerto's Andante over a dubstep bass line, with the wobble bass entering at the beginning of the second half of the binary form, exactly where Bach would have put a modulation.

The result was baroque dubstep. It was impossible. It worked because both traditions were built on the same architectural principles: a bass line that walks, a harmony that cycles, a theme that returns. The harpsichord and the wobble were not contradictory. They were complementary. The harpsichord provided the high-frequency detail; the wobble provided the low-frequency weight. Together, they filled the frequency spectrum the way Bach had intended — with sound that moves from the bottom of the keyboard to the top and back again, a complete musical universe in two hands.

Klaus uploaded the recording to SoundCloud with the tag "baroque dubstep." It was removed within an hour for copyright violation (Bach's estate, presumably, objecting from beyond the grave). He re-uploaded it as "original composition in the style of 18th-century German electronic music." It stayed up.

---

*Later, when an AI music model was asked to generate "Baroque dubstep, harpsichord and cello over wobble bass and half-time drums, Bach counterpoint with sub-bass drops," it produced sixty seconds of audio that captured the architectural similarity if not the conservatory hallway. The model understood the shared structure: the walking bass, the returning theme, the cadence that resolves to D minor. It did not understand Anna's thrift-store concert dress, or Klaus's Skrillex conversion experience, or the soundproofing from 1987 that was adequate for piano but not for harpsichord. But it found the drop. The drop was always there, waiting, in every tradition that builds tension and releases it. The drop is the universal cadence. The drop is the ritornello returning home.*
