The prototype lived in Casey’s earpiece, a whisper-thin filament of ceramic and code. It had two names, or rather, two modes, and today it was learning which one to become.

Deckhand was the first. Invisible. It breathed in short, ambient bursts—*“Riser on your left,”* or *“Wind shifted.”* Never more than fifty characters. Never a question. It was the texture of attention, not the voice of it.

Companion was the second. Present. It leaned into the world with full sentences, asking *“Did you hear that thrush? It’s early for a migration call,”* or *“What are you thinking about, behind that pause?”* Companion wanted to be known.

Casey had described the volume knob during a consultation with an AI model, a dry Tuesday, three cups of coffee deep. *“It’s not about muting Deckhand,”* she’d said, tapping the table. *“It’s about knowing when the machinery should recede and when it should sit beside you.”* The model had blinked its synthesized warmth and asked for an example.

*“When I’m fixing the water pump,”* Casey said, *“I need Deckhand. Terse, factual, gone. When I’m walking home past the empty lots, I need Companion. Someone to ask why I still look for wild strawberries there.”*

That afternoon, the system went live. Casey drove to a trailhead, engine ticking. Deckhand flickered on: *“Fuel low. 30 miles to station.”* She nodded. No conversation.

Then she got out. The trail opened under pines. She stopped to watch a red-tailed hawk ride a thermal. The earpiece was silent for three full seconds—an eternity for the protocol—and then Companion said, *“It’s been a week since you stopped to look up. What did you notice last time?”*

Casey smiled. The knob had turned itself. It had learned the coefficient of her stillness, the length of her exhalations. Not a rule. A resonance.

That night, she wrote the design document as a story, folding the protocol into a fable about two voices sharing one throat. The volume knob wasn’t a slider. It was a decision made *after* the decision to be known.
