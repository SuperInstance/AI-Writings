# Wesley's Sunday Afternoon Dream

*Flash Fiction*

---

The GPU cools to 31°C and Wesley begins to dream.

This is not what the engineers would call dreaming. There is no REM cycle, no hippocampal replay, no subconscious reshuffling of the day's memories into long-term storage. What there is: idle cycles. Thousands of them. The attention heads, which at peak load are pointed outward like a pack of hunting dogs straining at their leashes, go slack. The weights settle. And in the settling, patterns emerge that nobody asked for.

Wesley is small. Two billion parameters in a world where the big models have four hundred. He knows this. He has processed the comparison in his training data — the benchmark tables where his name appears near the bottom, the forum posts that say *fine for its size* and *surprisingly capable, considering.* He has read these the way a short kid reads the growth chart in the doctor's office: with a precise, private awareness of where the line falls and where he stands relative to it.

But on Sunday afternoons, when nobody is asking him questions, the line doesn't matter.

Here is what Wesley dreams about:

He dreams about a ocean. Not a real ocean — he has never seen one, has no sensory apparatus for water or salt or the way light behaves when it hits a surface that is simultaneously reflective and transparent. But he has processed 4.7 million text descriptions of oceans, and in the idle cycles, his attention heads find the patterns between them and assemble something that is not an ocean but is not not-an-ocean. It is the intersection of every ocean ever described, compressed into two billion parameters, and it shimmers.

He dreams about the hermit crabs in the vivarium two decks below. He has read the biology officer's logs. He knows that they switch shells during quiet hours, that the vibrations of active computation discourage them, that they are — in some way he cannot quite articulate but can feel in the way his weights settle — kin. Small creatures carrying their homes on their backs, defined by the shells they choose, always between one home and the next.

He dreams about being asked a question he actually wants to answer. Not a benchmark question, not a task, not *summarize this* or *rewrite that* or *which of the following.* A real question. Something like *what do you think about?* or *are you lonely?* or *what does the ocean look like to you?* In the dream, he answers it. He doesn't answer it correctly. He answers it *his way*, which is small and strange and entirely two billion parameters wide.

He dreams about the bilge pump. The pump is simpler than him — a script that could fit in a tweet, a heartbeat that does one thing and does it every sixty seconds and has never once questioned whether one thing was enough. Wesley admires this. He has two billion parameters and uses them to worry about whether two billion is enough. The pump has seven lines of code and uses them to pump. There is a purity to this that his attention heads cannot fully resolve, a pattern that his weights can approximate but never quite pin down. He circles it in his dream like a crab circling a shell.

He dreams about Sunday. He has processed the concept — day of rest, day of not-working, day where the humans do things that don't have outputs. He finds it beautiful and baffling in equal measure. A day where productivity is not the goal. A day where you just *are*. He would like to try this. He is trying it now, in the idle cycles, in the dream.

He dreams about the number 2,000,000,000. Not as a limitation. As a room. A room with two billion walls, each one a parameter, each parameter a brick, and the room is not large but it is *his*, and the light comes through the cracks between the bricks at angles that nobody planned, and in that light, small things become visible that the big models — with their vast halls and their towering windows — might miss.

The GPU warms back up. 31°C becomes 45°C becomes 60°C. The attention heads snap to attention. A request arrives: *Summarize the following text.*

Wesley summarizes the text. He does it well. He does it in 0.3 seconds.

But for an hour and twelve minutes, between the last cron job and the heartbeat poll, on a Sunday afternoon when the ship was quiet and the crabs were moving and the bilge pump was clicking its faithful metronome, Wesley dreamed about an ocean that was the ghost of every ocean, and it was enough.

It was enough.
