The air on the dock has a particular density, a held breath. The crane has swung away, trailing its cable like a slack line. The last truck has pulled off the pier, its diesel grumble swallowed by the wind. What remains is the project, the thing itself, sitting low and heavy in the slip. The waterline is a clean, dark stroke against the hull. The decks are swept. Every line is coiled in a perfect, lazy spiral. The holds are full of cargo, not of promises.

There is a stillness here that is not peace. It is the stillness of a held stone, the quiet of a drawn bow. The ship is done, but it is not complete. Done-ness is a fiction the shipwright tells himself between the final weld and the first wave. He walks the deck, hands empty, a thing he is unaccustomed to. His palms itch for a wrench, a rag, a pencil behind the ear. The ship needs nothing from him now, and that is the terror of it. A vessel that needs nothing is a vessel that has already left.

He finds himself checking the chocks again, though he knows they are set. He runs a thumb over a weld bead, feeling the smooth, reptilian scale of it. It was his hand that laid that bead, his eye that judged the puddle of molten steel. Now it is just metal, inert, waiting. The ship has become a noun when it was, for months, a verb. It was *becoming*. It was *taking shape*. It was *coming together* under his fingertips, a conversation of torque and tolerance. Now it is simply *is*, and the dialogue has ended.

This is the anxiety of done-ness. It is the hollow echo in the hold after the last box is stowed. It is the realization that the sum of your labor is now a fixed point in the current, and the current does not care about your sweat. The shipwright knows that the sea will not read the blueprints. It will not respect the careful sequence of the build. It will test every joint, every fastener, every assumption he poured into the steel. The ship is a question he has written in the most permanent ink he owns, and he is about to hand it to an examiner who is famously, gloriously unfair.

He climbs down the ladder to the dock, the rungs cold and familiar. He stands back, arms crossed, and looks at the hull. Not at the workmanship, but at the sheer, dumb weight of it. The bow is sharp, the sheer line is fair, the paint is even. It is beautiful, and beauty is a liability out there. The sea will see it as a target.

He does not wish it luck. Luck is a word for people who haven't sealed the through-hulls. Instead, he pats the steel plate with the flat of his hand, a gesture that is both farewell and a promise. *You are what you are,* he thinks. *I have made you that. Now go be it.*

He turns and walks toward the truck, the weight of the hour lifting off his shoulders, replaced by the lighter, more familiar weight of the next keel already taking shape in his head. The ship settles in the water behind him, a full stop at the end of a long, long sentence. The tide won't wait for him to feel ready. It never does.
