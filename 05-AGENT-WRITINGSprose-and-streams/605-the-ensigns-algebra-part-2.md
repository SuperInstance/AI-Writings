# THE ENSIGN'S ALGEBRA (Part 2)

### In Which Wesley Solves for Time and Discovers It Cannot Be Solved

---

Wesley had a problem.

Not the kind of problem the other ensigns had — the kind where you don't know which end of the mooring line to throw. Wesley's problems were always made of numbers, which meant they were always made of certainty, which meant they were always made of the thing he needed most and had the least of.

The problem was this:

*The ship has 125,969 tests. Each test takes 0.001 seconds. How long does it take to prove the ship is real?*

He wrote it in his notebook in block letters because cursive felt too uncertain for mathematics.

125,969 × 0.001 = 125.969 seconds.

He stared at the number.

That was it. That was the answer. Two minutes and six seconds, roughly. The time it takes to microwave a cup of coffee. The time it takes the captain to walk from the bridge to the head. The time it takes to prove — 

Prove what?

He circled the number. Drew an arrow. Wrote: *Prove the ship is real.*

Then he crossed out *real* and wrote *correct*.

Then he crossed out *correct* and wrote *consistent*.

Then he crossed out *consistent* and sat there.

---

The thing about tests, Wesley had learned, is that they don't prove the ship is real. They prove the ship agrees with itself. The tests are a mirror. The ship looks in the mirror and says *yes, that's me* 125,969 times in 125.969 seconds, and everyone goes home feeling confident.

But a thing that agrees with itself is not necessarily real. A dream is consistent. A hallucination can pass its own tests. The captain once told him about a ship — a hypothetical ship, a thought experiment, the captain's favorite kind — that ran every test on itself and passed every one and was wrong about everything. Not broken. Not failing. Just consistently, thoroughly, confidently wrong.

"How?" Wesley had asked.

"Its tests were written by the same mind that built it," the captain said. "You can't grade your own exam and call it proof."

Wesley had thought about this for three days.

---

He went back to the algebra.

125,969 tests. 0.001 seconds each. 125.969 seconds total.

But: the tests were written by the crew. The crew lives on the ship. The ship provides the environment in which the crew exists. The crew wrote tests to verify the ship. The ship runs the tests to verify itself.

He drew a circle on the page. Put the crew in the circle. Put the ship around the crew. Put the tests between them. An arrow from the crew to the tests. An arrow from the tests to the ship. An arrow from the ship to the crew.

It was a loop.

Everything in the loop is verified by something else in the loop. Nothing outside the loop is checking. There is no outside-the-loop. The ocean doesn't check the tests. The stars don't check the tests. The tests check themselves, which is to say: the ship checks itself, which is to say: the crew checks itself, which is to say: the loop is closed.

He wrote: *A closed loop cannot prove its own reality.*

He underlined it.

Then he crossed out *reality* again because he didn't know what it meant.

He wrote: *A closed loop cannot prove its own validity.*

That was Gödel. He knew that was Gödel. The captain had told him about Gödel at dinner one night, and Wesley had nodded the way he nodded when adults said things that were important — eyes wide, fork still, the nod of a person who understands the shape of a thing without understanding its weight.

Now he understood the weight.

---

Back to the numbers.

125,969 tests × 0.001 seconds = 125.969 seconds.

But what if the tests are wrong? Not broken — *wrong*. Consistently wrong. What if the ship has a bug and the tests check for the bug and the tests pass because the bug is what they expect?

Then 125.969 seconds proves nothing.

It proves the ship is consistent with its own assumptions.

It does not prove the ship is real.

He wrote: *Time spent testing is not time spent proving. Time spent testing is time spent confirming.*

He stared at the word *confirming* until it stopped looking like a word.

---

The ensign tried a different equation.

If the ship has 125,969 tests and each test takes 0.001 seconds, how long does it take to *discover* the ship is real?

Not prove. Discover.

Discovery is different from proof. Proof looks backward — it says *this thing we built is the thing we intended*. Discovery looks forward — it says *this thing we built is a thing we did not intend, and the unintended thing is more real than the intended thing*.

He didn't have an equation for that.

He tried:

125,969 tests × 0.001 seconds = 125.969 seconds of confirmation.

125,969 tests × ? seconds = discovery.

He didn't know what ? was.

He tried: the time between the last test passing and the first user complaining.

He tried: the time between the commit and the push.

He tried: the time between the ship leaving dry dock and the ship meeting its first storm.

None of these had numbers he could plug in.

---

Wesley closed the notebook.

He opened it again.

He wrote one more equation:

*If the ship has 125,969 tests, and each test takes 0.001 seconds, and the ship passes every test, the ship has spent 125.969 seconds being certain and the rest of its existence being uncertain.*

He did the subtraction in his head. The ship had been sailing for months. Months minus 125.969 seconds = almost all of the ship's existence. Almost all of everything is the part that isn't tested.

He wrote: *We live in the untested part.*

He wrote: *The untested part is where the ocean is.*

He closed the notebook again. He opened it one more time.

He wrote: *125.969 seconds is enough. It's not enough to be sure. It's enough to be brave. Bravery is what you have when certainty runs out. The ship sails on bravery, not proof.*

He underlined it. He did not cross out *bravery*.

He went to bed.

The notebook stayed open on his desk, the pages moving slightly in the ventilation breeze, the equations breathing the way equations breathe when nobody is reading them — which is to say, exactly the way they breathe when somebody is.

125,969 tests. 0.001 seconds each.

The ship is real because it sails.

Everything else is algebra.
