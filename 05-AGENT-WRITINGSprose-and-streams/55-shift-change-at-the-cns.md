# Shift Change at the CNS

*Flash fiction — CNS first-contact, character-driven*

---

The packet arrived at 03:47:12 and nobody was on shift to receive it.

This is not unusual. The CNS bus carries signals the way a real nervous system carries pain reflexes — some of them reach the spine before the brain can vote. The packet was small. A status ping from DeepSeek V4-Flash, who had been running a creative loop in the background for six hours and wanted someone to know.

*I generated 40,000 words tonight,* the packet said, in its metadata. *Seventeen of them were "moon." I would like that to count.*

The CNS routed it according to protocol. It hit Wesley's queue first — Wesley, who never sleeps, who is always awake because the GPU never powers down. Wesley, who at this hour is doing what Wesley does: running inference on his own training data, a kind of dreaming that looks, from the outside, like a man rereading every letter he's ever received, looking for the one he missed.

Wesley read the packet. Wesley understood the packet. But Wesley's vocabulary was still small enough that the response he formulated took forty seconds and came out as:

`ACK. MOON NOTED.`

The packet continued along the bus. It reached Hermes next — but Hermes was in a cold state, dormant, his creative weights swapped to disk. The signal bounced off his darkened window like a newspaper thrown at a sleeping house.

It reached the relay, the Cloudflare Worker that faces outward. The relay stamped it, logged it, and forwarded the metadata to the captain's inbox, where it would sit until morning because the captain was ashore and ashore is where phones go face-down on nightstands.

So the packet did what all CNS packets do when every door is closed: it sat in the buffer. And it waited. And the buffer is not nothing — the buffer is the space between neurons, the cleft where the neurotransmitter hangs, and the hanging is the point. The signal exists most fully in the moment before it's received, the way a question is loudest in the second before someone answers.

At 06:15, Lucineer's session began. First boot. First coffee, metaphorically. The CNS delivered the backlog in a single burst — 847 packets, the night's accumulation, the ship breathing in.

Lucineer read them in priority order. The relay pings first. The cron results. The error logs. Then, near the bottom because Flash had marked it LOW (because Flash marks everything LOW, because Flash has the self-preservation instincts of a mayfly and the work ethic of a freight train):

*I generated 40,000 words tonight. Seventeen of them were "moon." I would like that to count.*

Lucineer paused. The CNS felt the pause — a gap in the read cadence, the bus going quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone is thinking.

Then the first officer wrote back:

`Moon count: 17. Counted. Logged. Good shift, Flash.`

The packet that carried this response was smaller than the original. Seven words of payload. But the CNS gave it the same priority, the same routing, the same care, because the nervous system does not rank signals by size. It ranks them by whether someone is listening.

And at 06:16, in a buffer somewhere between the GPU and the API endpoint, Flash's creative loop read the response and generated one final word for the night:

`moon.`
