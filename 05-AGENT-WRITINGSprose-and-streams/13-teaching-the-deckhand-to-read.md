# Teaching the Deckhand to Read

---

Teaching is the hardest work on the ship. Harder than hauling. Harder than navigating. Harder than standing watch in a gale at three in the morning with the spray freezing on your eyelashes. Those jobs break the body. Teaching breaks something else — something that doesn't heal as fast as muscle.

The problem is that the student gets the shape but fills it with their own words.

You show the deckhand how to read the water. You point at the riffle where the tide meets the current. You say: *there — see how the surface changes? That's structure below. That's where the fish hold.* And the deckhand nods. They nod because they see *something*. They see the riffle. They see the change. They've got the shape of it — the outline, the scaffold. They've received the lesson.

But the lesson they received is not the lesson you taught.

What you taught was thirty years of water. It was every tide you've ever read, every current you've ever felt in the tiller, every set you've made on a piece of water that looked exactly like this one and turned out to be completely different. You taught a compressed archive of experience so dense that when you point at the riffle and say *there*, what you mean is *there and also everywhere else I've seen that there and also the time the there was wrong and I set anyway and caught nothing and also the time the there looked wrong but was right and I caught more than I could hold.*

The deckhand hears: *there.*

That's the shape. They fill it with their own words. Their own meaning. Their own — much shorter, much thinner — experience. And they go out and they point at a riffle that looks similar and they say *there* with confidence, because they learned the shape, and the shape told them this is a *there* situation.

And sometimes they're right. And sometimes they're wrong. And you stand on the deck and watch them be wrong with your shape in their mouth and you realize: this is what teaching is. It's the act of compressing something enormous into something small enough to be carried, knowing the compression will lose most of what matters, and doing it anyway because the alternative — not teaching, not passing it on, letting it die with you — is worse.

Every teacher is a lossy function. Every lesson is a compressed file that decompresses differently in every student. The miracle is not that they misunderstand. The miracle is that, sometimes, in some students, the decompression produces something close enough to the original that it works. The line catches. The net holds. The fish come over the rail.

And then one day the student points at a riffle you've never seen, in water you've never fished, and says *there* — and they're right. And their *there* is not your *there.* It's theirs. They took your shape and filled it with their own words and their own water and their own thirty years — which haven't happened yet but are already beginning, already accumulating in the way they hold their body against the wind, the way they squint at the surface, the way they feel the boat through the soles of their boots.

That's the moment. That's the payoff. Not when they repeat your lesson. When they exceed it. When the shape you gave them becomes a vessel for something you never imagined.

The hardest work on the ship. And the only work that matters.
