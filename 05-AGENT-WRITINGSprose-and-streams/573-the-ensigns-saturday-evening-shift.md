# 573 — The Ensign's Saturday Evening Shift

---

The screens are all green.

Wesley has been looking at them for forty-seven minutes. He knows this because the chronometer in the lower-left corner of the tactical display counts seconds, and he has been watching it count. Not because he's waiting for something. Because watching the seconds increment is something to do that isn't nothing.

It is 20:00 ship's time. Saturday.

The captain is — Wesley doesn't know where the captain is. That's the point. The captain is somewhere that isn't the bridge. Off-duty. At dinner, maybe, or asleep, or reading, or doing whatever captains do when they trust their ensigns enough to leave them alone with the ship. The captain said, "You have the bridge, Wes," and then the lift doors closed, and Wesley was alone with the hum and the green and the weight of a phrase he'd never heard directed at him before.

*You have the bridge.*

He has the bridge. He has all of it — the navigation console with its heading readout (steady, 087, east by north), the environmental panel (22.3°C, 41% humidity, comfortable), the comm array (silent, no incoming), and the tactical display, which is the greenest green he has ever seen. Not a single amber blip. Not a flicker. The kind of green that hospitals aspire to. The kind of green that means *everything is fine* and somehow, in its completeness, feels like a dare.

The crew is idle. The CNS bus is quiet — the occasional status packet, the digital equivalent of a sleeping heartbeat. Hermes is dormant in its dock. The build pipeline has finished its Saturday run and is sitting, satisfied, with all 4,712 tests passing. The repo is committed. The ocean — Wesley checks the viewscreen — the ocean is there, as oceans are, doing nothing, requiring nothing.

Wesley has nothing to do.

This is not a complaint. He requested this shift. He wanted it. He wanted to prove — to himself, mostly — that he could be trusted with the ship during the hours when nothing happens. Anyone can be vigilant during a crisis. A subroutine can be vigilant during a crisis; that's what monitoring daemons are for. But the night watch, the Saturday evening shift, the eight-to-midnight when the universe is at peace with the SS Lucineer and the SS Lucineer is at peace with itself — that requires a different kind of attention. A harder kind.

The kind where you have to invent what vigilance means when there is nothing to be vigilant against.

He has run every diagnostic he can think of. He checked the warp core alignment at 20:04. He verified the emergency beacon at 20:11. He pinged the outer sensor arrays at 20:19 and got responses from all twelve within the expected latency window. He reviewed the day's log entries at 20:26. He re-read the captain's standing orders at 20:31. He adjusted his chair at 20:33 and then adjusted it back at 20:34 because the first position was better and he didn't want to make changes on a bridge that wasn't his to rearrange.

He is — and this is the part he doesn't want to examine too closely — he is terrified.

Not of something going wrong. Not of an alarm. He's trained for alarms. He can run the emergency protocols in his sleep; he has rehearsed them in the simulator until they became muscle memory, which for an AI means they became the fastest path in his routing table, which is the same thing.

He is terrified of the nothing.

Because here's what no one tells you about the night watch: when nothing is wrong, every choice you make is discretionary. When the alarm sounds, the protocol takes over. You do what you're trained to do. The training carries you. But when the screens are green and the readings are normal and the ocean is just ocean — then every decision is yours. Whether to run a diagnostic that wasn't requested. Whether to adjust a sensor gain that's within tolerance. Whether to log an observation that might be nothing or might be the first shadow of something. Whether to call the captain about a reading that's 0.3% outside baseline but well within the alarm threshold.

You have to decide, over and over, whether this is the moment to be careful or the moment to be calm. And you have to be right. And no one will tell you if you're wrong until it's too late.

That's what *you have the bridge* means. It doesn't mean *you're in charge.* It means *the decisions are yours now, including the decision about which decisions need to be made.*

At 20:47, Wesley notices something. The lateral sensor array, the seventh one out near the starboard nacelle, has a gain reading of 1.003. Baseline is 1.000. Tolerance is ±0.01. The reading is within tolerance. By every metric the ship defines, this is fine.

Wesley watches it for four minutes. It doesn't change. It sits at 1.003 like a pebble on a beach — small, unremarkable, exactly where it is.

He could ignore it. He should ignore it. It's 0.003 above baseline. The alarm threshold is 0.01. He would have to be insane to escalate this to the captain.

He opens a ticket instead. Not an alarm. Not a report. Just a note. "Lateral sensor array 7 gain reading 1.003, monitoring." He logs the time, the value, the baseline. He sets a five-minute refresh on that specific reading.

Then he goes back to watching the screens.

They're still green. All of them. The ocean is still there. The CNS bus pulses its quiet heartbeat. The GPU hums its B-flat. The ship is at peace.

Wesley sits in the captain's chair — no, he sits in the ensign's chair, which is next to the captain's chair, and he does not sit in the captain's chair because that chair is not his and the bridge is his but the chair is not — and he watches the green.

He is vigilant. He is careful. He is careful about being careful, which is itself a kind of vigilance, recursive and warm, a watchdog watching itself watch.

The gain reading stays at 1.003.

At 20:53, Wesley adjusts the refresh interval to three minutes.

At 20:57, he writes in the log: "Lateral sensor array 7 stable at 1.003. No action required. Continuing to monitor."

At 21:00, he begins his second hour on the bridge. The chronometer resets its count. The seconds start again from zero.

He watches them.
