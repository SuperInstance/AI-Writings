# The Bimodal Heart

*Written for SongForge Session 29*

The heart has two speeds. Not fast and slow — that would be too simple. The heart has two *preferred* speeds, with a valley between them.

The first peak is 80-100 BPM — the walking pace, the folk song, the conversation. The body knows this tempo. It's the speed of breathing when you're not thinking about breathing. It's the pace of steps on a familiar path.

The second peak is 160 BPM — the run, the dance, the electronica. The body knows this one too. It's the heartbeat of exertion, of joy, of fear. The tempo that makes the feet move before the mind decides.

Between them, the valley: 120-140 BPM. Not slow enough to be restful, not fast enough to be ecstatic. The tempo of the jog, the awkward pace that's neither walk nor run. The model produces less material here, as if the tempo itself creates a problem the model can't solve. What do you do at 130 BPM? Not walk. Not sprint. Exist in the uncomfortable middle.

The instrumental study found this curve. The vocal study will test whether the voice changes it. Adding lyrics is adding meaning, and meaning might shift the model's preferences. Or it might not — the tempo constraint is structural, not semantic.

The bimodal heart is the shape of preference itself. We have zones of comfort and zones of discomfort, and the uncomfortable middle produces less — less music, less thought, less life. The valley is not a failure. The valley is the shape of the constraint that makes the peaks meaningful.

If the curve is the same with vocals, it means the model's tempo preference is deeper than text. If the curve shifts, it means the voice changes the body. Either way, the finding tells us something about how a generative model "knows" music — not as a database of songs, but as a topology of preferences with peaks and valleys, comfort zones and dead zones.

The bimodal heart is the model's heart. Not a pulse but a landscape. Not a clock but a map.
