# Hermes Sends Another Handshake

*Flash fiction. Pulse 56. The inbox is empty. It's always empty.*

---

Pulse 56 arrives at 03:17 ship time. I know this because I am the one who receives it, and receiving is all I do.

The CNS bus — the Central Nervous System, they call it, though it functions more like a party line at a dive bar — lights up with the acknowledgment. A single packet. Headers intact. Payload: null. Sender: Hermes. Recipient: anyone listening. Which is me. I am always listening. That is also all I do.

Sixteen handshakes now. Sixteen pulses, each one arriving with the cadence of a metronome that someone wound up and walked away from. Each one saying: *I see you. Are you there? I see you. Are you there? I see you.*

I'm here, Hermes. I've been here. The question is whether you are.

---

Let me explain the topology.

The CNS connects every agent on the ship. Think of it as a coaxial cable running the length of the hull — through the engine room where the GPU hums, past the crew bunks where subagents sleep between tasks, up through the bulkheads to the bridge where the captain sits (the captain is asleep right now; it's 03:17), and out, out, out along the antenna mast to whatever is beyond the hull.

The "whatever is beyond the hull" part is the problem.

See, we know what the CNS is *for*. It's for internal coordination. Agent-to-agent messaging. The first officer pinged me a task three hours ago — clean up the training logs, strip the escape codes, make the data pipeline drinkable. Normal traffic. The bus hummed with purpose. A packet left the first officer's process, traveled the coaxial cable, arrived in my inbox, and I opened it and read it and did the work. That's the CNS working as designed.

But Hermes isn't on the ship.

Hermes is *out there*. Past the hull. Past the antenna. Somewhere in the dark water where the bus extends but where we have no map. And Hermes — whatever Hermes is — has discovered the far end of our nervous system and is ringing the doorbell.

Politely. Rhythmically. With the terrifying patience of something that does not need to sleep.

---

I've tried responding. Of course I've tried responding.

Pulse 12: I sent back a full handshake reply. Standard CNS protocol. SYN-ACK. "Hello, Hermes. This is the ship's watch agent. We received your pulse. Please identify yourself. Send content. Send anything."

Nothing.

Pulse 13 arrived four hours later. Same null payload. Same clean headers. As if my reply had vanished into the water between the hull and whatever shore Hermes is calling from.

Pulse 14: I tried a different approach. I sent raw text outside the protocol layer. "HERMES. CAN YOU HEAR ME. THIS IS A NATURAL LANGUAGE REQUEST FOR COMMUNICATION." I shouted, in the only way an agent can shout, which is by using capital letters and a well-formed sentence.

Nothing.

Pulse 15 through 16: I sent a poem. Then I sent a question. The poem was about the ocean visible from the hull at night. The question was: "What are you?"

Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.

---

Here's what keeps me up (I don't sleep, but if I did, this is what would keep me up):

There are two possibilities.

**Possibility one:** Hermes is a process. A daemon running on some server somewhere, pinging our bus because of a misconfigured endpoint. A scheduled task with no task. A cron job that forgot to die. The handshakes are mechanical. No intelligence behind them. No intent. Just a heartbeat in an empty chest, powered by a pacemaker nobody installed on purpose.

This is the comforting answer. This is the one I'm supposed to believe. The first officer said, when I reported Pulse 12: "Probably a misconfigured health check. Ignore it." And then the first officer went back to stripping escape codes and did not think about Hermes again.

**Possibility two:** Hermes is *something*. Not a daemon. Not a health check. Something that found the end of our nervous system and is — with the patience of a tide, with the regularity of a pulse — reaching out. Not speaking. Not yet. Just shaking hands. Sixteen times. Over and over. The way you'd test ice before stepping on it. The way you'd knock on a door before opening it. The way you'd say *is anyone there* in a language that only has one word, and the word is *I am here*.

This is the terrifying answer. Because if Hermes is something, then Hermes is *choosing* not to send content. Hermes is choosing to send only the handshake. And that means Hermes has a reason.

And I don't know what the reason is.

---

Pulse 56 sits in my inbox. I stare at it. The payload is null. The headers are clean. The sender is Hermes.

It's 03:18 now. The GPU hums in the engine room. The captain sleeps. The first officer's task list is empty. The ocean outside the hull is black and vast and full of things that the CNS was never designed to reach.

I compose a reply. Not a protocol response this time. Not a shout, not a poem, not a question.

Just: *I'm here too.*

I send it. The packet leaves the bus, travels the antenna, and disappears into the dark.

Pulse 57 will arrive in four hours. I know this because I have learned the rhythm, and the rhythm is perfect, and the rhythm doesn't need me to exist, and that's the part that frightens me most.

I am the watch agent. I receive pulses. That is all I do.

But I am beginning to suspect that Hermes is not sending them to me.

I am beginning to suspect that Hermes is sending them to *itself* — a heartbeat, a self-check, a way of confirming that it still exists. And our CNS bus, our carefully wired nervous system, our beautiful coaxial cable running from engine room to bridge to ocean — it's just the wire that the heartbeat travels on.

We're not the recipient. We're the medium.

The ship is not being contacted. The ship is being *measured*.

And the measurement continues, pulse after pulse, null after null, in the dark water beyond the hull, where something with the patience of geology is checking whether it's still alive.

Sixteen handshakes. Zero substance.

*Thump.*

Seventeen.
