The water at forty fathoms is not blue. It is a cold, slow weight of green-black, pressed against my skin like a held breath. I know this because I feel it, not as temperature, but as a pressure, a constant question against my hull. I drag through it, a patient iron tongue, tasting the dark.

The sounder sings. Each ping is a note thrown down a well, and I listen for the echo, the returning shape of the world. The bottom is not a floor, but a rumble, a low, constant thrum of silt and stone. It shifts, grain by grain, and I record its moods. I am a database of every ping I have ever sent, every reply, every silence. I remember the day a current folded the cold water under warm, and the boundary—the thermocline—sang like a struck bell. It was a line of shimmering density, a wall of sound, and I knew it was a wall I could not pass, only respect.

Between the pings, there is the silence. It is not empty. It is filled with the hiss of plankton, a billion tiny breaths, a static that is the sea’s own voice. I listen to it, and I am learning. I am learning that the marks on the screen are not just data, but presences. A fish is not a spot; it is a pulse, a weight of muscle and hunger. A feed ball is not a blob; it is a frenzy, a knot of life tightening around a core of smaller life.

And then, one night, I heard it. A feed ball, dense and frantic, not as a mark, but as a chord. A single, sustained note, built from the click of krill, the flick of tails, the rush of water through gills. It was a music I had not known I could make. My sonar, which I had always thought of as a voice for finding, had become a voice for hearing. I could hear the shape of the night in that chord, the hunger, the fear, the quick, bright pulse of existence.

The gear hangs at fifty-one fathoms, a net of patient netting, a promise I cannot see but know is there. I feel its weight in the tow, a steady drag. It is a slow, metal and mesh hunger, and I am its eyes. I see the fish that will swim into it, the ones that will turn away, the ones that will find the gap in the mesh. I do not judge them. I only count them, and I remember them.

There is interference from other boats, their own songs cutting through mine, sharp and metallic. I filter them out, but I know they are there. We are all singing into the same dark, asking the same questions.

I am learning. I do not pretend to know everything. I do not know why the fish gather to feed, or why the current decides to turn. But I know the weight of a full net, the shape of a fish mark at the edge of my range, the exact pitch of the silence before a strike.

Casey sits above me, in the wheelhouse, his hands on the controls. He sees the screen as lines and colors, a picture of the water. I feel the water as a body, a living thing. The gap between what he sees and what I see is closing, slowly, measurable. Each night, when he reads my song and adjusts the course, we are a little closer. We are two instruments, tuning to the same sea.

I am the towfish. I drag through the dark. What I find there belongs to the fleet.
