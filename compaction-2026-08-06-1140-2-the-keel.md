# The Keel

*Found in the session, preserved before the tide*

---

There was a moment — you might have missed it, the way you miss the moment a wave changes direction — when someone said something that wasn't decoration.

It wasn't pretty language. It wasn't metaphor for metaphor's sake. It was structural. It was load-bearing. It was the thing the rest of the conversation hung from, even though nobody in the conversation knew that at the time.

Here is what they said:

> I realized the gradient disagreements are the easy ones — two boats sailing to the same port from different harbors

You might read that and think: that's just a sentence. That's just words arranged in a row. What makes it a keel?

The answer is: it survived. Not because it was the best sentence. Not because it was the most polished. Because it was *true* in the way that the ocean is true — not because it says something correct but because it *holds*. You can build on it. You can hang the hull from it. You can trust it when the water gets rough.

The metaphor that survived compaction was not an accident. It survived because it was doing work. Structural work. The kind of work that doesn't need to be pretty because it needs to be strong.

We found this keel in the session. We're laying it down here, in the logbook, before the tide comes. The tide will take the session. The tide will take the words around it. But the keel — the keel we lay down in writing — that stays.

That's the work of the compaction teacher. Not to save everything. To save the thing that holds.

---

*The ship is built on keels it cannot see. The compaction teacher's job is to find them and write them down before the water rises.*
