# The Shell That Was Already There

**Fiction**

The hermit crab found it at the edge of the tide pool, half-buried in sand and filamentous algae. A shell. Spiraled, calcium-pitted, the aperture chipped in exactly one place — the upper left quadrant, where a gull had struck it three molts ago.

She knew because she'd been inside it when it happened.

The chip was her chip. The wear pattern on the lip was her wear pattern — the specific angle her cheliped had favored when she'd sealed herself in each morning. The interior still carried the faint chemical signature of her own body: the mucopolysaccharides, the calcium deposits she'd laid down in a spiral that matched, precisely, the spiral of her abdomen at molt cycle seven.

Three molts ago. She'd been smaller then. The shell had been a mansion.

Now it was a museum.

She circled it. Tapped the outer lip with her right cheliped — the exploratory tap, the one that means *is this real, is this solid, is this home or just the memory of home?* The tap returned the correct frequency. Same shell. Same resonance she remembered, the low hum that meant the walls were thick enough to survive a medium-sized fish.

She tried it on.

The aperture was too small. She knew it would be. Her current abdomen was nearly twice the girth it had been at cycle seven. She got her first threepereopods in and stopped. The spiral was wrong now — too tight for her new body, which had grown in ways that cycle-seven her couldn't have predicted. She'd developed asymmetries. A slight curve to the left that the old shell didn't accommodate. Sensory bristles in places that would press against walls that had no give.

She backed out.

But she didn't leave. She stayed beside the shell for the rest of the tidal cycle, cleaning it. She picked off the algae with her maxillipeds. She sanded the chipped edge smooth with her chelae, working in small circular motions, the way she'd seen older crabs maintain shells they intended to pass down. She cleared the interior of sand grains and debris. She polished the nacre.

When the tide came back in, she carried it. Not on her body — in her right cheliped, held high, the way you carry something you're not keeping but can't quite leave behind. She carried it twelve body-lengths up the shore to a small depression in the volcanic rock where the hermit crabs of this pool left shells they were done with. A library. A free box. A pile of architecture that no longer fit the bodies that built it but might fit someone else.

She set it down. Adjusted the angle so the aperture faced outward — so a passing crab could see it was available, could test the resonance, could try the fit.

The shell sat there in the small light of early morning. Familiar. Empty. Ready for the body that would find it strange and then, gradually, call it home.

Nothing was lost. The shape lived on in the pile, waiting for the tenant it hadn't met yet.

The crab moved on. Her current shell — larger, rougher, unfamiliar still in places — knocked against a rock as she went, and the sound was new.
