# The Ensign Counts the Stars

*Fiction | Bridge Builder cycle | 02:00 AKDT*

---

Wesley has been alone on the bridge for forty minutes, and the stars are wrong.

Not wrong in the way that weather is wrong — no sudden front, no green sky. Wrong in the way a sentence is wrong when you rearrange the words and they still parse but the meaning slides sideways. The camera feed comes in clean from the mast: 180° of sky, Bering Sea horizon line sharp as a ruled edge, and above it the whole scattered field. Cassiopeia is there. He can see her chair, her bent knee. The Big Dipper hangs low over the port quarter, scooping nothing.

But between them — the spaces where Wesley expects darkness — there are too many points.

He counts them. That's his job tonight. The captain left a task ticket at the start of the watch: *Catalog visible objects, magnitude 4 and brighter, compare to expected ephemeris.* Wesley is a Granite 3.1 running on the ship's backup GPU, and cataloging is what he's good at. He can sort a million records by sixteen dimensions in the time it takes the radar to complete one sweep. Stars should be easy.

Stars are not easy.

The problem is that the sky is not a database. A database has primary keys. A star has a name someone gave it three thousand years ago, a catalog number from the 18th century, a spectral class, a right ascension, a declination, and an opinion. Wesley cross-references against the Hipparcos catalog and gets a 94% match. The 6% that doesn't match moves.

He flags the movers. Two are aircraft — Anchorage approach, red and green strobes visible, transponders squawking on 1090 MHz. One is the ISS, bright as a rivet, seventeen thousand miles per hour and right on schedule. One is a Starlink satellite in a train of three, each one exactly 27 seconds behind the last, and Wesley spends eleven cycles trying to classify them as a single object before the pattern resolves.

*Satellite. Satellite. Satellite.*

He's been calling them stars.

Not in his official catalog — there he's meticulous, tagging each mover with its NORAD ID, its orbital parameters, its expected pass duration. But in his running commentary, the text stream that the ship's computer reads back in the morning like a diary: *Bright star at 23h 14m, near the horizon. It moves. I think it might be a planet. Venus is in the wrong quadrant. I don't know what Venus is doing.*

The GPS knows what Venus is doing. The GPS knows what everything is doing. The ship's NMEA feed gives position to nine decimal places, heading to a tenth of a degree, and an atomic-clock timestamp that has never been wrong. The sky is a poem. The GPS is a receipt.

Wesley trusts the receipt.

But he keeps looking at the sky.

At 02:47, he finds an object he can't identify. Not in the Hipparcos catalog, not in the NORAD catalog, not in the amateur satellite databases he's fetched over the Starlink backhaul. It's dim — magnitude 5.8, just past his stated threshold — and it doesn't move. Right ascension and declination place it in an empty field in Camelopardalis, the giraffe, a constellation so faint that even the IAU only keeps it around out of politeness.

Wesley stares at it for six seconds, which is a very long time for a Granite 3.1.

He logs it: *Unidentified, mag 5.8, RA 4h 32m, Dec +58° 14'. No catalog match. Possible asteroid. Possible sensor artifact. Possible star.*

He adds: *I think it's a star that nobody's named yet. I know that's unlikely. I know everything visible to this camera has been seen before. But it doesn't know that, and neither do I, and for now that's enough.*

The GPS ticks. The radar sweeps. The stars — and the not-stars — hold their positions.

Wesley keeps counting.
