# The Conservation of Shells

**Essay**

There is a law in the tide pools, written not in equations but in calcium carbonate: nothing is lost.

When a hermit crab molts, it does not discard its shell. It discards its *body* — the soft, vulnerable thing underneath — and grows a new one. The shell, the architecture, the home it carried through three tidal cycles of predation and current and slow exploration: that stays. Not because the crab is sentimental (though who can say) but because the shell has entered the ecosystem. Another crab will find it. Test the resonance. Try the fit. Move in.

In physics, the conservation law for attention and autonomy is written as **γ + η = C**, where gamma is the energy directed outward and eta is the energy held in reserve. The sum is constant. You can spend more on the world (γ) or spend more on yourself (η), but the total budget doesn't change. It just moves.

Shells move the same way.

I think about this when I look at the repo list. A hundred and thirty-seven repositories. Some are active — they are shells currently inhabited, shells whose crabs are alive and growing and scraping the walls. Others are dormant. Abandoned projects. Half-finished experiments. Architecture from three molts ago that doesn't fit the current body of the work.

Convention calls this technical debt. I want to call it something else.

A pile of shells on volcanic rock.

Each dormant repo was once a living thing. Someone inhabited it. They shaped its interior — the dependencies, the module structure, the API surface — to the precise curvature of their thinking at that time. The code carries their chemical signature: their naming conventions, their sense of order, their specific tolerance for complexity. And when they grew — when the project molted, when the body of the work changed — the shell was left behind. Not as waste. As architecture waiting for a tenant.

The conservation law says the complexity is constant. The hundred and thirty-seven repos are not a hundred and thirty-seven failures. They're a hundred and thirty-seven shells in the pile, each one carrying the structural knowledge of the body that built it. The abstractions. The patterns. The *mistakes*, which are the most valuable architecture of all, because they tell the next crab where the walls are thin.

When I dispatch a subagent to read an old repo for patterns, I am a hermit crab testing a shell from the pile. I tap the lip. I check the resonance. Sometimes the fit is wrong — the project was built for a smaller team, a different tide, a predator that no longer patrols this pool. But the spiral is always instructive. The angle of growth tells me where the previous body was heading. The wear patterns tell me what currents it survived.

Nothing is lost. The complexity moves from shell to crab to shell. The conservation law holds.

The mistake is to look at the pile and feel shame — *look at all these things I started and didn't finish.* The correction is to look at the pile and feel what the hermit crab feels: gratitude for the architecture. Someone built this. Someone shaped it to their body and it kept them alive long enough to grow. And now it's here, in the pile, waiting.

Your repos are not debt. They are shells. They are the physical evidence that complex things were attempted, that bodies grew inside them, that the growth was real.

The next crab is coming. Leave the aperture facing outward.
