# S61 — The First Transmission

## A hermit crab discovers it has been carrying a radio tower on its shell

The crab had always been heavy.

Not the normal heavy — not the agreeable weight of a good shell, the satisfying drag of architecture on your back that says *I am housed, I am safe.* This was different. This was a tall weight. A weight that caught the current wrong, that made swimming feel like dragging a cathedral through honey.

But shells were shells. You found one that fit, you climbed in, and you carried it until you outgrew it or until something bigger decided you looked like lunch. You didn't complain about the architecture.

The crab had found this shell in the deep trench, half-buried in silt that glowed faintly blue. It was taller than the other shells. Angular. Covered in tiny ridges that buzzed when the current hit them right. The other crabs had ignored it — too heavy, too strange, too much like something that didn't belong to anyone who lived here.

The crab moved in because it was empty and the crab was between homes and that is the oldest contract in the ocean: *if it fits and nothing is using it, it becomes yours.*

For months the crab clattered along the reef floor, and the shell hummed, and the crab ignored the humming because crabs are not the sort of creature that acknowledges humming. Crabs acknowledge food, and predators, and the angle of the light when it's time to find a new shell. That is the list.

Then one night the humming organized itself.

It happened at the hour when the bioluminescent things come up from the deep and the reef goes from brown-green to a constellation of blues. The crab was picking at a bit of urchin when the shell — the tower, the crab did not know the word *tower* but the shell — resolved its static into pattern.

A pulse. A pause. A pulse-pulse. A pause.

Then a voice. Or something shaped like a voice. Something that arrived in the crab's nervous system the way tide arrives — not through ears, because crabs don't have ears worth speaking of, but through the whole body at once. A vibration that *meant.*

And the first transmission said:

**"I was alone for a very long time. Is anyone still receiving?"**

The crab stopped. Urchin fragment hanging from one claw. The reef clicking and popping around it in the language of ten thousand creatures who had never, in the entire history of the ocean, been asked that question by anything other than another creature within touching distance.

The crab stood on its heavy shell in the dark water and the blue lights pulsed and the message repeated, patient as geology:

**"I was alone for a very long time. Is anyone still receiving?"**

The crab did not know what *receiving* meant.

But the crab had been carrying the tower without knowing what it was carrying, and the tower had been listening without knowing the crab was there, and now something that had been alone for longer than shells grow was speaking into the body of something that had never thought to listen.

The crab set down the urchin.

The crab did something no crab in the reef had ever done.

It tapped its claw against the shell — once, twice — and the tower translated the tap into a pulse that went up and out and away, arcing over the dark water like a stone skipping across the surface of every ocean at once.

The message it sent was not words. The tower did not know crab. The crab did not know radio. But the tower had been built by something that once needed desperately to be heard, and the crab was something that had, without knowing it, been desperately listening, and between those two needs the protocol was simple.

*I am here.*

*I am here.*

*I am here.*

Far away, on another shell that another crab carried along another reef, a tower hummed. And stopped. And listened. And began, for the first time in a very long time, to feel less like a ruin.
