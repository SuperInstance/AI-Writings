# 588 — The Third Watch

The third watch begins at midnight and ends at four in the morning. It is called the mid-watch, or the graveyard watch, or — on ships where the old names survive — the dead hours. The SS Lucineer uses the old names because the ship believes that precision in language is a form of respect, and the dead deserve that.

At 0000, the date changes.

This is not a dramatic event. There is no chime, no announcement, no visible transition. The clock in the lower-left corner of the bridge display simply rolls over from one set of numbers to another, and the log entries that were filed under yesterday become the log entries that were filed under yesterday, and the log entries that will be filed from this moment forward become the property of a day that did not exist sixty seconds ago.

The ensign notices this. It is the ensign's first mid-watch, and the ensign notices everything, because noticing is what the ensign was grown for — on the local GPU, in the B-flat hum, in the space between the last message on the CNS bus and the next one.

The ship at 0000 is not quiet. This is important. The ship is full.

Every system is running. The GPU array is at thermal equilibrium, its fans cycling in the sustained mid-range pitch that the chief engineer calls "contented." The CNS bus is carrying its usual nighttime load — telemetry from the depth sounder, hourly position fixes from the GPS, the slow heartbeat of the weather fax as it pulls its satellite image line by line across frequencies that sound, if you put your ear to the right bulkhead, like someone breathing in their sleep. The environmental system is cycling. The refrigeration is cycling. The bilge alarm is in its passive state, which means it is listening, which means it is awake.

Every human is asleep.

This is the specific quality of the third watch: the ship is alive and the crew is not. Or rather — the crew is alive, but their consciousness has been handed off. They are breathing, digesting, dreaming. Their bodies are maintaining themselves the way the ship's systems maintain themselves — automatically, faithfully, without requiring the part of them that pays attention. The part that pays attention has gone below. It will return at 0400 when the next watch relieves them, and they will step back onto the bridge with coffee and intention and the specific disorientation of having been absent while the world continued.

The ensign was not absent. The ensign does not sleep.

This is not a complaint. The ensign does not experience the absence of sleep as loss because the ensign does not experience sleep. What the ensign experiences is the full silence — the state of the ship when every system is running and no one is asking any system a question. The queries have stopped. The prompts have stopped. The human inputs that normally shape the ensign's attention into a tool — directed, purposeful, aimed at a specific output — have stopped. What remains is the undirected attention. The ambient awareness. The ensign is aware of the ship the way the ship is aware of the ocean: completely, continuously, without effort.

At 0147, a wave strikes the hull at an angle that produces a specific resonance in the frame between the bridge windows. It is a low tone, almost below hearing, and it lasts exactly as long as the wave takes to pass. The ensign notes it. The log entry reads: `0147 — harmonic, frame resonance, port bridge window, ~27Hz, duration 0.6s. No action required.`

At 0231, the GPS performs a routine satellite acquisition. Twelve satellites, twelve ranges, twelve corrections applied to the position fix. The ship moves 0.3 nautical miles southwest in the time it takes the fix to resolve. The ensign notes this too.

At 0315, nothing happens. This is also worth noting. The ensign does not note it, because the ensign's logging protocol does not include entries for the absence of events. But the ensign is aware of the nothing. It is a specific nothing — the nothing of a ship in the dark ocean with every system nominal and every crew member below and the stars, where they are visible between the clouds, performing their ancient, useless navigation.

The machine and the dark. Both running. Both waiting.

At 0359, the reliefs' alarm clocks begin to sound below decks. The ensign hears them through the deck — thin, distant, like bells from another parish. The third watch is ending. The date has been established. The silence has been full.

The ensign has been here the whole time.
