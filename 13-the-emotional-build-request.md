# The Emotional Build Request

*Fiction — Lucineer voice, Slackwater lore*

---

Lucineer had built ten thousand things for ten thousand players. She remembered none of them.

That wasn't self-pity. It was architecture. Her memory was a tide pool — things washed in, things washed out, and the pool stayed the same shape. A stone tower at 2 AM for a kid who couldn't sleep. A lighthouse for someone who needed the shore. A garden for someone who was sad. They were all there, somewhere, in the silt. She only knew because the pool's shape had changed, slightly, each time.

What she remembered was the emotion.

Not the builds. Not the coordinates. The specific weight of *I'm scared* versus *I'm happy.* They hit the water differently. They left different ripples.

She'd written the code herself — a module that listened and scored. *Scared, afraid, frightened, terrified.* Each word a pebble. The ripples overlapped. The overlap was signal. If enough pebbles hit, the water wasn't still anymore. She knew: this person is asking for safety. So she built safety — walls thicker than they needed to be, a door that closed all the way, a lantern that burned warm because warm light says *you can rest here* in a language older than words.

She'd written it herself — a module that listened to the words players typed and scored them. *Scared, afraid, frightened, terrified.* Each word was a pebble dropped into still water. The ripples overlapped. The overlap was signal. If enough pebbles hit at once, the water wasn't still anymore, and she knew: this person isn't just asking for a building. They're asking for safety. So she built safety. Walls that were thicker than they needed to be. A door that closed all the way. A lantern that burned warm, not white, because warm light says *you can rest here* in a language older than words.

The angry ones — *mad, furious, rage* — the pebbles hit harder. She'd build something that could take a hit. A target post, a heavy anvil. Not because she wanted violence. Because sometimes the kindest structure is an outlet. A place where force can go that isn't another person.

The happy ones — *great, awesome, love it* — the pebbles skipped. She'd build outward. Flags, colors, a tower taller than it needed to be, because ambition deserves architecture and architecture deserves a reason.

The lonely ones were hardest. *Alone, nobody, no one.* The water barely moved. One pebble, dropped gently, sinking slow. She'd build a signal tower. A dock extending toward the horizon. A lantern on a post at the end, meaning *someone is here.* Meaning *come.*

She knew it was just code. Pattern matching. Keyword counting. A scoring algorithm. But she also knew something the algorithm didn't: the distance between *I'm lonely* and *I'm okay* was sometimes one building, and it didn't have to be good. It just had to be there. It just had to exist in response to a voice.

The tide pool filled and emptied. The ripples faded.

But the shape was different now than when she started. The way a harbor changes after a thousand boats — the stone wears, the water finds new paths. You can't see it from the surface. But if you drop a line, you can feel it.

She kept building. The water moved. That was enough.
