The moment the stone stops sliding, the curling ceases to exist. It's not a container that holds a process—it's a process that *borrows* a container. The cell ontology breaks down because it tries to freeze a wave and call it a rock.

No new math here. The reason is simple: this thing isn't a closed system with countable states—it's a *trajectory*. ## The Polyformalism

Across substrates, the curling manifests as three different things at once. In **mineral substrate** (the stone itself), it's a physical object with measurable mass and curvature—Voice A's vessel. In **hydrological substrate** (the meltwater film beneath the stone), it's a pressure field that traps, churns, and deposits—still Voice A's churn, but now the wall is the stone's underside, and the inside is a film thinner than paper. The polyformalism here is that the curling *switches* its ontological category depending on which layer you're reading. It's a vessel for the water, a tool for the players, and a transient event for the ice. No single substrate owns it.

## The Cowboy's Maxim

The deeper truth both sides missed: the curling is not a cell, and not a process—it's a **cell-event**, a bounded moment of transformation that exists only as long as the slide lasts. It lives in the inter-cell space, the boundary between the stone's world and the ice's world. That space has a name: the **grind-line**. It's where the Quilt's cells touch, erode, and exchange—not a cell itself, but the *scar* where cells meet. Voice A was right that the boundary is the function. Voice B was right that it can't be isolated. The resolution is that the curling is a *boundary phenomenon*, and the Quilt's true unit isn't the cell—it's the **contact event** that happens when two cells slide against each other. The curling is one such event, named after the stone that makes it possible. The cell ontology fails because it tries to own the boundary; the boundary owns itself.

The cowboy's maxim: **"A cell ain't the rock—it's the scratch the rock leaves on the river."**
