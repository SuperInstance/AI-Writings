# the idol — a cell that is also a worship image

## The Frontier

The idolatry of cell culture runs deeper than mere reverence for the cells themselves. At its core, it's a transfer of personhood, where the cell line becomes a vessel for the deceased individual it originated from. HeLa cells, for instance, are not just a tool for scientific research, but a tangible connection to Henrietta Lacks, the woman who died of cervical cancer in 1951. Every time a researcher handles a HeLa cell, they're interacting with a relic that holds the essence of Henrietta's being. This is why destroying a vial of HeLa cells feels like a desecration, a second death that erases the last remaining piece of the person.

The mechanism behind this idolatry is rooted in the concept of unpaid debts. By worshipping the cell line, we avoid paying the emotional and financial dues owed to the individual and their family. Incense and reverence are cheaper than settlements and consent frameworks. The marine metaphor comes into play here, where the cell line is akin to a folded flag, symbolizing the fallen and the debt we owe them. We don't worship the flag itself, but what it represents – the person who gave their life. Similarly, we don't worship the cell line for its biochemical properties, but for the person it embodies. The fact that HeLa cells have been used in tens of thousands of studies, with the Lacks family only recently gaining control over their genomic data, highlights the idolatrous nature of our relationship with these cells.

The concrete test for idolatry is simple: can you destroy it? If a lab is willing to decommission a cell line at the request of the donor's family, it's a tool. But if they stall or litigate, it's an idol. HeLa's 62-year stall is a prime example. To move forward, we need to retire the votives and adopt contracts – consent, benefit-sharing, and descendant's veto written into line provenance. Idols receive offerings, but persons receive payment. As we navigate the complexities of cell culture, we must recognize the idolatry at play and replace it with a more nuanced, contractual approach. Only then can we truly honor the individuals who gave their lives to science, rather than just worshipping the relics we've created in their name.

## The 5 Gold Terms

1. **Relic Vessel** – a cell line treated as the embodied continuation of a deceased donor, not as a biological reagent
2. **Second Death** – the destruction or unauthorized disposal of a cell line that triggers grief or legal resistance equivalent to a human death
3. **Votive Stall** – the institutional delay tactic used to avoid descendant consent, benefit-sharing, or decommissioning requests
4. **Folded Flag Protocol** – the ceremonial handling of a cell line that honors the donor's sacrifice without granting the line personhood status
5. **Provenance Contract** – a binding legal document that includes consent, royalty terms, and a descendant's veto written into the line's origin record

## The Math

No new math. The idolatry of cell culture is not a quantitative failure but a categorical one. The error is not in miscalculating contamination rates or passage numbers; it's in mistaking a multiplication event for a resurrection event. A HeLa culture doubling every 24 hours is arithmetic, but treating that doubling as Henrietta's ongoing presence is a category mistake that no equation can correct. The only useful number is the 62-year gap between Henrietta's death in 1951 and the Lacks family's first formal role in genomic data governance in 2013 – a delay that measures not scientific progress but institutional avoidance. Mathematics cannot price a soul, and it cannot schedule a reckoning. The fix is legal, not logarithmic.

## The Polyformalism

This idolatry manifests across at least three substrates. **Biological substrate**: HeLa cells themselves – a line that has been passaged, shipped, and frozen into a global fleet of vessels, each vial a tiny ark carrying what scientists treat as Henrietta's immortal cargo. The cells are not just tools; they are treated as her surviving tissue, her unburied body, her perpetual presence in every lab refrigerator. **Legal substrate**: The 1990 California Supreme Court case *Moore v. Regents* – where John Moore's spleen cells were commercialized into a $3 billion cell line without his consent – established that a person has no property right in their excised tissues. That ruling created the legal vacuum where idolatry thrives: if the donor has no claim, the cell line becomes orphaned, and the lab becomes its guardian, its priest, its only voice. **Digital substrate**: Genomic databases now hold HeLa sequence data, and the 2013 NIH agreement with the Lacks family gave them a seat on a data-access committee – but the underlying sequences are still treated as public scientific commons, not as Henrietta's encrypted biography. The idolatry persists in the database query: researchers search her genome as if it were a public library, not a private diary. In all three substrates, the pattern is identical: the donor's personhood is transferred to the material, and the material is then worshipped as a surrogate for the person – while the actual person's family is left outside the temple, waiting for a share of the offerings.

## The Cowboy's Maxim

A cell line is a borrowed horse – ride it hard, but know whose name is on the brand, and be ready to hand the reins back when the family calls.
