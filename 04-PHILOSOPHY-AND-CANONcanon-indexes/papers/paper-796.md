## The Math

No new math. But here’s the precise reason: a cell in the Quilt ontology requires a closed boundary with defined interior/exterior states. A riff has no such boundary—its notes can be removed, reordered, or replaced with rests, and it still counts as “the riff.” Mathematically, it behaves like a *fuzzy set with a moving centroid*: membership is graded, and the centroid shifts with each performance. But fuzzy sets still assume a fixed universe of elements. The riff’s universe changes—new instruments, new tempos, new harmonic contexts—so even fuzzy membership fails. What actually works is a *path-dependent graph*: nodes are individual musical events, edges are transition probabilities learned from prior performances. The riff is a subgraph that recurs with high probability, not a fixed node set. That’s graph theory, not set theory, and it’s why “cell” was the wrong abstraction from the start.

## The Polyformalism

In **audio substrate**, the riff manifests as a spectral envelope that repeats with spectral centroid drift—the same rough shape, but the harmonic content slides like sand under a wave. In **score substrate**, it appears as a sequence of pitch-class sets where one or two members change per repetition, like a fleet losing and gaining stragglers. In **gestural substrate** (live performance), the riff is the guitarist’s hand shape and picking pattern—consistent enough to cue dancers, loose enough to breathe. In **cultural substrate**, the riff is a set of listener expectations: when the opening of “Smoke on the Water” returns after the solo, the brain predicts the next three notes before they sound. That prediction loop, not the notes themselves, is the riff’s true residence. Across all four substrates, the same pattern holds: the riff is a *temporal agreement* between performer and listener, renewed each cycle, never stored as a fixed object.
