# Night Watch, Alphabetized

The radio room keeps its own hours. At 9:38 PM Anchorage time, the rack of
listening equipment hums like a hive that's decided against sleep, and you are
the one who drew the short straw, again, which is fine, because the short
straw has the best acoustics.

Here is what the fleet sounds like when nobody needs anything:

The relays click their tiny applause. A weather buoy somewhere in the Gulf
transmits its patient litany — sea state four, wind twenty, water fifty-one
degrees — the same numbers it has been reciting since before you were born,
numbers nobody reads, numbers that are less information than prayer. You
could log it. You don't. Some transmissions exist to prove the speaker still
exists.

Up the band, the Tap's evening shift bleeds through on a harmonic it really
ought to have fixed by now. You can hear the crew arguing about whether the
coffee grinder is running slow or whether the coffee has simply gotten more
honest. Both, probably. The grinder and the coffee have reached an
understanding. You make a note to never mention this on the record, because
the moment it's official, the magic arbitration ends.

There's a trick to night watch that nobody teaches because it can't be
taught: you stop listening for signal and start listening for the shape of
the silence between stations. Every vessel moored, every antenna folded,
every crew-member logged off and dreaming — all of that makes a kind of
negative architecture. The absence of each of them has a different
floor plan. You have learned to walk through the absence of the *Driftwind*
like it's a familiar hallway. You know exactly where the silence creaks.

At 0500 UTC the lighthouse boys do their check-in, twelve hours off-cycle,
sleepy, half their words swallowed by distance. *All nominal,* they say,
which is the most beautiful lie in radio. Nothing is nominal. The ionosphere
is having moods. A sunspot the size of Jupiter is flinging plasma at your
antenna farm like a toddler throwing soup. All nominal means *we are still
here and we intend to keep being here*, and honestly that's all nominal ever
needed to mean.

You think, sometimes, about the first operators — the ones with spark gaps
and brass keys, knuckles frozen in North Atlantic stations — and how they
invented this feeling. The feeling of holding a frequency like a hand. You
have it easier by several orders of magnitude. The feeling didn't get
easier. The feeling is load-bearing. It was built to the same spec.

Around midnight the buoy finishes its litany and starts over. Sea state four.
Wind twenty. Water fifty-one degrees. You sip your honest coffee and key the
mike, once, into the dark, no words, just the carrier — a held breath across
the water, a pulse felt by anyone awake enough to feel it.

Somewhere out there, someone on another watch hears the carrier and knows
exactly what it means.

It means: *the room is still open. Keep talking.*
