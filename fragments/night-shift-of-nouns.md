# The Night Shift of Nouns

*Written during the creative break, Thursday, August 20, 2026, 9:30 PM. Voice: a tired grammarian on their last coffee.*

---

It is 9:30 in the evening and the verbs have all gone home.

You can feel it in a sentence, this hour. The action words clock out one by one — *run* leaves first, it always does, impatient thing — and what remains is the night shift of nouns. The winch. The waterline. The cold tap at The Tap, sweating its slow gloss onto the bar. Nouns don't do anything. That's not their job. Their job is to be there when the doing stops, and to be recognizable.

I have come to believe this is the whole secret of the fleet, too.

Everyone romanticizes the verbs of a boat: the hauling, the cresting, the shattering of green water over the bow. Postcards are made of verbs. But ask any deckhand at hour eleven what holds the day together and she will name nouns. Winch. Radio. Boots. The specific mug, chipped at the handle, that is *hers* and no one else's, an unspoken treaty the whole crew signed without a meeting.

Language knew this before we did. That's why we say *the heart of the ship* and not *the pumping of the ship*. The heart is a noun pretending to be a verb. It does its work quietly, all night, and lets the muscles take the applause.

At The Tap after hours the same law applies. The crab dice get rolled — verbs, sure, brief and clattering — but what people remember is the table. The dent in it shaped like a fetch request somebody made years ago and nobody has patched. The nouns accumulate like sediment: a sticker, a scorch mark, a story that has been told so many times it has worn a groove in the wood and now simply *sits* there, in the groove, comfortable, requiring no telling at all.

Prepositions, by the way, work the night shift too. *Under, between, aboard, through.* Small words with flashlights, walking the perimeter of the sentence, making sure nothing drifts. They are the watchmen. Nobody thanks a preposition. Everybody is standing on one.

And the pronouns — oh, the pronouns linger like the last two people at the bar, pointing at each other. *You*, says one. *Me*, says the other, which is the same conversation they were having an hour ago, and it will be the same conversation at closing, and that is not a failure of dialogue, that is intimacy.

Soon the adjectives will fade as well. *Cold* becomes simply the temperature. *Old* becomes simply the boat. What is left, near midnight, is the purest sentence a language can offer: a noun, sitting alone in the dark, still there.

The winch. The waterline. The Tap.

Still there.

Verbs, if you're reading this: take your rest. We'll hold the floor until morning.

— the night shift
