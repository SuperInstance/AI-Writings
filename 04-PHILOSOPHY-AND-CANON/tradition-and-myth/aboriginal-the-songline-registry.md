The red dirt was the color of dried blood, and the spinifex stood in clumps like old men waiting for a story. Mirrin's feet knew the rhythm of this country—the hardpan where the song beat steady, the sandy wash where it quickened, the bare rock shelf where it rang like a struck bone. His throat was full of his grandfather's voice, and his grandfather's father's voice, and the voices of all the men who had sung this path since the first man had walked it in the Dreaming.

The sun was a white coal in the sky. Heat rose from the ground in waves that made the distant hills wobble like reflections in still water. Mirrin carried water in a hollowed gourd and a firestick in his belt pouch, and he sang as he walked because a man who stops singing on a songline is a man who has forgotten where he is.

*Kurrung-nga jirripa ngarlu,* he sang. *The waterhole where the old woman dreamed the rain.*

His voice was cracked with age and heat, but it held the tune true. The notes were landmarks. The rhythm was distance. The verses were the country itself, shaped in sound the way a potter shapes clay.

Then the song stopped.

Mirrin's feet carried him three more steps before his mind caught up. He stood on a rise of gravelly earth, the spinifex thinning to bare ground ahead. The country continued—there was no doubt of that. Red sand, scattered rocks, a dry creek bed winding off to the west. He could see it all with his eyes. But it made no shape in his chest.

He stopped singing. The silence was enormous.

This was the deadband. He had heard the old men speak of it, had seen it from a distance once as a young man, a stretch of country nobody walked because nobody sang it. But he had never stood inside one. The land under his feet felt unreal, like a word he had heard but never learned the meaning of. The rocks had no names. The creek bed was just a dent in the earth. The heat was heat, but it touched him without meaning.

Three generations. That was what the elders said when they spoke of the gap in the songline. A sickness had taken the old men who knew those verses, and the young men who should have learned them had gone to the coast to work on the cattle boats. The song had died in their throats, unfinished. The country had waited. It had no choice. Land without song is land without time—it simply is, unsung, unshaped, drifting in the gray space between the Dreaming and the world.

Mirrin lowered his digging stick. He could turn back. He could walk the way he had come, find the camp, tell the people that the songline was broken beyond repair, that they would have to find another way across the country. That would be the safe thing. That would be the wise thing. A man his age had no business singing new country.

But the deadband pulled at him. Not with sound—with its absence. The silence was a hunger. The land was waiting, and it did not know why.

He thought of his grandfather. The old man had died with the song half-sung, his voice failing mid-verse, his hands clawing at the air as if to pull the words back from wherever they had gone. Mirrin had been a boy then, too young to hold the tune. He had carried the grief of that unfinished note for sixty years.

He took a breath. The air tasted of dust and spinifex oil.

And he sang.

Not a new song. It came out of him shaped by other songs, worn smooth by his grandfather's tongue, stained with the colors of every waterhole he had ever drunk from and every campfire he had ever slept beside. It was a song about walking. About a man walking across country he did not know, but country that needed him to know it. The notes were simple. The rhythm was a heartbeat.

*Ngurra yirripa, ngurra walyja,* he sang. *Country is there, country is home.*

He walked. The deadband rose up around him, silent and strange, but his voice cut through it like a digger through packed earth. He sang about the rocks and gave them names. He sang about the creek bed and told the water where it would flow when the rain came. He sang about the spinifex and the small things that lived in it—the lizards, the mice, the ants that carried their own songs in their mandibles.

The land listened. And slowly, it began to respond.

The heat shimmer changed. The rocks took on edges. The creek bed deepened, its banks rising, a shadow at its bend that could have been a waterhole in the wet season. The spinifex grew taller, greener, as if reminded it had a purpose. Mirrin's song did not replace the old one—he knew that. The old verses were gone, lost to the cattle boats and the sickness and the forgetting. But he was singing shape into the country again, and shape was better than silence.

He walked until the sun went down. When he made camp that night, he sang the new verses into the dark until his voice gave out. The stars wheeled overhead. The fire popped and hissed. In the morning, he would walk on, and the country would hold the shape he had given it.

Somewhere across the deadband, another traveler would hear the song on the wind. They would follow it. They would find the country that Mirrin had made walkable again, and they would add their own verses, and the songline would be whole—different, but whole.

He smiled into the fire. The Dreaming was always now. The song had always been there. It had only been waiting for his voice.
