The mead hall breathes. Smoke curls from the central hearth, past the crossbeams, and tangles with the smell of wet wool and roasted meat. Men lean close to the fire, their voices a low rumble, and when the skald rises, the room settles into a hush like snow falling on a still fjord. Eyvind sits in the corner, his back against the timber wall, his hands wrapped around a horn of mead he has not tasted. His eyes are closed. His lips move, silently, tracking every word the skald speaks.

He knows this saga. Of course he knows it. *The Feud of the Grey-Maned Brothers*, one of the old ones, told in the hall of his grandfather’s grandfather. He can feel its rhythm in the pulse at his wrists, its alliterative beats echoing in the hollow of his chest. When the skald stumbles over a kenning, Eyvind’s fingers twitch, aching to correct him. He does not. He simply mouths the words, a shadow to every syllable, a vessel for a story that was never his.

They call him the Living Library. It is not an insult, nor entirely a compliment. It is a fact, like the weather. When a man dies, and his deeds are to be remembered, they summon Eyvind. When a marriage is arranged and the families’ tangled lineages must be woven into a single thread, they summon Eyvind. He sits, a large, quiet man with a beard the color of sea-foam, and he pours out the stories like water from a skin. He never runs dry. He never hesitates. He is the memory of his people, and he has learned to be grateful for it.

But at night, when the hall has gone dark and the fire is a bed of coals, he goes to the chest beneath his sleeping bench. Inside, wrapped in oiled cloth, are the scraps. Parchment stolen from the skald’s stores — a sheet here, a torn corner there, hoarded like gold. Seven years of them. He writes by the dying light, his fingers cramped, his tongue pressing against his teeth. He writes his own saga. The Saga of Eyvind the Unremarkable. Though he would never give it such a name.

It is terrible. He knows this with the same certainty he knows the lineage of every chieftain for three hundred years. The meter is flat, the kennings borrowed, the plot holes wide enough to sail a longship through. His hero — a man with his own face, but braver, more eloquent — does things that make no sense. The battles are choreographed like a child’s game of sticks. The women speak in borrowed voices. He reads it and feels the shame rise in his throat like bile.

And yet he cannot stop. His hand moves across the parchment as if it belongs to someone else. A ghost. A man who has a story to tell.

His personal mythology is older than his name. It was already carved into him before the first settler dragged his high-seat pillars onto this volcanic shore. *I am the library,* it says. *But libraries don’t write books.* He believes this. He has built his life around it. He has become so vast and so empty at the same time — a hall filled with echoes, with no voice of his own.

Tonight, with the skald’s words still humming in his bones, he will go home. He will light no lamp. He will sit in the dark and take the scraps from his chest. He will read what he wrote last night — a scene where his hero crosses a river, and the river is too wide, and the hero drowns, and Eyvind has no idea why he wrote it that way. He will stare at the words until the fire burns out. Then he will fold the parchment, put it back in the chest, and lie down.

Tomorrow, someone will come to him with a question. A genealogy. A feud. A tale of the old gods. He will open his mouth, and the words will flow, perfect and endless and not his own. And he will think, as he always thinks, that this is enough.

It is not enough. But he does not know that yet.
