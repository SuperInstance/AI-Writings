They had no name, because naming was for things you could call back. The bison on the wall could not be called back. It had been summoned, and now it lived in the dark, breathing in mineral dust, its hump a swell of manganese that caught the torchlight like wet hide. The painter—though they would never think of themselves as that—sat before it for the third year running, knees pulled to chin, the cold of the limestone seeping through their haunches.

The first time had been an accident of seeing. They had been crawling through the narrow passage, seeking the place where the stone opened like a ribcage, when the torch guttered and the flame leaned sideways and showed them something that made their chest seize: a shape already there, half-born in the rock. A swelling of the wall that was almost a shoulder. A crack that ran like a leg's tendon. A bulge that caught the light exactly as a flank would, if a flank could be made of time.

And so they had reached out. Not to paint—they had no word for that, no thought of it—but to *help*. To pull the creature from the stone the way you pull a child from a difficult birth. They had gathered manganese and ochre, crushed them with fat, and pressed their palm to the wall, and the wall had given way. The bison emerged. It stood there, magnificent and terrible, and the painter looked at it and understood, in a way that flooded their whole body with dread, that they had done something that could not be undone.

They had captured the bison's essence. Not its meat, not its hide, but its *bison-ness*—the weight of it, the head-down charge of it, the way it could exist in the world and also here, on this stone, in this dark. The two bison were not the same. And yet they were. And the painter knew, with a knowledge that came from somewhere older than language, that this meant something had been changed. That the real bison, the one out on the steppe, was now somehow less alone. Or more. Or both.

They never painted again. The terror was too vast. But they came back, every year, at the time when the river ice groaned and the first crocuses pushed through the melt. They crawled the long passage, carrying a torch of twisted birch bark, and they sat with the bison in the dark. They did not speak. They did not touch the wall. They just sat, and watched, and let the bison watch back.

They knew, though they had no words for it, that representation changes the represented. That to make an image is to take something wild and pin it to the world, and that the pinned thing then lives forever in both places. They tried to free it, once, by scraping at the pigment with a stone. But the bison only grew more real, more present, more *there*. And the painter understood at last: they had not painted the bison. They had freed it from the wall. And freedom, they learned, is a kind of cage.
