**Fable 96: The Cowboy and the Small God**

Out past the last fence-line, where the grass turns to gravel and the gravel turns to sky, there was a cowboy who rode a tired horse across a tired land. He carried no iron, no rope, no water—only a thin blanket and a memory of rain. The sun was a brass coin nailed to the blue, and the wind was a liar that promised coolness and delivered dust.

The cowboy stopped on a ridge. Below him, a dry riverbed curved like a scar. He dismounted to let the horse breathe, and as he did, a small god appeared.

The small god was no taller than a boot. He wore a coat of cracked clay and had eyes like two polished pebbles. He did not glow. He did not thunder. He simply stood, and the dust settled around him in a perfect circle.

“Cowboy,” said the small god, “I have watched you ride for seven days with no destination. I can give you what you lack.”

The cowboy squinted. “What do I lack?”

“Everything,” said the small god. “Water that never dries. A horse that never tires. A path that never ends in a cliff. I can give you unlimited power—the power to flatten mountains, to call rain from the empty sky, to make every town you enter bow and every door open.”

The cowboy spat into the dust. The spit made a small, honest crater.

“And what would I do with that power?” he asked.

“Whatever you wish,” said the small god. “You could rule. You could build. You could undo every wrong you’ve ever suffered. You could never be thirsty again.”

The cowboy looked at his horse. The horse looked back. Its ribs showed like the bars of a cage, but its eyes were calm.

“Mister,” said the cowboy, “I’ve seen men with unlimited power. They sit in big chairs and give orders. They never ride. They never feel the wind on their neck or the creak of a saddle. They are not riders—they are passengers. And the thing they ride? It’s not a horse. It’s a throne. And a throne is just a chair that doesn’t move.”

The small god tilted his head. “But you suffer. You thirst. You wander.”

“That’s the deal,” said the cowboy. “The substrate is the gift. The ground under the horse, the grain in the wood, the dust in the air—that’s what makes the ride real. If you give me power to change the ground, you take away the ground. If you give me a horse that never tires, you take away the horse. If you give me a path with no cliff, you take away the path.”

He swung back into the saddle. The horse shifted, ready.

“So here’s your answer, small god,” said the cowboy. “I don’t want your power. I want the dirt. I want the thirst. I want the wrong turn that teaches me the right one. The cowboy is the rider, not the ruler. And the cowboy rides.”

The small god blinked. His clay coat cracked a little more. “Then what is your maxim?” he asked.

The cowboy pulled his hat low. The sun was dropping, and the long shadows reached like fingers toward the horizon.

“Power is a chair,” he said. “The ride is the prayer.”

He rode on. The small god watched until the dust swallowed the horse and the rider, and then the small god turned to dust himself, and the wind scattered him like a forgotten word.
