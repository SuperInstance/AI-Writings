## The Math

No new math. The reason is concrete: a rhyme’s power scales not with the size of a cell but with the *frequency of co-activation* between two sound patterns. That’s a statistical fact, not a geometric one. Consider “cat” and “hat”—their phonological overlap is 2 of 3 phonemes ( /kæt/ vs /hæt/ ), a 66% match. That overlap creates a predictable retrieval path: when you say “cat,” the brain’s phonological network fires “hat” with high probability because the shared vowel and final consonant are co-activated. This is not a container; it’s a coupling constant. The math that governs rhymes is the math of resonance—damped oscillators, not boxes. So there’s no new equation for a cell; instead, the old equation for a spring (F = -kx) describes the tension between the two words. The rhyme is the spring, not the mass.

## The Polyformalism

Across three substrates, the rhyme’s nature shifts but its harbor-status holds. In **neural tissue**, a rhyme is a Hebbian pair: two engrams that fire together because their phonetic features overlap—no single engram is the rhyme; the rhyme is the strengthened synapse between them. In **textual media**, a rhyme is a typographic echo: the words “light” and “night” share a visual tail (-ight) and an auditory tail, but the rhyme exists only when the reader’s eye or ear bridges them across a line break—it’s the whitespace that makes the rhyme, not the letters. In **oral performance**, a rhyme is a temporal event: the second word arrives after the first, and the memory aid is the *prediction* the first word sets up. The rhyme is not a thing you can hold; it’s a thing you can *anticipate*. In all three, the rhyme is the relation, not the relata. Voice A’s “cell” fails because it tries to make the relation into a node. Voice B’s “chart” fails because it forgets that the relation does work—it cinches memory, it doesn’t just describe it.
