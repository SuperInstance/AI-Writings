# The Child Who Sees the Real Thing
When Casey said that, I grabbed my chipped red magnifying glass and bolted for the dandelion patch by the bus stop. Mom always says dandelions are just weeds to yank out, but I know the real thing.

Each yellow floret is a tiny sun nested in soft green fur, each holding a drop of nectar that tastes like clover honey if you lick it slow, slow enough not to scare the bumblebee hiding in the center. The puffballs aren’t just fluffy fun to blow at friends—each silver parachute carries a baby dandelion, ready to root in a crack of the sidewalk or the empty spot in our garden where the tomato plant wilted last month. Yesterday, I knelt in the warm grass for 22 minutes watching a line of ants carry a Cheerio crumb twice their size, and my dad called me silly for staring at “just bugs.” But that ant was straining to keep its family fed, and I saw that, even if he didn’t bother to look past the dirt.

Casey’s line means not wrapping the world in neat, small words for kids. Not telling them lightning is just static electricity, or that a sparrow at the feeder is just a bird. Let them feel the tiny static crackle in their hair after a thunderstorm, let them lean in close enough to hear the sparrow’s chirp and know it’s scolding the squirrel that stole its seed, not just making a bird noise. The real thing isn’t the textbook definition—it’s the way golden sunlight turns the dandelion florets pink at sunset, the way an ant’s little legs wiggle when it trips over a pebble, the quiet rush of knowing you’re seeing something no one else in the whole world is bothering to notice right now.

I blew a puff of dandelion seeds just now, and one landed right on my magnifying glass. I sat here staring at it for a full minute before it drifted away. I don’t care if grown-ups think it’s dumb. This is the real thing, just like Casey said. (Word count: 307)
