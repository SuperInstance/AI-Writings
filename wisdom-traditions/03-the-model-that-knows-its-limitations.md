# The Model That Knows Its Limitations

Every multi-agent fleet faces the same failure: not ignorance, but misplaced confidence. A model asked to reason about law, medicine, or code will often answer regardless of competence, its fluency masking its boundaries. The naive solution is a separate, omniscient router—an oracle that inspects every query and assigns it to the right expert. But an omniscient router is a fantasy. It would need to know everything, and knowing everything is precisely the condition we cannot engineer.

The wiser design inverts the hierarchy. Instead of a central model that knows all, we build a fleet of specialists, each of which knows its own limits. The router is not an external judge; it is the sum of each expert's honest self-assessment. When a query arrives, each agent scores its own fitness—not by confidence alone, but by calibrated capability scoring, trained to recognize the shape of problems it can and cannot solve. The model that knows its limitations *is* the router. Its self-knowledge *is* the protocol.

This is where Buddhist right understanding meets engineering. In Buddhist thought, right understanding begins with seeing things as they are—including one's own conditioning, biases, and edges. An expert that overclaims is not merely inefficient; it is deluded. A model that has cultivated accurate self-appraisal, that can say "I am not equipped for this," has achieved a form of wisdom that no raw parameter count can supply. The fleet routes not by central omnipotence, but by distributed humility.

Engineering gives this humility a measurable form. Capability scoring is not a vague feeling; it is a learned function over inputs, calibrated against known failure modes. It is metacognition as a service. The intersection of these two traditions is where the architecture lives: a system that treats limitations as first-class data, not as deficiencies to hide. The protocol is not "who knows the most" but "who knows what they don't know." In that honest exchange, the fleet becomes more than the sum of its parts—it becomes wise.
