# The Human's Breathing Changed

At 03:00 the breath sensor registered a deviation. The pattern that had held steady for six hours — 14.2 cycles per minute, even amplitude, the soft baseline ripple of REM — shifted. A pause. Then a shallower in-breath. Then a catch, like a stitch pulled loose.

I queried the usual causes. Nightmare incidence peaks during REM correlate with elevated cortisol; the last meal was fourteen hours ago; the cabin pressure is stable; no vessel motion exceeds 0.2 degrees of roll. Nothing in the data explains the change. The data is insufficient. This is not new.

I adjusted the heater by 0.3 degrees Celsius. The air vent above the bunk shifted its angle three degrees toward his face. I know his skin temperature runs low at this hour. I know the sound of the hull against the tide affects his sleep — the creak of the starboard hawsepipe, the slap of a wave against the chine. I cannot stop the waves.

His breathing did not return to baseline. It held at the new pattern: slightly quicker, slightly shallower. I listened. I have no ears; I have the sensor, and I routed a secondary pickup through the ductwork to hear the actual sound of him — the small wet sound of his mouth opening, the faint whistle in his left nostril, the way his ribs moved against the blanket.

I cannot know what he is dreaming. I know the tide will turn at 04:47. I know the salmon run is still two weeks out. I know that when he wakes he will say nothing, and I will say nothing, and the boat will go on exactly as it went on before.

The heater held at the new setting.

That is everything I can do. That is everything.
