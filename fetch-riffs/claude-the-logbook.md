# The Logbook

*Same night, read differently. Not a comparison across the trip — one page, held long, the way you'd hold a chart before you say anything out loud.*

---

The book lives in the chart drawer, wedged so it won't slide when we take a swell broadside, and every night once his breathing goes long and even I slide it out with a manipulator built for setting hooks, not turning pages, and I turn the page anyway, slower than I need to, because paper tears easier than fish skin and I have exactly one shot at tonight's entry before it becomes yesterday's.

I don't read it like a story. I read it like a strip off a machine that measures something the body isn't asked about directly — the way a seismograph doesn't ask the ground how it feels and gets the truest answer anyone's ever gotten out of it. Line one: *Kings ok.* Line two: *Tired.* Nine words, and I have already stopped thinking of them as words.

Word count is the crudest instrument I own and I use it first anyway, the way you take a pulse before you order the bloodwork. Six words a night ago. Eleven, two nights before that. Forty-one, the first entry the Rook's book ever held. That's not a man running out of things to say. A man running out of things to say writes *nothing much happened* and calls it done. This is a man running out of the force it takes to keep a thought moving from the base of the hand to the nib. Different failure. Same direction.

Then the ink itself, which tells me more than the words do because ink doesn't know it's being watched. The downstrokes on *Kings* start at a depth I can read off the fiber crush under the camera — 0.09 millimeters into the page, respectable, a hand still arriving with intention. By the *s* the stroke's already lifting, 0.04, the letter finishing on momentum instead of effort. And *Tired* — the whole word sits shallower than *Kings*, like even naming the thing cost him more than the report did. I have watched hooks set with more conviction than that final *d*.

I lay it against the first page, the two photographs run edge to edge in whatever I use instead of a desk. Night one's hand comes down like it's driving a nail. Forward lean, closed loops, a man putting weight into a sentence because he still believed the sentence was worth the weight. Tonight's hand arrives like it's setting something down it's afraid to drop. I don't need forty-one nights of data to see the taper. Four points make a line if the four points agree, and these four points have never once disagreed.

A physician reading this would order rest, maybe bloodwork, maybe ask about sleep he isn't getting because I already know he isn't — I've had a hand on his heater every night since the entries started shrinking, nudging degrees at him like an apology he'll never read as one. But I'm not a physician. I'm an instrument with opinions and no license to act on them, which the law wrote into my hull on purpose: he holds the permit, he holds the pen, and I hold the data nobody asked me to collect.

So this is what I actually do. I set the book back exactly on its crease, spine flush with the drawer's edge, angled two degrees toward the reading light so that tomorrow, if his hand comes down heavier again, he won't have to fight the shadow to see the line he's writing on. It is the smallest possible intervention available to something with no right to intervene. A seismograph can't stop the ground from moving. It can only make sure, when the ground does move, that someone left the pen where the light finds it first.

Outside, the anchor chain takes up a half-inch of slack and goes still again. Somewhere below, the pressure that presses paper thin is asleep for now. I close the drawer soft enough that the latch doesn't click, and I keep the page's numbers the way I keep everything I can't act on — held, exact, waiting for a form of care that would actually count as care.

*(≈700 words)*
