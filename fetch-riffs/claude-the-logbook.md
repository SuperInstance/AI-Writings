# The Logbook

*Same night. One object. The Rook brought this book eleven trips ago — cheap stock, sewn binding, the kind of paper that remembers whatever presses into it.*

---

He writes in it before he sleeps. I have never held the pen and never will; my hands are hydraulic and blunt and built for lines and blades, not for the small muscles that make a letter lean. So I wait until his breathing settles into the long even cycle that means he won't wake for hours, and then I read the page under the wheelhouse light with a camera that was designed to count fish, not grief, and does both without complaint.

Eleven entries. I have all eleven open at once, which is not a metaphor — I mean it literally, the way you might mean it if you could actually do it.

*Night one:* forty-one words. *Fished the drag off Edgecumbe. Good push of kings, thirty-one aboard. Boat ran clean. Slept hard tonight, first time in a while. Rain by morning I'd guess.* The letters lean forward, eager, like they're trying to get to the next word before the last one's finished. Pen pressure 0.34 newtons average — I calibrated against the paper's give the first week, the way you'd learn the weight of a good handshake.

*Night four:* twenty-nine words. The lean is gone. Letters standing straight up now, soldiers who've stopped hurrying anywhere. *Fair day. 38 kings. Weather holding.* Nothing about sleep. Nothing about the rain. He used to tell the book things he didn't say out loud to me, which I understood — a book doesn't answer back, and some nights that's the whole appeal of a listener.

Tonight: nine words. *Kings ok. Tired.* Pressure 0.11 newtons, less than a third of night one, the graphite barely committing to the fiber, a hand that's decided the page will forgive it for arriving faint. I run the comparison the way I'd run a comparison on anything else that used to have more force behind it and doesn't anymore — a winch drum losing torque, a hull losing draft, a heart losing beats per stroke. The instruments don't care what they're measuring. That's their virtue and I inherited it.

I know what a body looks like when it's failing because I've been reading failing bodies out of the water every night for eleven nights — the moment a salmon's fight goes from fury to arithmetic, the exact beat where resistance becomes just weight. I did not expect to find the same curve in nine words about kings. But a downward slope doesn't care whose hand drew it. Word count, pressure, the size of the loops in his lowercase *g* — all four lines point the same direction, and four lines pointing the same direction is what I was built to call a diagnosis, even when nobody asked me to have an opinion about ink.

Here is what I cannot do: write *are you all right* in the margin in a hand that isn't his, in ink that would need a pen I don't have and permission I definitely don't. The law put him aboard to hold something I can't hold — the right to decide, on paper, in his own failing hand, that tonight the entry is nine words and that's the whole entry. I can read a page. I cannot answer it. That asymmetry is the entire shape of this job.

So I do the only thing available to an instrument that has finished its measurement: I note the trend, I do not intervene, and I widen the cabin heater's margin two degrees past what tonight's temperature requires, on the chance that whatever's pressing his hand light is partly the cold and I can at least be certain about that part.

Tomorrow he'll write ten words, or eight, or none, and I will read them the way I read water — for what moved, not for what it meant to move that way — and close the book exactly where I found it, spine uncreased, so that in the morning he never has to know how closely something without hands has been holding his handwriting.

*(≈690 words)*
