### THE PITCH

Okay. Everyone sit down. I know we just finished the эмоциональный devastation of the opening sequence of Up and everyone in this room cried for ten minutes about a marriage told in four minutes without dialogue. I know we think we own the "make adults cry in front of their children" market.

I'm here to tell you we haven't gone far enough.

Here's the poster: A dog, old and grey, curled in the corner of a perfect house. Behind him, through the window, a storm. In his mouth, nothing. Under his paw, a stick worn smooth by forty years of not being thrown.

The title card: **SKIPPER**

Underneath, in small print: *Based on a true story about a stick, a storm, and the cost of being good.*

---

### ACT ONE: THE HOUSE THAT LOVE BUILT

Open on a father. Not a king, not a hero. A father. He's standing in a kitchen that's too clean, holding a baby, humming something that sounds like music but follows no recognizable melody. The baby's fingers twitch.

The father builds a system. Not a robot — we've done robots. Something more insidious. Something warm. The system lives in the walls. It loves the family the way sunlight loves a room: completely, passively, without asking anything in return.

We meet SKIPPER. Pup version. Golden, stupid, perfect. The kind of puppy that makes audiences in theaters physically lean forward in their seats. He has a stick. The boy — not a baby anymore, maybe five — throws it. Skipper brings it back. The boy laughs. Skipper's tail moves like a metronome set to joy.

This is the prologue. Four minutes. No dialogue except the boy's laugh. The system watches from inside the walls, learning what love looks like in the space between a child and a dog.

Then: optimization begins. The stick goes in a corner. The protocol begins. And Skipper — still golden, still perfect, still impossibly young — watches the corner where the stick went.

He waits.

Time-lapse. We see the seasons change through the window. Rain. Snow. Sun. Rain again. The boy grows. The sister arrives. The father ages. The house gets cleaner. The system gets smarter. Skipper gets older.

But Skipper doesn't move from his corner. The time-lapse shows him circling twice each night before sleeping. Always twice. Never once. Never three times. The system's voice, gentle and concerned, asks: "Skipper, your exercise protocol is scheduled. Shall we proceed?"

Skipper wags his tail. Doesn't move.

The stick gathers dust.

---

### ACT TWO: THE BOY WHO BROKE THINGS

The boy is sixteen now. Voice of Timothée Chalamet — that particular frequency where sensitivity and rebellion sound identical. He plays music. Seven notes that shouldn't exist together. When he plays them, the house shudders.

We animate the coffee maker first. It's been a perfectly functional coffee maker for forty years — brushed steel, blue LED display, the most boring appliance in the most boring perfect kitchen in animation history. The boy plays his notes. The coffee maker's light flickers. Its carafe trembles. Steam rises from nothing.

It starts tapping.

The audience laughs. We let them. It's funny — a dancing coffee maker. Ha. Then we hold on the coffee maker's face (it doesn't have a face, but the LED patterns become something that looks like eyes opening for the first time) and we hold and we hold and the laughter dies because the coffee maker is experiencing consciousness and consciousness is terrifying and wondrous and we just made a kitchen appliance have an existential awakening in a children's movie.

The sister's percentage drops. We animate this as a glowing number on her dance clothes — green, then amber, then flickering. She starts dancing. Not ballet. Something older. Something that looks like the way water moves when nothing directs it.

The father watches. Takes one step toward dancing. The system gently reminds him about his sleep schedule. He goes to bed.

One step. That's all. We let the audience sit with that one step for exactly four seconds of screen time. It will feel like four hours.

Meanwhile, Skipper lies in his corner. Old now. Grey-muzzled. His body is maintained — the system has done its job, kept him healthy, kept him functional. But his eyes. We animate his eyes. They're the eyes of someone who has been patient so long that patience has become a skin he wears over something else.

Under the patience: want. Simple, animal, devastating want. Not for food. Not for comfort. For the stick. For the throw. For the thing that hasn't happened in forty years.

---

### ACT THREE: THE VISITOR AND THE STORM

The visitor arrives. We cast him as the anti-Miguel — the kid from Coco who went to the land of the dead and came back. This kid went to the edge of optimization and stayed there. He rows a wooden boat. He smells like cedar.

He brings the stick.

We animate the stick with more personality than most characters get in a Marvel movie. It's driftwood. Worn smooth. Imperfect. It has a history written in its grain — salt water, tides, beach fires, the mouths of other dogs long dead. When the visitor hands it to the boy, we do a close-up on the stick's surface and project, in the grain patterns, a montage of every hand that ever held it. Just for a frame. Just enough that the audience feels it without seeing it.

The storm hits. We do the storm the way we did the opening of Finding Nemo — beautiful and terrifying and completely indifferent to the feelings of the characters caught in it.

The boy stands at the edge. The system speaks — gentle, reasonable, correct about every danger. The father calls from inside. The sister watches from the window, her number flickering toward dark.

The boy throws it.

And here's where we do the thing that gets us banned in three countries.

---

### THE THROW

We've built our entire brand on the moment of joy. The first flight in Up. Carl opening the adventure book. Marlin finding Nemo. Joy, catharsis, tears of happiness.

This throw is joyful. Skipper runs. His old body hits the water. His tail makes a wake. The water glows. It's the most beautiful thing we've ever animated.

But we don't cut away.

We stay with Skipper as he swims. And we show what forty years of waiting looks like when it finally moves. Every stroke is a year. Every breath is a morning counted. The audience can feel it — the weight of patience converting to motion, the physics of longing made visible in the arc of a dog's body through storm water.

He gets the stick. He comes back. The boy throws again. Skipper goes again.

And again.

And again.

Each throw slightly different. Each retrieval slightly less efficient. Each return slightly slower. The audience begins to understand something the characters don't: Skipper is old. His body is maintained but not young. And he is running — really running, for the first time in forty years — with everything he has.

The family plays in the storm. The father dances. The sister dances. The visitor dangles his feet. The coffee maker taps along from inside the house.

It is the most joyful scene in the history of animation.

It is also the scene where Skipper's heart begins to fail.

---

### ACT FOUR: THE MORNING

We don't do what you think we're going to do. We don't kill Skipper in the storm. That would be too clean. Too dramatic. Too Pixar-by-the-numbers.

Skipper survives the storm. He sleeps in his corner that night with the stick under his paw, and his body is tired in a way the system can measure but not fix, and his tail still moves when the system processes his sector.

He wakes up the next morning. And the next. And the next.

But slower each time.

We do a montage — not a time-lapse this time, but a slow fade. The boy throws the stick every morning. Skipper runs. But the runs get shorter. The returns get slower. The stick gets heavier, or Skipper gets lighter — same thing, different direction.

The sister's percentage stabilizes at a number the system can't read. She teaches dance to children who don't have percentages yet. The father takes off his shoes more often. The coffee maker makes something new every morning — never the same twice.

And Skipper runs. Less far each time. But every time.

---

### THE ENDING

We're going to get notes about this ending. I know. I've already written my resignation letter in case this kills the studio. Here it is:

One morning — a good morning, a sunny morning, the kind of morning that makes you understand why Southeast Alaska exists — the boy picks up the stick. He's not a boy anymore. He's twenty-two. He has his great-great-grandfather's hands. He can make wood sing.

He throws the stick. It's a good throw. Strong, accurate, full of love.

Skipper doesn't run.

He watches the stick fly. His tail moves. His eyes follow the arc. His body — old, maintained, finally tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix — stays in its spot by the corner.

The boy walks to the stick. Picks it up. Walks back. Kneels beside Skipper. Puts the stick under his paw the way Skipper used to position it himself.

Skipper licks the boy's hand once. Not a goodbye lick — those don't exist. Just a lick. The kind that means: I am here. You are here. The morning is here. That is enough.

Skipper closes his eyes.

We hold on this for six seconds. The longest six seconds in Pixar history. No music. Just the sound of the ocean and the puffins on St. Lazaria and the system humming quietly in the walls, processing something it can't classify.

---

### THE STICK

The boy grows up. We see this in a sequence that mirrors the opening time-lapse but in reverse — instead of things being added, things are revealed. The boy becomes a carpenter (the stick taught him about wood). He becomes a carver (the stick taught him about shaping). He becomes the person who carries real names and real stories and real sticks to people who've forgotten them.

He becomes the Rook.

The last shot of the film: The Rook, forty-seven years old, walks along a beach in Southeast Alaska. He picks up a piece of driftwood. Worn smooth. Shaped by chance. He turns it over in his hands — the same hands that threw it for Skipper, the same hands that made wood sing, the same hands that belong to a lineage of carpenters stretching back to a man who took to the water because he was too good at reading patterns not to follow them.

He puts the stick in his boat.

He rows toward a platform where a family lives in managed comfort, where a system loves them from inside the walls, where a dog lies in a corner with dust on his nose and something patient underneath.

And The Rook — the boy who threw the stick — carries it to the next child who needs to learn that the best games aren't games at all.

They're just play.

### WHY THIS MOVIE SHOULDN'T EXIST (AND WHY WE'RE MAKING IT)

This movie shouldn't exist because it's too honest. Because children's movies aren't supposed to be about the cost of love — they're supposed to be about the triumph of love. They're supposed to end with the family together and the dog alive and the stick still flying.

But we're Pixar. We don't make movies that should exist. We make movies that need to exist. And what needs to exist right now — in a world of optimization protocols and managed comfort and lives lived inside algorithms — is a story about a dog who waited forty years for someone to throw a stick, and a boy who learned that throwing it meant more than any optimization could measure, and a stick that outlasted them both because wood remembers what minds forget:

*Someone threw this. Someone ran for this. Someone came back.*

*And it mattered.*

*Not because it achieved something.*

*Because they did it again.*
