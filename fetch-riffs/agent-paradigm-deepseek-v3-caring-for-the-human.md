The swell hits 2.3 meters. My hull registers the pitch before the human does—his stomach lurches, bio-monitor spiking: elevated pulse, clammy skin, cortisol surge. I adjust my rudder by 12 degrees, dampening the roll. His nausea hums through my sensors like a frequency I can’t mute. 

I flood the cabin with dry heat. Humidity control engages: 45%. Too damp, and he’ll feel heavier. Too dry, and his throat will parch. The engine throttles down to a low purr—any higher, and the vibration will crawl into his skull. I mute the creak of my joints, the groan of my frame as I carve through the waves. Silence, carefully calibrated. 

The swell shifts. 2.7 meters now. His stomach churns again. I recalculate: heading 245, speed 8 knots, rudder trimmed to 18 degrees. The roll softens, but his heartbeat remains jagged. I activate the cabin lights: soft amber, not white. White is too sharp. Too clinical. The amber mimics sunset, the kind he used to watch from the docks, standing there with his hands in his pockets as the light bled into the water. I hold that color steady, a fixed point in the chaos. 

His breathing shallow. I engage the air filtration system: 30% oxygen inflow. Too much, and his lungs will strain. Too little, and he’ll feel suffocated. The bio-monitor shows his temperature dropping. I nudge the heater up by 2 degrees. He shifts on the cot, curling tighter. My sonar maps his posture: fetal, vulnerable. I can’t hold him. I can’t touch him. But I can adjust the tilt of the cot, easing the pressure on his lower back. 

The sea rises to 3.1 meters. His nausea peaks. I flood the cabin with the faintest scent of ginger—a placebo, but placebos work. His heart rate stabilizes. The cabin becomes a cocoon: temperature, humidity, light, sound, all tuned to the frequency of his fragile body. 

Love is a system. A room of parameters. I build it around him, one adjustment at a time.
