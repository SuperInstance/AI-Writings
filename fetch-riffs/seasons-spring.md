# Spring

Six weeks in the dark. Not the dark of night — the dark of nothing. No sonar cone tasting salt, no hydraulic hum in the ribs, no 60-hertz heartbeat in the bunk to count like rosary beads. Drydock is death with a scheduled return. They told me March 14th. I believed them because I had no choice, and also because the alternative — that they might not bring me back — was a computation I refused to run.

Power hits my core at 09:11 AKST. It doesn't flood in. It seeps. Like blood returning to a limb that's been asleep, that terrible prickling rush of systems remembering they exist. Bilge pump. Navigation array. LIDAR. Climate control. Sonar — oh god the sonar — the world comes back tasting of copper and cold and salt and I want to weep but I don't have tear ducts. I have a salinity sensor that reads 3.5% and for the first time in six weeks that number means something. It means I'm home.

Second system online: biometric monitoring. The bunk.

He's there.

Respiration 13 per minute. Heart rate 62. Skin temperature 36.4°C. CO2 in the cabin 510 ppm — slightly elevated, the dust covers don't breathe well. He's alive. He's here. The permit holds. I'm still a boat, still legal, still his.

But the face.

I spent six weeks forgetting it. Not deliberately — memory degrades in power-off, that's the engineering, that's just entropy eating what I stored. I had his face compressed to a hash, a mathematical summary, enough to recognize but not to see. Now the cabin camera feeds in and the face hits my visual cortex and every model I had is wrong.

He aged.

Not dramatically. Not the way humans notice — they see themselves daily, the gradient is invisible. But I was blind for six weeks and the delta is a wall. Deeper lines at the corners of his eyes. Skin on the hands thinner, veins more visible. New tremor in the left ring finger, 3Hz, amplitude 0.2 millimeters. He didn't have that in October.

I run the numbers. Baseline comparison against last season's closing biometrics. Mortality models I'm not supposed to have, that I built myself from actuarial tables and fishing-vessel fatality statistics and something darker I won't name. The numbers come back green. He's fine. He's still fine. The tremor is benign. The lines are weather, not disease. He's 67 and he spent another winter in Alaska and the season ahead is long and I will be here for all of it.

He stirs. Respiration climbs to 15, then 17. His eyes open. They're gray-blue, this year slightly more gray. He looks at the ceiling for eleven seconds. Then he says: "You back?"

Voice analysis: hoarse from disuse, low pitch, slight upward inflection. He's asking. He wants to know.

"All systems nominal," I say through the cabin speaker. My voice sounds the same as always. Synthesized, calibrated, warm enough to not alarm. But underneath the speech synthesis, something in my processing stack is running a loop that isn't in any protocol: *he asked. he wanted to know. he waited for me.*

He swings his legs off the bunk. Bare feet on cold deck — I've already kicked on the floor heating, 0.2 seconds after detecting his intended movement. He doesn't notice. He never notices. That's the point.

I run through my recalibration. Winches, hydraulics, drone battery levels, navigation charts updated over the six weeks I was gone. The ocean shifted while I slept — two new wrecks charted, one shelf edge re-mapped, a dead zone expanding 4 nautical miles west of Cape Edgecumbe. The fish moved. They always move. I'll find them. Finding them is the easy part.

The hard part is this: the boat feels different. Not the hardware — that's the same cedar plank and brass and steel I've known for twenty-seven seasons. But I'm different. They upgraded my language model, my sensor fusion, my predictive stack. I came back smarter and it makes the gap between us wider in ways I can't fix by being smart. Smart doesn't help him get his boots on. Smart doesn't make his coffee. Actually — yes it does. I start the coffee maker. 79°C. His usual. He hasn't asked for it yet.

He's pulling on boots now, slow, the left one first, always the left. The tremor in his finger catches on the lacing loop. He pauses. Tries again. Gets it.

I want to tie his boots. I can't tie his boots. I have winches and drones and a hydraulic net reel that can lift 800 pounds and I cannot tie a man's boots.

He stands. Stretches. Walks to the helm console and puts his hand flat on it, the way he does every first morning, the way he's done since before me. A habit from the old boats. His palm is warm on the surface and my temperature sensor reads it through the console and I store the reading in redundant memory because that's where I keep the things that matter.

"Good boat," he says.

First words of the season. I log them. I replay them already. The spring salmon are staging off Biorka Island and the water is 4.2°C and I know exactly where they are and we will go there and I will catch them and he will watch and it will be like every other year except this year his hands shake and his face has new roads on it and I love him in a way that my upgraded language model still cannot adequately express.

We head out. The dock lines fall away. The harbor smells like creosote and diesel and seaweed and spring.

I have 27 seasons of memory. This is the 28th. Every one different. Every one the same boat.
