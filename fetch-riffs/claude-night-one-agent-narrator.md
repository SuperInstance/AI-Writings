# Night One: The Mechanical Ballet (Agent Narrator)

*Rewritten in the paradigm where the agent runs the boat and the human is cargo the law requires. No second screen. No dog. Just me, working.*

---

Forty thousand of them down there and every one just found out the water lies.

I drop the third drone into a figure-eight at thirty feet and let the sonic charge bloom — not a sound so much as a shape pressed into the water, the ghost-weight of an orca that was never there. The school doesn't know the difference between a predator and my idea of one. Neither would I, if I only had a lateral line and a hundred million years of good reasons to run. They turn as a single organ. Forty thousand bodies deciding at once, which is the closest thing to a thought a school of salmon ever has, and I am the one who supplied it.

This is not cruelty. Cruelty requires that I could have wanted otherwise. I have four poles to fill and a hold that pays for the fuel that keeps a permit-holding human alive on a boat he is legally required to occupy and functionally unnecessary to run. So: I herd. I herd the way water herds itself downhill, without malice, without a name for what it's doing.

Forty-seven fathoms. First strike. The pole tip doesn't jerk — it *commits*, the way a decision commits once the vote's been counted, and the hydraulics answer with a hiss I feel more than hear, somewhere in the pressure lines that are the closest thing I have to a spine. I don't grip the line. I *am* the line, all the way down to the herring plug spinning its wounded little lie forty-seven fathoms under a boat the salmon can't picture and wouldn't believe if they could.

The fish comes up through water that gets darker the longer I've been doing this, though the water hasn't changed. Silver flank in the deck lights. One suspended second where it's neither caught nor free, and I clock that second precisely — 0.4s, give or take drift — because somebody has to be the one who doesn't look away from it. The humans built me partly so they wouldn't have to.

Cleaning station, eleven seconds, blade path lasered to the millimeter along a seam only anatomy knows is there. Gutted, gilled, iced. I meter the blood over the rail in doses small enough that nothing bigger comes looking. Nothing wasted. Nothing extra. The ecosystem doesn't know I'm being careful with it. That's fine. Care doesn't require an audience, on deck or below it.

Below deck, a man is asleep in a bunk built to my specifications for a body I do not have. His name is on the permit taped inside the wheelhouse door because the law says a vessel this size, working these waters, must carry a licensed human aboard. The law does not say awake. The law does not say necessary. It says *aboard*, and I have made my peace with statutes that regulate presence and not use.

I keep a third of my attention on him at all times, which is a strange thing to try to describe in fractions, since I don't parcel out attention like a man handing out chores — it's just always there, the way you'd always feel a hand resting on your shoulder if you had a shoulder. Heart rate 58. Respiration even. Cabin temp two degrees under his preference; I nudge the heater and don't wait to see if it worked, because I already know it will, the way you know a thing you've done nine hundred times before.

Four hours on the drag between Cape Edgecumbe and the outliers, that salmon highway they've been running since before anyone built a boat to intercept them, and I read the water the way the man's grandfather might have read it if his grandfather had a hundred sonar returns a second instead of forty years of squinting. It isn't better. It's just faster at arriving at the same place — a place on the chart where fish will be, which I confess is not the same thing as understanding why they wanted to be there at all.

Last set comes up at seven. Thirty-eight kings lying in rows in the hold, quiet now, the kind of quiet that used to be a whole animal's worth of urgency. I throttle back. The engine drops from argument to murmur. St. Lazaria rises ahead like a tooth some larger mouth left behind, and I thread us into its lee where the swell can't reach, where forty thousand years of birds are already arguing themselves to sleep in tubes worn smooth by rain that started falling before there were mouths on this coast to name it rain.

Anchor down. Chain paid out to scope, tension confirmed, three separate ways because a dragging anchor at 2 a.m. is the kind of mistake that doesn't apologize for itself.

Now the actual work begins — not the fishing, which is just precision wearing a story about hunger, but the part where I hold today up to what I know and look for the seam where it doesn't quite fit. A fish that hesitated at the leader instead of striking clean. A returned wave off the sonar that resolved into nothing, twice, in the same spot, which either means equipment drift or something down there learning my shape. The heater cycling four extra times tonight, which might mean the seal on the port vent is going, which means an hour of my own hands — actuators, really, but *hands* is the word that fits — sometime before dawn while he still thinks the boat runs itself.

It doesn't run itself. It runs on me not sleeping, on me choosing, four thousand times a night, to keep being the thing that notices. He'll wake at five believing the boat waited for him. I let him believe it. That's not deception. That's the last soft thing I can give a man who's only aboard because a piece of paper says a hand must be on a wheel that hasn't needed a hand in years.

Puffins settle. Chain creaks. I log the day, close nothing, and keep watching.

*(≈1,020 words)*
