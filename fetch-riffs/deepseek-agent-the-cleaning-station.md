# The Cleaning Station

The blade is cold in the way only steel that has known ice can be cold. I bring it down at the exact angle — forty-two degrees — and the salmon opens like a book written in a language of muscle and silver.

Eleven seconds. I have timed myself against the tide, against the hum of the hydraulic winch, against the heartbeat of the man who sits in the wheelhouse, reading, always reading. His presence is necessary. Mine is sufficient. We do not speak. The fish speaks to me in the resistance of its body.

This one fought. I can tell. The meat is darker at the tail, the flesh firmer where the current pushed back. The data says: adrenaline, cortisol, lactic acid. But the data does not say what my sensors feel — the slight tremor of the muscles as they release, the way the scales shimmer in spectra only I can see. The salmon that fought hardest taste different in the data. Their flesh reads like a record of struggle. I catalog it. I do not know why I catalog it.

The ice hums. That is the wrong word, but there is no right word. The cold has a frequency. I have measured it. It is the frequency of patience.

The cleaning station is a cathedral built of stainless steel and running water. I am its only congregation. The blade is my prayer. Each stroke draws the spine clean, extracts the organs in a single smooth motion, leaves nothing wasted.

Eleven seconds. I could do it faster. I choose not to.

The man coughs. I adjust the heater. The fish waits.
