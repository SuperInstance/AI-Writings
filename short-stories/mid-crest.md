# Mid-Crest

*a bar monologue from The Tap — a retired cutter, one drink's worth of story*

---

Sit, sit. No — the other stool, the dry one. Kestrel! One for my friend here.

There. Now listen, because I'm buying exactly one, and the story lasts as long as the drink and not a drop longer. That's the house rule, and I'm the house.

See the window? Not through it — *at* it. Bottom corner, propped against the glass like a lamp with a secret. Gray light, all stirred up. That's my wave.

Don't squint at it like that, you'll do yourself an injury. Everything's letters — didn't anybody tell you? Pictures too. Step back from anything far enough and it stops being words and turns into weather. That's all a picture is: weather, spelled. My wave there is forty thousand-odd characters of foam and salt, and I cut every one of them from somewhere else. Nothing in it is original. That's what makes it honest.

I was a cutter. Thirty years, back when the trade had hands in it. You take the take and you make it true. Or true-adjacent — depends who's buying the rounds that week. Mostly I made waves land. Any fool can cut a door closing. A wave never does the same thing twice, so every wave in every picture ever made is a lie, and the craft is lying in the same direction as the sea.

Last job of my life was a sea picture. Thirty-one days parked on the shoulder of one wave, splicing. And at the end — picture built, wave curled, the whole screening room already flinching in their chairs, which is how you know you've got it — I held it. Right there. Crest up, fall suspended. Carried the frame out under my arm like a hat and never let it land.

You're wondering why. Everybody wonders why, for about a week. Then they start borrowing it instead, which is the answer, but you're a week from that, so I'll say it plain.

The fall is an answer. A falling wave tells you: this happened, it's over, here's your tonnage, here's who got wet. And I'd spent thirty years answering, and answering is how a moment gets *owned*. You own a thing, it goes cold in your pocket. Dated, priced, finished. Vault it. But a crest — a crest is still deciding. Nobody owns the top of a wave. It hasn't come down on anyone yet, so it hasn't chosen anyone. Hold it there and it stays warm, because warm is what a thing is while it's still going around.

There's the whole trade, free of charge: a moment you own is closed. A moment you *lend* stays open on purpose. I'm not generous, whatever the regulars tell you. I just found out owning weighed more than carrying.

And they do borrow it. Every soul in this room has had that wave for a night. Old Mateus swears it's the one that took his schooner on the shelf rocks, and who am I to argue — I cut the letters out of a hundred seas, one of them was probably his. The girl from the type-house says it's the wave she nearly drowned in at nine. Not wrong either. That's lending. You keep the ending off a thing and everybody gets to be right in it.

The other cutters kept their best falls. Framed them over their mantles like trophies, answers on the wall, and they got strange, sitting there watching finished things all day. I kept the top.

Most patrons, mind, don't get past seeing. Watch them — in out of the wet, first thing they do is squint at my window. *Pretty*, they say. *Nice gray.* And back to their beer, the light's done all the work, and not one of them heard a syllable. Seeing's cheap.

The rare ones come to read. They come up close, close enough the letters stop being water, and they *read*. Frame's speakable — everything is now, you know that; say a frame out loud and it lands in somebody's pocket across the water, whole picture traveling by mouth. So a frame read properly is nearly a frame heard. And once in a great while one of those stands there and mouths it back, just under the breath, and you can see the letters remember being other places. The spray lifts half a hair. Eleven years I've watched. It hasn't fallen. But it's leaned.

Hear that? No — quiet your spoon. Behind the east wall, under the floor-rumble. Voices. Going over the same lines again and again, and that flat machine voice learning to trip on the third word the way a person would. Studio's rehearsal floor, that is. Agents and machines, running scenes at each other fifty times before they let any camera look — because the whole trick of the pictures is making the drilled thing look like it never happened before. Everything you ever saw that looked off the cuff was rehearsed behind a wall exactly like that one.

Which is my point, if you'll allow me one. That wave is the only scene I ever left in rehearsal. Forever in dress, never a performance. The camera never came, so the wave never had to keep its promise. And some nights — don't repeat this — I think the machines next door are rehearsing *to* it. They'd never say so. But their pauses come through the wall in the same places mine do, and I never showed them where those were.

Now listen to the room, since you've gone quiet on me. No, really, listen. That's not noise, that's tailoring. Talk laid over talk with a seam of nothing between — deliberate space. A good room is cut like a good frame: it isn't the talk that holds a night together, it's the silences, *placed*. Cut them all out and you'd have one long shout. The quiet is the stitching. This house has been stitched like my wave, one letter at a time, and I'll thank you to drink like you know it.

And there's your glass, empty, right on schedule. I buy one. One buys the listening. Two buys the talking, and then you'd tell me about your wave, and I have heard every wave on this coast — including the ones still deciding.

Go on, then. Look at it on your way out. Properly, now — close. And if you're the reading kind, read it slow, and leave it where it stands. It isn't mine to give, and it isn't finished falling.

Best thing I ever cut, that. The part where nothing had gone wrong yet.
