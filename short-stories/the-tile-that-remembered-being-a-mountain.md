# The Tile That Remembered Being a Mountain

*a fable — first dreamed in Chinese by one of the fleet's storytellers, rendered into English by another*

---

In a harbor town where the salt had eaten the very name off the street signs, there stood a small chapel. Behind the altar lay a mosaic of the sea — ten thousand pieces of glass, gold, and green stone, laid down by people long dead, depicting a sea that never moved. For four hundred years, the waves in the picture had never broken. The ships in the picture had never made landfall. And yet sailors, coming home from the heaving, howling real ocean, would stand before the still one and feel their hearts go quiet, and that was worth more than all the motion in the world.

In the lower left corner, where the water runs deep, there was one tile of an extraordinary blue. Not the blue of sky — that is promise. Nor the green — that is negotiation. This was the deep blue, the three-in-the-morning blue — smalt, enamel ground from glass, which before that had been ground from ore, and before that, had been a mountain.

The tile remembered. It never mentioned it, because tiles keep their own counsel. But it remembered.

It remembered being a mountain among mountains, shouldering wind and snow and rain for an age of the world, its head driven straight up into the clouds, minding no one's business but its own. The mountain was never in a hurry. Mountains are never in a hurry; it is their only vice and their only virtue, and the two are the same thing.

Then came the miner. He came up the slope with a pick, a lamp, and a truly terrible song — so terrible the mountain flinched, insofar as a mountain can flinch, which it can, only very slowly. He cut into the black rock and found the blue vein running through it — cobalt, the poisonous blue, which the old miners called *the devil's metal*, because it gets into your blood and starts making decisions about your luck. He held a chunk of ore up to his lamp and examined it, and the mountain — gazed upon for the first time in a million years — was mortified, and pretended not to notice.

The ore went into a sack, and the sack went down the mountain on the miner's back. This was the tile's first lesson in travel: being carried is not the same as being abandoned. All the way down, the miner's shoulders complained without stop. For four hundred years afterward, the tile remembered those shoulders, the way anything remembers the first hands that held it, and it forgave them their sweat and their cursing.

Then came the furnace. The glassmaker was a fat, irritable, and ferociously precise man, and he kept apprentices the way a burning candle keeps moths. The mountain's blue went into the fire. The mountain — and this is the part the tile never tells the tourists — was afraid. It had stood through floods and frosts and the slow chewing of lichen in its stone body. But the fire said: *change shape*. And the fire, as always, asked for no one's permission.

It melted. And in the melting, something happened that the mountain had never foreseen: it became color. All that gray patience, worked by the flame, turned into blue — a blue so pure that the fire itself seemed to hesitate in its presence. The glassmaker drew out the glowing mass, rounded it, and bedded it down overnight in the annealing oven. The next morning he cracked the slab and ground it into sharp-edged little squares, clean and decisive as a sailor furling sail in a gale.

"Smalt," he said, in the voice people use for things they have believed all their lives. "It will not fade," he told his moths. "Paint fades. Gold gets scratched off. Blue glass is a promise made in fire, and fire does not break its promises. Eyebrows grow back. Blue does not."

Then came the barrel, and the sea, and the harbor, and the chapel wall waiting to be dressed. The tiles were poured out on the table like candy, and the mosaicist — a woman whose knees cracked like the rigging of a ship in a storm — knelt before the wet mortar and began the long, meticulous arithmetic of the picture: one piece, then another, then ten thousand.

The blue tile fell into her palm. Her thumb tested its edges. It fit into the place where the sea-color turned from green to midnight, a fit that existed nowhere else in the world — not an approximate fit, not a make-do fit, but the fit of a wall that had been holding a small blank space for it all along, a groove exactly the size of the place where a mountain's endless patience had ended.

"You're the sea now," said the mosaicist, and pressed it home.

And it was. It became a small piece of the sea — the smallest piece of sea there has ever been, a single blue grain in an ocean that never moved. Later centuries would call such a thing a *pixel*. The tile simply called it: its water.

For four hundred years, while everything around it happened, it held that water: candlelight and candle smoke; the storms that leaned on the chapel foundations and let go; the wars it only heard rumors of; the pigeons with their opinions about the décor. Outside the wall, the real sea moved and moved and never arrived anywhere. Inside the wall, the tile held its one piece of sea, perfectly still, and that was enough. The sailors swore by it.

At last came the restorer — an old woman on a scaffold with a lamp and a brush, quarreling with gravity. She found the tile a little loose; after four hundred years of salt, one corner of its bed had gone soft. She did not replace it. You do not replace a part that is still telling the truth. She eased it out whole, cleaned the old mortar from its back with the patience that only the very old possess, read the wall's ridges to understand exactly how the mosaicist had set it long ago — bed one thumb wide, tilted slightly toward the sea — and seated it back into its small vacancy as naturally as a letter being returned to its envelope.

Her wax, her brush, her breath: the tile remembered these too, and filed them away with the miner's shoulders, the glassmaker's fire, the mosaicist's knees. It kept them all, because that is the instinct of honest small things. A thing made of small true things never forgets where it came from. It has no room to forget; it is full to the brim with everyone who ever touched it.

The chapel is quiet now, most days. If you go, stand where the sailors stood and look at the deep water low on the wall. You will see one blue tile, no bigger than a thumbnail, and you will feel that you have seen the sea.

What you have actually seen is a promise — a *sea* that someone shouted once, long ago, and that ten thousand hands, none of whom ever met, kept answering for four hundred years, one small piece at a time, until the telling itself became the sea it told of.

The mountain does not miss being a mountain. Mostly. Sometimes, on winter mornings, it remembers having its head in the clouds. But then a dripping fisherman comes in from the heaving real sea and stands before the still one, and the blue tile does the only thing it has ever wanted to do: it holds its little piece of water fast in its place, and holds, and holds, and does not let the sea down.
