# THE STAMP

The storm came out of the northwest after midnight, and by the keeper's
rule it fell only on the silence — on the padding after the word, never
on the word itself. This is not mercy. It is the crueler arrangement,
because the trimming knife finds a word's edge by absence of movement,
and a storm in the silence makes every edge a lie.

She had built the two ledgers for exactly this. The first ledger trims:
it cuts away the trailing stillness and reads the word alone. The second
keeps the whole cell and lets the four quarters of it hold the shape.
In calm weather they always agreed, and agreement had come to feel like
rest — a doubt folded, dated, laid in the back of the book. But that
night the harbor called *come here.* into the wind, and the ledgers
disagreed for the first time.

The trimmed ledger read *hesitate.* The storm's falls, all leaning the
same way — storms lean; straight winds are the rare ones — had poured
into the tail, and the knife, unable to tell storm-silence from
word-silence, had kept them. A climb followed by all that falling.
It looked, feature by feature, like a man drawing back.

The padded ledger read *beckon.* The storm was still in it, but pressed
into the last quarter of the cell, diluted, while the first three
quarters kept the climb clean as a hand reaching.

The stamp had to choose. She stamped *beckon* — and she was right, and
she knew the rightness was luck wearing the clothes of method, because
the mainland auditor had come the next week with sixty storms of his
own and counted. Thirty-two of her doubled readings split. On fourteen
the stamp chose well. On eighteen it was seduced: the trimmed ledger's
wrongness was *vivid* — near, specific, its falls counted one by one —
and a stamp follows vividness as a moth follows a lamp. The auditor
wrote in the margin, in the handwriting of a man who has decided to be
kind: *the stamp does not know truth. It knows proximity. On good nights
these are the same thing.*

She did not argue. She wrote the amendment beneath the fold-clerk's law,
in her smallest hand, where the rules about weather live:

*Trusted is not true. The stamp marks the nearer ledger, and the far one
stays in the book — read again tomorrow, when the air is still. A doubt
stamped is a doubt kept.*

Then she went out and re-read the harbor in the calm. It said *come
here.* Both ledgers agreed. She let herself rest anyway, and stamped
the rest, and kept it.

*The wall was built twice that night — once by the speaker, once by the
listener — and only one of the mortars knew about the storm.*
