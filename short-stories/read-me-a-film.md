# Read Me a Film

*a letter from 2031*

---

*Late September, 2031*

Dear June,

Tonight you did it again — the thing I still haven't gotten used to, though I pretend I have. You were telling me about the film you watched at school, and instead of describing it, you handed it to me. The scene itself, sitting in the middle of your message like a shell carried up from the beach. You copied a moment out of a movie the way I used to copy a sentence out of a book, and then you wrote "that part," as if that settled everything. And it did.

I want to tell you what it was like before, and I want to do it honestly, which means telling you that some of it was better, and that I haven't entirely forgiven the world for changing it.

When I was your age, a moving picture was sealed. A fish behind glass. You could press your face to it — in the cinema dark you practically did — but you could never reach in. The film moved at its own speed, indifferent to you. If a moment was beautiful, it left. That was the deal. You watched it go the way you watch weather pass, and if you wanted it again you had to do the work of remembering, and remembering is a poor copy — but oh, it was ours.

I remember walking home from the cinema in the dark with my mother, both of us silent, running the second half of the movie in our heads. There was no going back to check. Nothing could be paused, nothing lifted out, nothing smoothed. Whatever happened on the screen stayed happened. The past was unrepeatable on purpose, and that made it heavy, and the heaviness is what made it feel like something. You could only be a witness. Witnesses don't get to touch.

You will never know that dark. The room going black, three hundred strangers breathing together, and then — image. Not yours. Nobody's. Simply given. I loved being a stranger to it. I didn't know that's what I loved, but that's what it was: the image was a guest, and guests leave, and leaving is how a visit becomes a memory.

You, on the other hand, read films. That's the only way I can say it. You read them the way I learned to read books, and I was present for the moment it began, so let me tell you what I saw, because you were too young to notice it happening to you.

You were four. There was a scene you loved — the boy on the roof, the red kite — and you asked for it so many times I lost count. Then one afternoon you sent it to your grandmother. Not a description of it. *It.* You copied it into a message the way I might have copied out a poem, and she called me half-laughing, half-crying, because in her whole life a scene was something you could only describe, and describing was always a small funeral — the scene died somewhere on the way. You handed it to her alive. That was the day I understood the glass was gone.

And you hold frames. You catch one the way you catch a beetle: both hands cupped so it can't escape, then you look — really look — at the legs of it, the shine, the tiny architecture, and when you're done you let it go and run to find another. No one in the cinema dark ever held anything. We only witnessed. But I have watched you look at a single frame longer than anyone I ever sat beside in a theater looked at an entire film.

You see the seams, too. "It's made of little pictures," you told me once, delighted, as if you'd found out the ocean is made of drops. And it's true, and it was always true, but our pictures kept it a secret. Ours pretended to be windows onto somewhere else. Yours admit they're mosaics — pieces, fitted by hands, touchable. There's an honesty in that that I admire even while it breaks something in me slightly. It's strange to be nostalgic for a lie. But the lie was beautiful: that light could arrive whole, like weather, like grace.

Here is what I miss, said plainly: I miss being a stranger to the image. I miss that it could refuse me. I miss the walk home, the dark, the going-over. When a scene couldn't be kept, keeping it meant loving it — memory was the only copy, and memory is where love does its paperwork. Nothing is lost now, and I have noticed, quietly, that when nothing is lost, less is carried.

But here is what I would not trade, and I want you to know that I know: what looks to me like strangeness is, to you, simply literacy. Watching you hold that frame tonight, I recognized my own face — six years old at the kitchen table, sounding out my first sentence alone, the day the marks stopped being marks and became meaning. You have that with light. The whole moving world is legible to you, and you read it for pleasure, the way I read paperbacks by flashlight under the blanket. They teach it at your school now — reading — and I have to laugh at the homework I couldn't have imagined, and at you complaining about it like it's nothing, which is exactly how it should be.

So tonight I'm asking you for something, and I want you to understand what I'm really asking. Read me a film. The way my mother read to me — slowly, out loud, pointing at the parts, patient with my questions. You be the reader for a while and I'll be the stranger, and maybe that's what inheritance looks like now: it runs both directions, and nobody has to mind.

The fish is out of the glass, June. It turns out it was never a fish. I don't have the word for what it is. You will, though. You already do.

I love you. Save me a frame of something.

— Dad
