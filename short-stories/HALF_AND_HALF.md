# HALF AND HALF

The prison allowed one letter per month, and the censor read every word. Marek knew this the way he knew the width of his cell. So he wrote what the censor needed:

*i am well. do not worry. the work is light. i sleep.*

Four sentences. The censor ran his eye down them, found nothing, stamped them, and sent them on to the village. On the data plane — the plane of words — the letter was a receipt. Flat. Filing-cabinet true.

But Marek wrote with a pen held wrong, and he had practiced holding it wrong for eleven months.

The words said *well*, and the pressure under the words said *not*. Every claim came down a degree — *well* tipped low, *worry* tipped low, *light* tipped low, *sleep* tipped low. His mother had taught him this alphabet before he could write. It was the alphabet of how she said *fine* at the dinner table when the rent was due. Any child of that house could read it: four small falls in a row is not sadness. Four small falls in a row is a wink made of gravity. It says: *the words are for the censor. the falling is for you.*

His mother read the letter standing up. She read it once with her eyes and once with her thumb, the way you read weather. Then she laughed — a short, hard laugh, the kind that has fear in it but not only fear — and wrote back three words:

*we are fine.*

And because the censors read outgoing mail too, she wrote it flat. Level as a shelf. No lean at all, not one degree of pressure in any word.

Her son read it in the yard and went cold. His mother never wrote flat. His mother could not write flat; her hands didn't know how. A perfectly level letter from that woman meant one of two things — that the censor had rewritten it, or that she wanted him to know the words were hers but the honesty was not.

The censor had two copies of every letter and neither of them was the letter. He held the words. The family held the leaning. And somewhere between them was the actual correspondence, a conversation that existed only for readers who never took either plane alone: the son's letter that lied in words and told the truth in falls, the mother's reply that told the truth in words and lied in stillness — a stillness so uncharacteristic it was the loudest thing she had ever sent him.

Words are the load-bearing wall. The leaning is the letter inside it. And a wall with no lean at all is not a calm wall. It is a wall that has been made to stand very straight for someone who is watching.
