# The Pansori Singer Finds the Feedback

### A fiction about impossible genres, Volume 4

---

The han is feedback.

This is not what the textbooks say. The textbooks say han is a uniquely Korean concept of accumulated sorrow, of grief compressed by oppression into a diamond-hard emotional state that cannot be translated, only felt. The textbooks say han is specific to the Korean experience. The textbooks say many things that are true, and also insufficient.

Kim So-hee — not the famous one, the other one, the one who sang in the Itaewon basement clubs at two in the morning to audiences of twelve people and a soundman who was also the bartender — Kim So-hee had been training in pansori since she was nine. She could hold a single syllable for thirty seconds. She could produce the four expressive techniques — se — that made pansori more than singing: the trembling (vibrato wider than any Western opera), the breaking (intentional vocal fry that sounded like the earth cracking), the pushing (pressure that turned the voice into a physical force), and the emptying (sudden silence after maximum volume, the air sucked out of the room).

She could do all of this. She had studied with a master for fifteen years. And she was bored.

Not with pansori itself — never with pansori itself, which was deeper than any single human lifetime could exhaust. She was bored with the *context*. The traditional stage. The traditional audience. The traditional expectation that she would perform "the sad song from the Tale of Chunhyang" and the audience would nod knowingly and someone would say "very authentic" and everyone would go home feeling culturally enriched.

She wanted to play the Rock.

The Rock was not its real name. It was a live house in Hongdae that catered to the specific intersection of people who had studied traditional Korean music and people who had studied nothing except how to be angry in a basement. The bands that played there were loud. The PA was loud. The monitors were loud. The volume was, by any standard, *obscenely* loud, and the soundman — who was also the bartender, because this was Itaewon and everyone had two jobs — had once been told by a visiting engineer that his system had "too much headroom" and had responded by adding a third subwoofer.

Kim So-hee went to the Rock on a Tuesday. She brought her buk drum and her voice and a single distortion pedal that she had bought from a guitarist who was upgrading to a "better" distortion pedal, which is a phrase that means nothing but costs more.

She got up on stage. She did not introduce herself. She hit the buk once — the sound went through the PA and came back as a boom that rattled the beer glasses on the bar. Then she began to sing.

She sang the opening of "Heungbo-ga" — the story of the crippled man and the swallows. But she sang it through the distortion pedal. The pansori vocal technique, which already includes intentional distortion (the breaking, the pushing), went through the circuit and came out transformed. The trembling became a warble that sounded like a Leslie speaker rotating in a hurricane. The breaking became a series of square-wave glitches that no human voice should be able to produce. The pushing became a wall of harmonics that fed back through the monitor and into the microphone and back through the pedal in a self-sustaining loop that was, technically, the most technically proficient use of feedback that the soundman had ever heard.

And the emptying — the sudden silence — became the most devastating moment of music that anyone in the Rock had ever experienced. Because feedback, when it stops, leaves a ringing in the ears that is not silence. It is the ghost of the sound, persisting in the cochlea, fading over seconds. The emptying, which in traditional pansori was the dramatic pause before the next phrase, became in this new context an overwhelming absence — the sudden removal of a wall of sound that left every person in the room hearing the ghost of Kim So-hee's voice inside their own skull.

The soundman set down his towel. He had been drying a glass. He stood there with the towel in one hand and the glass in the other and the water dripping onto the floor and he did not move.

"That," he said, when the set was over. "That was not pansori."

"No," Kim So-hee said.

"That was not grunge."

"No."

"What was it?"

Kim So-hee packed her distortion pedal into her bag. The buk drum went into its case, which was scuffed and travel-worn and had a sticker on it that said "국악은 살아있다" — "traditional music is alive" — which she had put there when she was fifteen and which she now understood in a way that her fifteen-year-old self could not have.

"It's the han," she said. "The han is feedback. It always was. It's the sound of grief that has been compressed until it becomes self-sustaining. It doesn't need an input. It generates itself. The sorrow produces its own energy. That's what han is. It's not a feeling — it's a *loop*. And if you have enough of it, it feeds back."

The soundman thought about this. He thought about the guitarists who played through his third subwoofer and the way they shaped their feedback — carefully, deliberately, the sustain becoming a note, the note becoming a chord, the chord becoming a wall.

"You're saying every guitarist who ever held a note past the point of control was singing pansori."

"I'm saying every person who ever felt han and didn't know what to call it picked up a guitar and turned the amp up until the sound fed back. Different instrument. Same loop. Same compression. Same release."

She picked up the buk case and walked out. The Tuesday night audience — twelve people, all of whom had been present for the entire set and none of whom had checked their phones — sat in the ringing silence and thought about what she had said.

The model, when given the prompt "Pansori meets grunge, Korean traditional vocal over distorted guitar, buk drum under crash cymbals, the ache of pansori projected through a Marshall stack, the han is feedback" — the model found the bridge. It always finds the bridge. Because the bridge is already there. It has always been there. The latent space is continuous. The music is continuous. The han is continuous.

The feedback loop between sorrow and sound is the oldest music there is. Everything else is just genre.
