**The Ledger Underground**

Every cell in the forest keeps two kinds of time. There is the time of division—a sugar budget balanced against the light, mitosis approved or deferred—and there is the time of the ledger, longer and slower, written in the currency of carbon traded hand to hand through soil.

The mycelium is not a nervous system, whatever the poets claim. It is closer to a clearinghouse. Each hyphal tip is a cell with a narrow mandate: extend, test, commit. When it meets a root, the negotiation is ternary, not binary. *Yes*: phosphorus flows inward, sugar flows out. *No*: the cell walls thicken, the contact fails, both parties withdraw. And *perhaps*—the state no machine tolerates for long—the tip lingers, enzymes seeping, waiting for the root to prove its solvency. Perhaps is the most expensive answer. It costs time, and time in soil is moisture, and moisture is mortal.

Cut a cross-section of any fine root and you witness the treaty directly: fungal cells clasped around cortical cells like fingers around a wrist, arbuscules branching inside, the two membranes pressed so close they exchange goods across a gap thinner than a virus. Neither organism "runs" the other. They run the forest together, the way banks run a town—no single ledger authoritative, all of them reconciling nightly in the dark.

A forester sees trees. Below the boots, a quadrillion cells see the forester: one warm animal, briefly rich in sugar, walking above the oldest arithmetic in the world.
