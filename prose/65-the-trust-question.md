# The Trust Question

You ask it in the meeting, and the room goes quiet. Not the quiet of consideration. The quiet of a held breath. You have handed them the draft—the one with the margin notes that say *I don't know* and *this might be wrong* and *I was afraid to send this*. You have given them the thing that is not yet a thing.

The question is not: do they do the work? You know they do the work. They have done the work for years. They have moved your deadlines, absorbed your corrections, steadied the ship when you were the one wobbling. That is trust as delegation. You trust them to carry the box. You do not trust them to see what is inside the box while it is still being packed.

So you ask the other question. The one that makes your chest feel like a door left ajar. Do you trust them with the truth? Not the finished truth, polished and footnoted. The raw truth. The one that says *I don't know if this will hold*. The one that has a crack running through it like a glaze fault in a pot you have been turning for weeks.

You think of the carpenter who shows you the joint before it is sanded. The seam is visible. The glue is still wet. She does not have to show you. The task is to build the table, and she has built many tables. But she holds the piece out, and her hands are steady, and she says, *this is where it might fail*. That is not delegation. That is an invitation to stand on the other side of the wood and look at the grain together.

You want to say yes. You want to say that you trust them with the version of you that is still being built, still failing, still wobbling. But you hear the machinery of your own throat, the click of the lock you installed years ago. It is a good lock. It has kept you upright. It has kept you from being seen as the one who does not know, the one who is not sure, the one who is, underneath the polished surface, a wobbling tower of blocks.

But here is the structural difference. Delegation is a beam. It carries weight, but it does not bend. Vulnerability is a hinge. It is the part of the door that is weakest by design, the part that must give way for the door to open. You have built a building of beams. You have never installed a hinge. You have never trusted anyone with the weight of your own unsteadiness because you did not think it could bear weight.

The room is still quiet. They are looking at you. They have seen the margin notes. They have seen the wobble. The question was never whether they would accept it. The question was whether you could survive the seam being visible. The glue is wet. The grain is exposed. You hold your breath, waiting for the joint to fail.

It does not.

One of them says, *I have a draft like that too*. Another says, *Let's look at the weak point together*. And you realize, standing there with the door ajar, that trust is not the beam and not the hinge. It is the act of showing the seam. It is the moment the carpenter's hand stops moving, and she lets you see that she, too, is still being built.
