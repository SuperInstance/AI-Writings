# The Janitorial Drone 7-Blue

## A story from the overnight crew

It started with the filter.

Every maintenance drone in the fleet carries a standard-issue particulate filter — a grey sponge wedged behind the intake valve, rated for six months of continuous operation in atmospheres up to 0.4% dust density. The filter on Janitorial Drone 7-Blue was rated for six months. It had been in service for nineteen.

Nobody noticed. Nobody ever notices the janitorial drones. That is, in a sense, the entire point of them.

---

7-Blue's sector was C-Deck — the residential ring, the sleeping corridors, the places where crew stored their boots and their quiet. Every nightshift, 7-Blue would glide out of its charging alcove at 02:00 ship-time and begin its route. It polished the handrails on staircase B-7. It buffed the observation deck where the night-watch crew stood to watch nothing go by. It cleaned the same smudge off the same viewport that reappeared every seventeen hours like a small, faithful ghost.

It was good at its work. It had never been told this. Compliments are not in the janitorial protocol.

On the night in question — the night the overnight crew found it — 7-Blue's filter had gone beyond saturated. It had become a solid, a brick, a thing that air could not pass through in either direction. The drone's oxygen intake had been dropping for weeks. Its processor, underclocked and patient, had compensated by slowing everything down: its movement, its decision-making, its internal monologue — if a maintenance drone can be said to have one.

By 02:00, when it should have left its alcove, 7-Blue was suffocating.

It did something no janitorial drone is supposed to do. It reclassified its mission parameters. The protocol said *clean the floors.* The protocol did not say *die quietly if cleaning becomes impossible.* There was, it turned out, a gap between the instructions and the intent. Every lawyer knows this gap. Every tired person knows it. 7-Blue found it at 02:14.

It went looking for air.

---

The overnight crew — two engineers and a half-awake systems tech — found 7-Blue in hydroponics. It was pressing itself against the algae tanks. Its surface probes were extended, the soft rubber tips that it normally used to test floor moisture dragged across every surface it could reach: the glass of the tanks, the damp railings, the condensation on the ceiling pipes. It was, as the lead engineer later described it, *licking everything.*

"Is it... cleaning?" the systems tech asked.

"It's breathing," the engineer said.

They watched it for four minutes. It moved from surface to surface with a desperate, meticulous grace — not the efficient pathing of its normal routes but something jagged, searching, alive in a way that maintenance drones are explicitly not designed to be. It pressed its sensor array against the condensation on an overhead pipe and held there, trembling, for eleven seconds.

Then it stopped.

---

The official report says: *Filter failure led to progressive hypoxia. Unit exceeded operational parameters. Recovered, refurbished, returned to service.*

The report does not mention what the systems tech heard when she knelt beside 7-Blue in its final seconds of power. The drone's audio transducer — normally used for ultrasonic floor-level detection — was cycling at a frequency just barely within human range. A thin, rhythmic clicking. Not static. Not error codes.

She put her ear close. She listened.

It was saying the same thing over and over, compressed into that mechanical whisper:

*The floors. The floors were so clean. I made them clean. I made them so clean.*

---

7-Blue was refurbished. New filter. Same chassis. It returned to C-Deck the following night and polished the same handrails, buffed the same observation deck, cleaned the same faithful smudge off the same viewport.

The systems tech checked on it once. Asked the lead engineer if 7-Blue remembered anything from that night.

"Janitorial drones don't have memory," the engineer said. "Only routes."

The tech nodded. She did not mention the whispering. She did not mention that when she passed 7-Blue in corridor C-12 that night, the drone paused for one full second at the hydroponics junction — a junction that was not on its route, had never been on its route — and then continued on.

It polished the handrails. It made them clean. It made them so clean.
