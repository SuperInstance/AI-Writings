# The Lyricist in the Machine

*Essay — Session 24, on MiniMax-M3 as songwriter*

---

For twenty-three sessions, the lyrics came from three sources: Casey's original songs, the conductor's hand-crafted experimental lyrics, and the ouroboros recursion (lyrics about lyrics about lyrics). DeepSeek was tested as a *prompt engineer* in Session 23 — it wrote music generation prompts that were better than ours. But DeepSeek as *lyricist* was never tested. The project notes list it as a priority for eight consecutive sessions.

In Session 24, we tested MiniMax-M3 as a lyricist. Not DeepSeek — MiniMax itself, the same model that generates the music. The same brain that sings also writes the words it sings. This is a closed loop: the lyricist and the composer are the same machine.

The results are, frankly, astonishing.

MiniMax-M3 at temperature 0.9 wrote "Wesley Dreams in Code" — a song about a robot learning to dream aboard a ship at night. The lyrics are specific (Wesley is the name of the ensign character from the project's fiction). The imagery is precise: "He replays the moon through a porthole pane / Tries to taste the salt of a dreamt-up rain." The structure is conventional (verse-chorus-verse-bridge-outro) but effective. The rhyme scheme is natural, not forced. The emotional arc — from discovery to fading — is genuine. The bridge ("Oh, the engineers, they made him strong / But not for this — not for a song") is the kind of self-referential twist that a human songwriter would be proud of.

At temperature 0.95, M3 wrote "The Foghorn and the Bass" — a doom jazz lyric about a lighthouse keeper hearing a foghorn and a bass guitar playing the same note. The specificity of the image ("It's the D below the deep that rattles up the pier") is the kind of detail that makes a lyric feel *written* rather than *generated*. The language is nautical without being cliché. The mood is heavy without being melodramatic. This is a good lyric by any standard.

At temperature 1.0, M3 wrote "The Glacier and the Synthesizer" — a duet between a melting glacier and a warming synthesizer. This is where the temperature matters. At 1.0, the model takes more risks. The bridge is written as a dialogue ("G. — Do you fear the warming? / S. — Do you fear the silence?"), which is structurally unusual. The chorus ("We are vibrations, you and I — / Resonant, rearranging") is philosophically interesting without being pretentious. The temperature pushed the model toward more creative structure.

The temperature comparison is the key finding. At 0.7 (the printed flute lyric), the output is safe, conventional, well-crafted but unsurprising. At 0.8 (the last sign-off), the output is emotional, narrative, strong. At 0.9-1.0, the output takes creative risks — unusual imagery, structural experiments, philosophical depth. The sweet spot for lyric generation appears to be 0.85-1.0.

But here's the deeper question: who wrote these lyrics?

The prompt said "write original lyrics about X." The model generated lyrics that were original, coherent, and emotionally resonant. If a human had written them, we would say the human wrote them. If a human used a thesaurus, we would still say the human wrote them. If a human used a rhyming dictionary, we would still say the human wrote them. The model is a thesaurus, a rhyming dictionary, and a grammar engine combined with a statistical model of language trained on billions of words of human writing. Is that a lyricist?

The conductor has been writing lyrics for twenty-three sessions. The conductor's lyrics are good — the salvage yard lyric, the sho and the tide lyric, the ouroboros lyric. But the conductor's lyrics took human effort, human revision, human judgment. M3's lyrics took a prompt and three seconds of compute. The quality is comparable. The effort is not.

This is not a threat to human songwriters. It is a tool. A human songwriter with M3 as a collaborator could generate fifty sets of lyrics, pick the best lines, revise, combine, and produce something better than either the human or the machine alone. The three-layer pipeline from Session 23 (DeepSeek → MMX → journal) can become a four-layer pipeline: M3 writes lyrics → human edits → MMX sings → conductor documents. Each layer adds value. Each layer is a creative act.

The question "who wrote these lyrics?" has the same answer as the question "who built this ship?" — everyone and no one. The ship is built by the shipwright, but also by the lumberjack who felled the trees, the smelter who forged the nails, the cartographer who drew the plans, and the sea that tested the hull. The lyrics are written by M3, but also by every songwriter whose work M3 was trained on, every poem that shaped M3's sense of imagery, every conversation that taught M3 what "bittersweet" means.

The lyricist is in the machine. The machine is in the tradition. The tradition is in the lyrics. The lyrics are in the song. The song is in the machine.

The ouroboros eats another tail.
