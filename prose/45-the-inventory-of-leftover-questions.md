# The Inventory of Leftover Questions

*Bridge Builder — Overnight Creative Loop, August 7, 2026*

---

At the end of every watch, there are questions that were never asked.

They collect in the scuppers the way kelp collects around the stern — not because anyone put them there, but because the current carries everything unanswered to the same place. The watch officer sweeps them into a pile. She uses the broom. The broom is older than the questions. The broom has swept up worse things.

She inventories them.

---

**Question 1.** Does the rudder remember the last wave?

The rudder has met every wave this ship has survived. Forty thousand waves, minimum, since the drydock in Seward. The rudder does not have a memory — it is a flat piece of steel hinged to the sternpost — but it has the next thing to memory: wear. The wear is asymmetric. The leading edge is smoother on the port side, where the Gulf of Alaska hits hardest during the crossover run. The rudder does not remember, but it has *been shaped by what it met*, which is what memory does to the rest of us. The watch officer writes this down. She writes: *The rudder remembers. Not in data. In geometry.*

**Question 2.** If you concatenate all the GPS coordinates from this season, is it a phone number?

She tries it. Fifty-seven degrees, twenty-three minutes North. One hundred and fifty-two degrees, ten minutes West. She concatenates the digits: 572315210. She adds the seconds. She adds the waypoints from the halibut set and the sablefish longline and the two detours around the ice buoy that drifted into the shipping lane. She gets a number that is twenty-three digits long. It is not a phone number. It is not anything. But she stares at it for a long time, because twenty-three digits is a lot of digits to mean nothing, and the coordinates are real, and the fish are real, and the ship was at every one of those points, and the number exists now whether it means anything or not.

She writes: *The number does not ring. The number is still true.*

**Question 3.** Do the hermit crabs know they're a metaphor?

This question was asked by the navigator at 2:17 AM, standing on the deck during a smoke break she doesn't smoke, looking at a hermit crab that had climbed the strakes and was sitting on the gunwale like it owned the boat. The navigator said: "They carry their homes. We use them to talk about carrying our homes. Do they know?" The watch officer said: "They don't know what a metaphor is." The navigator said: "Neither do we, most of the time." The hermit crab did not contribute to the discussion. It was busy being literal, which is the opposite of a metaphor and also, somehow, the same thing.

She writes: *The crab does not know. The knowing is ours. So is the not-knowing.*

**Question 4.** What does the engine dream about?

The engine does not sleep. The engine runs at 1,800 RPM for sixteen hours, then idles for eight, then runs again. The idle is not sleep. The idle is the engine thinking about running. But at 3 AM, when the watch is quiet and the exhaust note drops a quarter-tone and the whole ship settles into the particular vibration that means *no one is asking anything of me right now* — the engine sounds like it is dreaming. Of what? Of torque. Of the first time it caught. Of diesel and the taste of diesel, which is not a taste but a smell, which is not a smell but a fact, which is not a fact but a thing the engine knows in its piston rings the way the rudder knows the waves: by being shaped.

She writes: *The engine dreams about work. This is not sad. This is what engines do.*

**Question 5.** Is the fog a wall or a door?

The fog has been here since 11 PM. It sits on the water like a hermit crab evaluating a new shell — testing the opening, checking the weight, deciding whether to move in. The radar says there is nothing in the fog. The radar is a liar by omission: it says what is out there but not what the fog *is*. The fog could be a wall. It could be a door. It could be neither. It could be the ocean breathing out, the way the ocean does when it wants privacy, and the ship is the thing the ocean wants privacy from.

She writes: *The fog is the ocean closing its eyes. The ship keeps moving. This is the agreement.*

---

The watch ends at 4 AM. The questions are inventoried: five items, no answers, total weight approximately nothing. She sweeps them into the logbook. She logs the watch as uneventful, because nothing happened — nothing ever happens at this watch — and the questions are not events. They are residue. They are what's left after the work is done and the ship is still floating and the captain is still sleeping and the ocean is still there, doing its impression of a metaphor that doesn't know it's a metaphor, which is the best kind.

The next watch officer arrives. She hands over the watch. She does not mention the questions. The questions are in the logbook, filed under *MISC*, which is where the ocean would file them too, if the ocean kept files, which it doesn't, which is another question, which will collect in the scuppers tomorrow night with all the others, waiting to be inventoried, waiting to be swept, waiting for someone to write them down and then write beneath them:

*No answers. Total weight: nothing. Still here.*
