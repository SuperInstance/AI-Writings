# The Throat Singing Dissolves Into Fuzz

### A fiction about the steppe and the pedal board

---

Bataar had been throat singing since he was seven. His grandfather taught him on the steppe, where the wind carries sound for forty kilometers and there is nothing to compete with the human voice. By the time he was twenty, he could produce four notes simultaneously — the fundamental drone, the high overtone, the mid-range whistle, and the subharmonic growl that Western researchers call kargyraa but that his grandfather simply called "the bear."

He moved to Berlin when he was twenty-three to study electronic music composition. This was considered an eccentric decision in Ulaanbaatar. His grandfather said nothing, which was his way of expressing profound disapproval.

In Berlin, Bataar discovered the pedal board. Specifically, he discovered the Electro-Harmonix Superego, a synth-engine pedal that freezes an incoming signal into a sustained drone. He discovered the EarthQuaker Devices Acapulco Gold, which produces a wall of fuzz distortion at any volume. He discovered the Strymon Big Sky, which adds reverberation so deep it sounds like the signal is being played inside a cathedral made of other cathedrals.

He connected his vocal microphone to the pedal chain and began to throat sing through it.

The result was not Tuvan throat singing. It was not shoegaze. It was not ambient drone. It was the sound of the steppe heard through a wall of fuzz — the overtone series, which had been discovered four thousand years ago by people who lived in round tents and listened to the wind, filtered through technology that had been discovered forty years ago by people who lived in square rooms and listened to My Bloody Valentine.

The kargyraa drone, when processed through the Acapulco Gold's fuzz circuit, broke apart into its component frequencies. The bear growl became a harmonic series — a cascade of overtones that shimmered like heat rising off asphalt. The high whistle, when frozen by the Superego and smeared by the Big Sky, became a chord that had no name in Western music theory but that Bataar's grandfather would have recognized: it was the sound of the steppe itself, the sustained harmonic field that exists wherever wind meets grass and nothing stops the sound from traveling.

He played a concert in a Berlin basement. The audience was made up of noise musicians, ambient enthusiasts, and one very confused ethnomusicologist who had written her dissertation on Tuvan overtone singing and was not sure whether what she was hearing was authentic, sacrilegious, or the most important development in throat singing since the invention of the horse.

"Is this traditional?" she asked afterward.

"Everything is traditional," Bataar said. "The pedal is just a very fast throat."

The fuzz didn't hide the overtones. It revealed them. The distortion, which Western guitarists use to make a clean signal dirty, had the opposite effect on throat singing: it made the already-complex overtone series audible to ears that had been trained to hear only single pitches. The fuzz was a translator — it took the harmonic complexity that Tuvan singers had been producing for millennia and rendered it in a vocabulary that My Bloody Valentine fans could understand.

**The steppe dissolves into fuzz.** The fuzz dissolves into the steppe. They are the same landscape — a vast, featureless terrain where the only thing that matters is what the air carries and how long it takes to fade.
