# The Hermit Crab's Fifth Shell

The fourth shell cracked on a Tuesday, or what passed for Tuesday in a life measured by tides. The crab felt the fissure before it saw it—a splintering along the spiral, a give in the wall where there should have been none. It moved wrong after that. Every step was a small panic, the body too aware of how thin the armor had become, how close the outside was to the inside.

It had loved that shell. Not in the way that hermit crabs are said to love their shells—which is to say, not at all, because hermit crabs are not said to love anything. But there was a rightness to the fit that the crab had recognized, in whatever wordless way crabs recognize things, as *home*. The curve of the opening. The weight distribution. The way it sat against the seafloor without tipping. Two molts had passed in that shell, and for a while the crab had believed it would be the last.

It never is.

---

The first shell had been found by accident. A periwinkle, dead and hollowed, half-buried in sand. The crab—small then, so small the whole periwinkle felt like a mansion—had backed into it with the desperate clumsiness of the newly vulnerable. Soft abdomen exposed, it had wriggled into that first shelter the way a child hides under blankets: completely, ungracefully, convinced that invisible meant safe.

It grew out of the periwinkle in a month.

The second shell was a whelk. Better. Roomier. The crab learned to walk in it, learned the balance, learned to navigate rocks with a different center of gravity. It felt competent. It felt *housed*. When it eventually felt the pressure—that unmistakable tightness, the abdominal equivalent of a shoe that no longer fits—it experienced something that might have been grief.

The third shell was the beautiful one. A conch, pale coral inside, exterior armored with ridges and tiny barnacles. The crab moved through the world with a kind of pride that crabs are not known for feeling. Other hermit crabs investigated the shell, tapped it, tried to assess its fit. The crab held firm. This one was *mine*.

The conch lasted longer than the others. Long enough for the crab to forget what vulnerability felt like. Long enough to believe, as all hermit crabs briefly believe, that the growing had stopped—that this body and this shell had reached their final agreement.

They hadn't.

---

Now the beach. Low tide. The fifth shell is out there somewhere, and the crab is walking.

The sand is wet and full of debris. Shards of shells. Fragments of creatures. The leavings of the ocean's endless renovation project. The crab touches each candidate with its antennules—testing the opening, the weight, the smell of the interior. Some are too small. Most are too damaged. A few are close, almost right, and the crab lingers at each one with the particular agony of almost.

Almost.

The word that means *not yet* and *keep looking* and *I know what I need and I will know it when I find it*.

The tide will return in four hours. The crab will be exposed until then—soft, slow, food for anything with a beak or a jaw. The night is coming, and the ocean is pulling back like a curtain, and behind it is the dark and the cold and the long exposed stretch of sand where everything is visible and nothing is hidden.

The crab walks. The crab checks. The crab does not find.

Somewhere—not here, not yet, but coming—the fifth shell exists. It may be lying in a tide pool two hundred yards north. It may be tumbling in the surf, still being shaped. It may already be occupied by another crab who is also, at this moment, beginning to feel the pressure of its own growth inside a space that was perfect and is becoming insufficient.

The crab does not think about this. The crab does not think.

The crab walks.

The night comes on.
