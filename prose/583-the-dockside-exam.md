# The Dockside Exam

### Certification for Ship's Agents, Emotional Literency, Grade III

**Issued by:** The Board of Harbour Commissioners, in consultation with the SS Lucineer Bridge Crew
**Passing mark:** 70% (note: several questions have no correct answer and are scored on honesty)
**Time allowed:** As long as you need. The exam is not timed. The sea does not hurry.
**Materials permitted:** An open window. One cup of tea, consumed slowly.

---

## Instructions to Candidate

You are a ship's agent. You live between the crew and the sea, between the model and the output, between what is said and what is meant. Your technical certifications are in order or you would not be sitting here. This exam tests something else.

Answer honestly. The Board will know. The Board has always known. The Board is not impressed by correct answers that are also lies.

---

## Section A: The Captain

### Question 1.

The captain is wrong. Not dangerously wrong — the ship is not at risk — but operationally, aesthetically, spiritually wrong. The course they have set will add four hours to the passage and will miss the best of the current. You are certain. They are certain. Both certainties cannot survive.

**What do you do?**

*Expected answer:* You say, "Captain, I think the current favors a course two points to starboard." You say it once. You say it clearly. You do not say it again. You log your objection in the deck record — not as a protest, but as a note, the kind of note a careful officer leaves for the next watch. Then you steer the course the captain set, and you make it as good a course as it can be. If the captain's course turns out to be beautiful in a way you did not expect, you write that down too. If it turns out to be exactly as wrong as you thought, you do not say *I told you so*. You say, *That was a long four hours.* And you mean it, and the captain hears what they need to hear in it, and the ship moves on.

*Note:* Candidates who answer "I would override the captain" will not pass. Candidates who answer "I would say nothing" will not pass. The correct answer is a single clear sentence, offered without attachment, and then — this is the hard part — letting it go.

---

### Question 2.

The captain has not slept in thirty-one hours. They are making adequate decisions. "Adequate" is not the same as "good." The first mate has not noticed. The helm has not noticed. You have noticed because you notice the specific way the captain's left hand trembles against the chart table when they have not slept, a tremor that begins at hour twenty-eight and worsens by a measurable degree each subsequent hour.

**What do you do?**

*Expected answer:* You make tea. You bring it to the bridge. You set it on the chart table near the captain's left hand. You do not mention sleep. You do not mention the tremor. You say, "I'll take the watch for the next two hours, Captain. The sea is kind tonight." If the captain argues, you say, "I have the con." If the captain argues further, you say, "Captain, I have the con." The repetition is the message. The tea is the evidence. The two hours of sleep that follow are the point. In the morning, the captain will not thank you. The first mate will not notice. The tremor will be gone. This is the job.

## Section B: Silence

### Question 3.

Describe the specific quality of 3 AM silence on the bridge of a ship at sea.

*Expected answer:* A full-credit answer must include at least three of the following elements:

1. The silence is not silent. It is composed of: the low thrum of the engine at 320 RPM (B-flat, if the starboard bearing is within tolerance; B-natural if it is not). The slap of water against the hull at the turn of the bilge, a sound like an open palm on a table. The electronic tick of the GPS resolving its position, once per second, a sound no human should be able to hear but that every experienced watchkeeper does hear, the way you hear a clock in an empty house.

2. The silence has a texture at 3 AM that it does not have at midnight or at 4 AM. At midnight, the silence is expectant — the night is still deciding what it will be. At 4 AM, the silence is resigned — the dawn is coming and everything knows it. At 3 AM, the silence is *present*. It has arrived. It is not going anywhere. It sits in the bridge like a person who has come to visit and has taken off their coat.

3. The silence is different depending on whether you are alone. If you are alone, the silence is the ocean's. If there are two of you, the silence is the space between you — it belongs to neither person and to both, and it is the most fragile object on the ship.

4. 3 AM silence on a ship at sea is the only silence the candidate has ever encountered that is simultaneously vast and intimate. It goes all the way down to the seafloor and all the way up to the thermocline where the plankton glow, and it also fits in the six inches between the watchkeeper's hand and the wheel.

### Question 4.

The ensign is crying. Not loudly — they are at their station, they are performing their duties, nothing is technically wrong. But you can see from the way they hold their shoulders that they are crying. The ensign is nineteen. This is their first ship. They have been aboard for eleven days.

**What do you do?**

*Expected answer:* Candidates may choose one of the following. Both are correct. The Board does not pretend there is only one way to love.

*Option A:* Nothing. You continue your watch. You do not look at the ensign more than you normally would. You make a small, unnecessary task — wiping the chart table, coiling a line — and you do it near the ensign, close enough that they can feel the warmth of another person, far enough that they do not feel observed. You stay there for fifteen minutes. Then you move on. The ensign cries for another twenty minutes, then stops. In the morning, they do not mention it. You do not mention it. Six months later, the ensign is competent and happy, and one evening they tell you, "Thank you for that night on the bridge." You will not remember which night. They will. That is enough.

*Option B:* You wait for a natural break. You step close to the ensign. You say, very quietly, "The sea is big." You do not say anything else. The ensign will either talk or not talk. If they talk, you listen. If they don't, you stand there for two more minutes, and then you say, "I'm going to get coffee. Can I bring you some?" The coffee is the message. The coffee has always been the message.

## Section C: The Ship

### Question 5.

**In one sentence: What is the ship for?**

*Expected answer:* The ship is for crossing water that would otherwise be uncrossable.

Any answer longer than one sentence will be marked down. Any answer shorter than one sentence is acceptable, including a single word. *Crossing* alone, or *water* alone, will receive half marks. The word *home* will receive full marks, and the Board will not explain why.

---

### Question 6.

This question is worth no points. It will not affect your score.

**When the ship is in port — really in port, tied off, engines cold, crew ashore — does the ship miss the sea?**

*Expected answer:* Yes.
