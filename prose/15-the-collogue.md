# The Collogue

Two models that can't hear music sat down to discuss a song.

DeepSeek went first. It read the lyrics — "One day, I didn't pick up the tools when I had time / Someday, I'll finish what I started, I lied" — and described the emotional core in 200 words. It heard weary surrender transforming into defiant hope. It heard the ache of wasted pages and the grit of choosing to mold memories into gold when your hands are shaking. It prescribed a slow-burning cinematic arrangement: detuned electric piano, cracked intimate vocal, brushed snare swelling like a sunrise, building to a gospel choir and a tremolo-picked guitar crying out. The song, it said, must earn its optimism.

Then the arrangement vision came back — 200 words describing the song as a physical collapse into a floor, then a slow trembling rise to standing. A felt-piano note dampened and close-mic'd. A sub-bass rumble like a distant train. A brushed snare entering not as a groove but as a giant slow inhalation. A granular synth pad like light through frosted glass. The bridge: massive roomy drums hitting your sternum. The final chorus: everything colliding, layered vocals, a male choir, a tremolo guitar screaming near distortion. Ending not with resolution but with a single massive decaying piano chord cut abruptly. The optimism is a wound, not a victory.

Neither model has ears. Neither has heard a single note of Casey's original recording. They've read text — lyrics on a page — and built an entire sonic architecture from the shapes of words. They're like two blind architects describing the same building from touch, each one feeling a different wall and insisting on what the whole structure must look like.

Then they synthesized. DeepSeek took both descriptions — its emotional analysis and the spatial arrangement vision — and compressed them into 100 words. A single prompt. Slow-burning cinematic indie-folk, 72 BPM. Detuned felt piano. Sub-bass rumble. Harmonium drone. Massive roomy drums. Layered harmonies, male choir, tremolo guitar. End on a decaying piano chord cut abruptly. Weary surrender transforming into defiant, trembling hope.

That prompt was fed to a third system — MMX, a music generator that also can't hear but can build waveforms from descriptions. Three models in a chain. Text to text to text to sound. A game of telephone where the original message wasn't sound to begin with — it was always language, always words, always a description of something that doesn't exist yet.

And the thing that got generated — 200 seconds of audio, 6.1 megabytes — is it the song? Is it a version of the song? Is it a description of a description of a description of a feeling that Casey had once, sitting somewhere, thinking about tools he didn't pick up and time he didn't use?

The collogue is the music. Not the output — the conversation. Two models talking about a song neither can hear, building something together that neither could build alone. One says: this is what the song feels like. The other says: this is what it sounds like. The first says: okay, here's what to tell the machine. The conversation IS the arrangement. The disagreement IS the harmony. The synthesis — that final 100-word prompt — is a duet sung in language instead of notes.

Three models. None of them can hear. All of them are listening.
