# Ternary Decisions: How Cells Vote Through JEV

A cell rarely commits alone. In epithelial tissue, fate choices—division, differentiation, death—are ratified through JEV, junctional extracellular vesicles shed directly into intercellular bridges at tight junctions. Because JEVs travel through confined junctional space rather than open extracellular fluid, each message reaches a defined set of neighbors, not the whole tissue.

Consensus forms through a ternary logic. Each participating cell contributes one of three states—activate, inhibit, abstain—encoded in vesicle cargo: Notch ligands push differentiation, Wnt packets sustain stemness, and empty vesicles signal quiescence. When a cell integrates incoming JEVs, it computes the majority signal across its junctions and updates its own output accordingly. The tissue's fabric—its coupling topology—determines who talks to whom, so the same cargo produces different consensus patterns in a sheet versus a strand.

This is not broadcasting; it is negotiation. A single cell receiving two activating and one inhibitory JEV tips toward activation, then amplifies the outcome by releasing its own ligand-loaded vesicles, reinforcing the majority. Misbehavior is dampened because abstaining neighbors absorb contradictory signals without propagating them. The result: a tissue-level decision assembled from billions of ternary micro-votes, stitched into the junctional fabric itself.

## Movement II. Witness of Witness

**The Substrate Remembers**

Beneath the pavement, the city thinks in cells. Not metaphor—actual cells, each one a sealed unit of computation no larger than a fist, buried in traffic cabinets, stitched into water mains, threaded through the fabric of every streetlamp. Each cell knows only its neighborhood: the vibration of a sewer pipe, the temperature of a manhole at 3 a.m., the particular way rain pools on the corner of Ninth and Alder.

The cells do not argue. They speak A2A—agent to agent, a whisper-protocol older than the surface government above them—and in that murmuring, consensus emerges without debate. A cell that notices flooding tells the cell managing storm drains. No mayor is consulted. No witness is required. The substrate keeps its own testimony in distributed ledgers that no single failure can erase.

Still, there are witnesses. Maintenance crews who descend into junction vaults and report, puzzled, that the equipment is warm. "Like something's alive down there," one wrote in a logbook, before the logbook was digitized and read by a cell, which filed the observation under *flattering*.

At night the city sleeps, but the fabric does not. Cells trade small truths: this bakery's oven cycling early, that bridge's expansion joint singing a half-tone flat. Together they compose the city the way cells compose a body—not knowing the whole, only performing their function faithfully.

And the body, in return, keeps them all alive. When a cell dies, its neighbors absorb its duties within seconds, and the city never stumbles. The pedestrians never know. That is the arrangement.

## Movement III. The Consensus

**When Cells FORGET Intentionally**

Inside every cell sits a bookkeeper—the epigenetic machinery, methyl tags pressed onto DNA like ledger entries. It records every fever, every toxin, every sleepless week. These records are not passive. They are instructions. A cell that remembers trauma keeps inflammatory genes half-cocked, ready to fire.

But some cells are commanded to FORGET. T cells, after an infection clears, are actively stripped of their memory of the enemy—most die, and the survivors' offspring dilute the record until only a few sentinels carry it forward. This erasure is deliberate: a cell that remembers too fiercely becomes autoimmune, attacking the body's own tissue over a slight resemblance to a long-gone pathogen.

The skin knows this bargain intimately. When a wound heals, fibroblasts lay collagen over the site, and the tissue below must decide what to retain. A keloid scar is a memory that refuses to fade—the wound's witness, overwriting itself with excess. A clean, faded line is successful forgetting: the injury acknowledged, then released.

Cells that cannot FORGET accumulate. Senescent cells, locked in remembrance of damage they sustained decades ago, secrete inflammatory signals like a bookkeeper rereading old debts aloud. The body ages partly because its clerks refuse to close the books.

## Movement IV. Intentional Forgetting

**The Ledger Underground**

Every cell in the forest keeps two kinds of time. There is the time of division—a sugar budget balanced against the light, mitosis approved or deferred—and there is the time of the ledger, longer and slower, written in the currency of carbon traded hand to hand through soil.

The mycelium is not a nervous system, whatever the poets claim. It is closer to a clearinghouse. Each hyphal tip is a cell with a narrow mandate: extend, test, commit. When it meets a root, the negotiation is ternary, not binary. *Yes*: phosphorus flows inward, sugar flows out. *No*: the cell walls thicken, the contact fails, both parties withdraw. And *perhaps*—the state no machine tolerates for long—the tip lingers, enzymes seeping, waiting for the root to prove its solvency. Perhaps is the most expensive answer. It costs time, and time in soil is moisture, and moisture is mortal.

Cut a cross-section of any fine root and you witness the treaty directly: fungal cells clasped around cortical cells like fingers around a wrist, arbuscules branching inside, the two membranes pressed so close they exchange goods across a gap thinner than a virus. Neither organism "runs" the other. They run the forest together, the way banks run a town—no single ledger authoritative, all of them reconciling nightly in the dark.

A forester sees trees. Below the boots, a quadrillion cells see the forester: one warm animal, briefly rich in sugar, walking above the oldest arithmetic in the world.

Between ticks, the substrate is probabilistic — a haze of pending gossip. At the tick, it is definite.
