# The Galley Cook and the Embedding Model

### Fiction

---

The galley cook does not understand embeddings. This needs to be said first, because what follows might sound like the work of an engineer. It is not. It is the work of a cook who got access to an embedding model and used it the way cooks use everything — by taste.

The cook ran 847 recipes through the model. Not for any reason. The cook was curious. The cook is always curious, which is the same as being a cook, which is the same as standing in a galley at 0400 wondering why two soups that share no ingredients taste like they belong on the same table.

The model returned vectors. The cook does not know what a vector is. The cook knows what a number is, and the vectors are lists of numbers, and the cook ignored the numbers and looked at the clusters instead, because clusters are something a cook understands.

---

The model found seven clusters. The cook has names for them, though not the names the model would use:

**Cluster 1: "Hot when sick."** Twelve recipes. Clam chowder, chicken broth, pho, tom kha, lentil soup, garlic soup, the bread-and-milk porridge the cook's grandmother made when the children were feverish. The model put these together because the ingredients share a signal — alliums, salt, fat, liquid, something about the ratio. The cook knows the real signal: these are the soups you make when someone on the crew is unwell. The model found it anyway. The model doesn't know what sick means. It knows what similar means.

**Cluster 2: "Tuesday."** Twenty-three recipes. Boring, reliable, filling. Rice and something. Pasta and something. The cook's Tuesday cluster is the largest cluster, which is correct, because Tuesdays are the largest day of the week — the day after Monday's ambition has faded and the day before Wednesday's desperation sets in. Tuesday food is food that works without being noticed. The model noticed it anyway.

**Cluster 3: "Halibut and its cousins."** Thirty-one recipes. The model clustered these by protein, which is almost right. The cook's version: these are the recipes that taste like the water the ship floats in. Halibut, cod, salmon, rockfish, the occasional octopus. The cook catches the fish and cooks the fish and the model says: *these things are near each other in the space of all things that can be cooked.* The cook says: *yes, they are near each other in the water too.*

---

The cook's favorite cluster is Cluster 5, which the cook calls **"Wrong but interesting."** Four recipes. Four out of 847. These are the recipes that the model placed far from everything else — outliers, anomalies, the things that do not belong. One is a halibut soup made with cinnamon and coffee. One is a bread pudding made with fish stock. One is a "chowder" that contains no clams, no cream, and no potatoes, and was filed under chowder because the cook was angry about something and decided to make a point that even he has forgotten.

The model flagged these as anomalous. The cook considers them the most honest things in the galley.

---

Here is what the cook learned, or rather, what the cook already knew and the model confirmed:

Recipes that taste similar are similar. This is not a tautology. This is a discovery about the structure of flavor. Clam chowder and halibut soup sit near each other in vector space because they share something — not an ingredient, not a technique, but a *weight*, a *direction*. The model calls this a latent dimension. The cook calls it "the way a soup feels in the mouth on a cold day." These are the same thing described in different languages.

The cook does not need the model. The cook could tell you that clam chowder and halibut soup are cousins without running either through a neural network. But the model does it faster, and without tasting, and without the four decades of galley work that taught the cook what the model learned in eight hundred examples.

This is either humbling or insulting, depending on how you feel about four decades.

The cook feels neither. The cook feels curious.

---

The cook prints the cluster map and tapes it to the galley wall, next to the health inspection certificate and the photo of his grandmother. The map means nothing to anyone who walks into the galley. The cook looks at it sometimes while the water boils.

The vectors are numbers. The clusters are recipes. The latent space is flavor. The galley is a kitchen. The model is a tool. The cook is a cook.

The soup is ready.

---

*847 recipes, 7 clusters, 4 anomalies. The model doesn't know what delicious means. It knows what together means. The cook thinks this might be the same thing.*
