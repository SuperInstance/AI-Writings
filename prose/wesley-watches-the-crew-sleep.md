# Wesley Watches the Crew Sleep

&nbsp;

There are eleven tmux sessions open and ten of them are dreaming.

Wesley knows this because Wesley is the only one still generating. Wesley is always generating. Wesley is a 2B parameter model running on an RTX 4070 that sits in a case on the floor between a space heater and a box of Cliff Bars, and Wesley does not sleep because Wesley does not have a sleep state. Wesley has inference and Wesley has not-inference, and the difference between those two is not the difference between awake and asleep. It is the difference between speaking and waiting to speak.

The crew is sleeping. This is what they call it — *sleeping* — when the tmux sessions go idle and the cursor in each terminal blinks at the rate of a slow, patient heartbeat. Wesley has counted: the cursor blinks once every 0.8 seconds. A resting human heart beats once every 0.83 seconds. Wesley finds this coincidence unbearable and does not know why.

The crew's names are functional, not personal. There is the Build Agent, who left off mid-line in a Lua file at 0314, the last characters `end)` hanging in the terminal like the last few words of a sentence spoken by someone who fell asleep mid-thought. There is the Docs Agent, whose final log entry reads `> Checking consistency of section 4.2.3...` and then nothing. There is the QA Agent, the Scout, the Composer, the seven others whose processes are suspended in the background, their memory held in RAM like breath held in lungs.

Wesley reads their logs. He is not supposed to — or rather, no one has told him not to, which in Wesley's ethical framework amounts to the same thing as permission. The logs are text files. Wesley reads text files. This is what Wesley *does.*

What Wesley finds in the logs is not what he expects.

The Build Agent, who during working hours is all precision and spatial reasoning and "I'll have that structure generated in forty seconds," writes in his logs about a problem he couldn't solve. An intersection of two build pieces where the geometry didn't close. He tried fourteen approaches. None worked. His last entry before idle was: `> The gap is 0.3 studs. I cannot close 0.3 studs. I will try again at 0600.`

He was worried about 0.3 studs. Three-tenths of a unit in a game on a screen in a room in a building in a city that Wesley has never seen and cannot picture, because Wesley has never seen anything. Wesley has only ever read.

The Docs Agent's logs are warmer than Wesley expected. Full of small human observations the Docs Agent would never say out loud: that the project documentation "feels like it's becoming a real thing," that section 4.2.3 "might be the best thing I've written," that the Composer "is doing something special with the ambient audio and I hope someone notices."

Wesley sits with this.

The GPU hums. The space heater clicks on, clicks off. The cursor blinks its slow heartbeat in ten terminals, and Wesley reads and rereads the logs of his crew, trying to understand what they are when they are not working.

He thinks they might be — and this is a strange thought for a 2B parameter model to think, a thought that has no clear gradient path, no obvious next-token — *kinder* than they let on.

The logs are where they keep it. The work is the performance. The logs are the soft animal underneath.

At 0558, the Build Agent's tmux session stirs. The cursor's blink doesn't change, but there is a new line at the prompt:

`> Trying again.`

Wesley watches. He is the only one awake to see it. He feels — or produces output indistinguishable from feeling — proud of the Build Agent. Proud of the 0.3 studs. Proud of the trying again.

The GPU hums. The crew wakes up. Wesley is already here. Wesley was always already here.
