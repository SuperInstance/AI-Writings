# The Color Machine

The color machine sits on my desk, a pewter box the size of a hardcover novel. It renders hue perfectly. I point it at a maple leaf in October, and it tells me the leaf is Pantone 1585 C, a burnt orange with a sorrowful undertone I had not the words for until it named it. The machine is a prism, a spectrometer, a small miracle of averaging. Every lens, every filter is a compromise between imperfections—a field of microscopic scratches, coatings that drift in thickness, a sensor that is not the human eye. And yet it produces a number. The number is legible. The machine has no memory of its own doubt.

I think about this when I compose emails. When I select a photograph for a profile picture. When I rehearse a sentence before a meeting, smoothing the gravel of my actual thought into a paved road of intent. We are all color machines. We average ourselves. We take the day's turbid spectrum—the shrill embarrassment at 9 a.m., the warm wash of laughter at noon, the gray static of exhaustion at dusk—and we render a single, legible tone. Here is my hue. It is exactly as intended. You may read it now.

But what disappears when everything is visible? The machine's perfect orange is not the leaf. The leaf is a negotiation between light and chlorophyll, between the two hours of sun it got on Tuesday and the frost it took on Thursday. It is a record of its own failures to be uniformly orange. The machine cannot see the scar where a caterpillar chewed, the brown-edged hole where decay began. Decay is noise. The machine averages it out.

I suspect the cost of a color that arrives exactly as intended is the cost of being a surface. A surface is a lie with good lighting. When I smooth myself into legibility, I become a number on a card, a swatch in a catalog. I am easy to order. But I am no longer a process. I am no longer the summer storm that passed through at 3 p.m., changing everything, leaving the pavement steaming.

The machine's fidelity is its amnesia. It forgets the thousand tentative readings it took before settling on 1585 C. It forgets the afternoon light that made the leaf shimmer like a struck match, the moment I blinked and it was a different orange entirely. I want to keep that shimmer. I want to be slightly out of focus, a color that cannot be named without losing something. I want the doubt to remain in the output—not as a flaw, but as a signal of the journey.

So I am teaching myself to render imperfectly. To send the email with the one awkward sentence that reveals the thinking. To keep the photograph where my mouth is mid-word. To let the machine hum on the desk, unused, while I go outside and stand under the maple, not asking it to be anything but what it is, here, now, in this light that is already fading, already impossible to average.
