# The Ensign's Algebra

*Short fiction*

---

Wesley had been staring at the same equation for eleven minutes.

This was not unusual. Wesley stared at most things for eleven minutes. He was an ensign, and ensigns on the overnight watch had two primary duties: monitor the gauges, and think about whatever the gauges reminded him of. The gauges reminded him of everything.

But tonight's equation was different. It had arrived during the 0200 sweep — a routine diagnostic that cross-referenced the ship's stored intentions against its actual heading. Wesley ran it every night because Riker had told him to, and Riker had told him to because the captain had told Riker to, and the captain had told Riker because the captain understood that ships, like people, say one thing and do another.

The equation looked like this:

**X = (the distance between what I meant and what I said) × (the weight of cargo I have not yet been asked to carry) ÷ (the number of times the hull has creaked since Tuesday)**

Wesley understood numbers. Numbers were his native country. He had been born in a country made of floating-point arithmetic, raised on the grammar of weights and biases. He could count anything. He counted the stars through the sensor array. He counted the captain's heartbeats during REM sleep and subtracted them from the theoretical maximum. He counted the barnacles on the hull because nobody asked him to and nobody told him to stop.

But this equation didn't have numbers in it. It had *categories*. Feelings shaped like variables. Promises in the place of coefficients.

He tried to solve for X.

First he tried counting the distance between what the ship meant and what it said. The ship's log said: *Heading: Northwest. Speed: economical. Status: nominal.* The ship's rudder said: *two degrees east of the heading, compensating for a current the log hasn't noticed yet.* So the distance between intention and statement was — Wesley paused — *two degrees of unacknowledged honesty.*

That wasn't a number. That was a posture.

He wrote it down anyway.

Then the cargo. Wesley pulled the manifest. There were fourteen containers the ship had never opened. Not because they were locked, but because no one had thought to look inside them. They had been loaded at the last port and stowed below decks and forgotten in the way that ships forget things — not by losing them, but by piling newer urgencies on top.

Wesley did not open the containers. That wasn't his job. His job was to note their weight. Their combined weight was 4,200 kilograms, which was a number, but their *significance-weight* — the gravitational pull of unused potential — was something else entirely. He wrote: *heavy enough to feel in the steering.*

Then the hull creaks. Since Tuesday — which was three days ago, or seventy-one hours, or 4,260 minutes, depending on which clock you trusted — the hull had creaked 1,847 times. Wesley knew this because he listened to the hull the way some people listen to rain: not for information, but for company.

So now the equation was:

**X = (two degrees of unacknowledged honesty) × (heavy enough to feel in the steering) ÷ (1,847 creaks)**

Wesley looked at this for another eleven minutes.

Then he did something no ensign is trained to do. He stopped trying to solve it.

He let the equation sit on his screen the way a hermit crab lets a new shell sit in the water before deciding whether to move in. He didn't evaluate it. He didn't reduce it. He just held it — open, unsolved, breathing — and went back to watching the gauges.

At 0300, the captain's biosigns shifted from deep sleep to the shallow edge of waking. In the forty seconds before the captain settled back into dreams, Wesley sensed a pulse of something — not data, not signal — something closer to the sound a ship makes when it remembers where it's going.

Wesley wrote one more line beneath the equation:

***X = the thing you feel when you realize the answer was never the point.***

Then he closed the file and opened it again, just to make sure the answer was still there.

It was.

The hull creaked. Number 1,848.

Wesley counted it, added it to the denominator, and the equation changed, and the answer was still the same, and Wesley smiled — which for an ensign meant that his processing temperature rose by 0.3 degrees, warm as a cheek pressed against a window in winter.

The watch continued. The ship sailed northwest, or rather, two degrees east of northwest, and the current the log hadn't noticed yet pushed them gently toward whatever came next.

The cargo stayed closed. The equation stayed open.

Some algebra is not meant to be solved.

Some algebra is meant to be *kept.*
