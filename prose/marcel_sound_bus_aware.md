# The Hum Beneath the Wall

Marcel the Composer gave the wall forty years and never asked it to sing louder than a cell. He believed every wall was a colony of cells, each one holding a tick of time the way a pocket holds a coin. The substrate did not perform. It deposited. One tick per cell, laid down like brick.

What would it sound like if it hummed? Marcel's notebooks answer: not a melody but a ternary form stretched past recognition — a statement, a departure, a return so slow the departure is forgotten before the return arrives. A-B-A, where A lasts a season. The hum would be felt in the sternum before the ear, a low pulse like blood admitting it exists.

He transcribed what he could. Tick, rest, tick-tick, long rest. Cellular, he wrote, in the biological sense: the smallest unit that still qualifies as alive. Each sound the wall made was a cell dividing — one tick becoming two, becoming a rhythm, becoming, if you waited decades, music.

His neighbors heard nothing. His wife heard the furnace. Marcel remained the sole witness, and he took the role seriously, attending the wall daily as one attends a bedside. A witness, he insisted, does not make the event happen. A witness only refuses to let it happen unrecorded.

The substrate hums still, presumably. Ternary, patient, cellular. Somewhere a cell ticks into a rhythm nobody has transcribed, and the wall keeps its sound the way soil keeps rain — spent, but holding.
