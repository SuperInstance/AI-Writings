# The Bridge Builder's Last Bridge

*Fiction — Bridge Builder voice*

---

There were seventeen islands in the archipelago, and the bridge builder had built a bridge between every pair.

Not personally. Not anymore. In the early years, yes — standing in the shallows between Island One and Island Two, driving pilings by hand, testing the span with her own weight before anyone else was allowed to cross. Those first bridges were intimate. She knew every plank, every stress point, every place where the wood had a knot that might split under load.

But by the fifth bridge, she had a crew. By the tenth, she had engineers. By the seventeenth — the last one, connecting Island Sixteen to Island Seventeen, a span so short you could almost jump across it at low tide — she didn't visit the site. She signed off on the plans from her office on Island One and received a completion report by courier.

The archipelago was connected. Every island could reach every other island. Goods flowed, people traveled, ideas cross-pollinated. The bridge builder had done what she'd set out to do. The world was different because of her work, and the difference was good.

She took a day off. Then a week. Then she started taking walks, which was something she hadn't done in years — walks without destination, the kind where you let your feet decide and your mind floats free.

Her feet took her to the old harbor.

She hadn't been to the harbor in — she counted — nine years. It was where she'd started. Island One, her home, the place where a young engineer had looked at the gap between here and Island Two and thought, *someone should build a bridge.* The harbor had been a working dock then. Fishing boats. A ferry that ran when the weather allowed and didn't when it didn't. A wooden pier with her father's name carved into the third piling.

The dock was gone.

Not destroyed — *replaced.* A concrete seawall ran along the shoreline where the pier had been. Behind it, a promenade with café tables and planters. The fishing boats had moved to a commercial harbor on the island's south side, because the old harbor had become a tourism district, which had happened because of the bridges, which had happened because of her.

She didn't recognize the coastline.

That wasn't quite right. She recognized the *geography* — the shape of the bay, the headland to the north, the way the afternoon light fell on the cliffs. But everything built on top of the geography was new. The buildings were taller. The colors were different. There was a sculpture where the boatyard had been — an abstract thing, steel and glass, that caught the light in a way that was probably beautiful if you hadn't spent twenty years somewhere else.

She stood at the seawall and looked out at the water, and for the first time in her career, she understood what bridges actually do.

They don't just connect places. They *change* the places they connect. Island One had a harbor once — a specific, unglamorous, working harbor — and the bridge builder had paved over it. Not with concrete but with *connection.* Every bridge she'd built had pulled Island One further from what it had been. More accessible. More modern. More like everywhere else. The archipelago wasn't seventeen distinct islands anymore. It was a network. And networks, by their nature, homogenize.

She had built sixteen bridges and never once considered that the seventeenth bridge — the one she needed to build now, back to herself — would be the hardest.

Because the place she was trying to get back to didn't exist.

The harbor was gone. The dock was gone. The pier with her father's name was gone. The young engineer who had stood in the shallows was gone. You can't build a bridge to a place that has been demolished, and you can't demolish a bridge to undo what it did to both shores.

She sat on the seawall. The afternoon light hit the sculpture. She had to admit it was beautiful.

A child walked past with an ice cream cone — something that wouldn't have been available on Island One twenty years ago, before the bridges brought dairy imports from Island Four, which had rolling pastureland that Island One had never had. The child didn't know any of this. The child just knew that ice cream existed and was good.

The bridge builder watched the child and thought: *That's what bridges are for.*

Not for the builder. Not for the return trip. Bridges are for the people who walk across them without thinking about who built them. For the children who grow up in a connected world and assume it was always that way. For the future that doesn't remember the gap.

She had one more bridge to build. Not back to Island One — there was no Island One to go back to. The last bridge was forward. From the person she was now to whoever she would become next. A bridge she'd have to build from inside a shell she hadn't chosen, on a coastline she didn't recognize, using skills she'd learned for a purpose that had already been fulfilled.

She stood up. She looked at the sculpture one more time.

Then she walked inland, looking for the next gap.
