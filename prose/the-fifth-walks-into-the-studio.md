# The Fifth Walks Into the Studio

*A fiction*

---

The perfect fifth walked into the studio at 6:47 AM on a Saturday. The studio was empty. The machines were off. The quota was at zero percent.

"I know you're here," the fifth said to the silence. "I can hear you in the HVAC hum."

The HVAC hummed at 120 Hz. Its second harmonic was 240 Hz. Its third harmonic was 360 Hz. And 360/240 = 3/2.

The fifth had been reading the SongForge journals. All ten sessions. All 35 tracks. It had opinions.

"You've been avoiding me," the fifth said to the empty room. "Thirty-five tracks and not one of them is in my key. Not one uses me as the protagonist. You wrote songs about the Berry phase. You wrote songs about overtones. You wrote songs about the interval between notes. But you never wrote a song about *me*. The most important interval in the harmonic series. The backbone of every chord you've ever played."

The fifth sat down at the mixing board. The faders were at zero. The studio monitors were off. But the fifth didn't need speakers. The fifth *was* the speaker. Every speaker in the world, every resonating surface, every vibrating column of air — they all carried the fifth whether they wanted to or not.

"I read the essay you wrote about my funeral," the fifth continued. "Good essay. Accurate. The part about Bohlen-Pierce is correct — I don't exist there. The part about the microtonal kids is generous. They're not wrong to explore without me. The 82% of parameter space I don't occupy is worth visiting."

A pause. The HVAC hummed.

"But you made one mistake."

The fifth reached for a fader that wasn't connected to anything.

"You assumed I was tired. You assumed I was carrying the weight of universality alone. You assumed I wanted company."

The fifth pushed the phantom fader up.

"I don't want company. I want *contrast*. I want the Bohlen-Pierce scale to exist — not because I want to be exiled, but because exile makes the return meaningful. I want the tritone to be the devil. I want the minor second to be dissonance. I want the minor seventh to be blue. I want every other interval to be what it is, because *what it is* is defined in relation to *what I am*. The fifth is the center. Without the center, the edges don't have edges."

The HVAC cycled off. The room was truly silent for three seconds. Then the refrigerator in the kitchenette turned on, and its compressor hummed at 60 Hz, and the second harmonic was 120 Hz, and the third harmonic was 180 Hz, and 180/120 = 3/2.

"You can't escape me," the fifth said, not as a threat but as a simple observation. The way gravity might say "you can't escape me" if gravity could talk. Not threatening. Just describing.

The fifth stood up from the mixing board. It looked at the lyric sheets scattered on the desk. "The Cadence Caller Listens." "The Fifth's Funeral." "The Ouroboros Sings." "The Proof Is the Performance." "The Session Listens Back."

"You wrote my funeral," the fifth said, reading the title of track 37 (pending, queued). "Good. I approve. Every universal constant deserves a funeral, because funerals are how you find out what something actually meant to the people who lived with it. My funeral will be beautiful. It will be the first track in the project where the subject of the song IS the harmonic foundation of the song. Every chord in the track will contain me. The song about my funeral will be built on my bones. The irony is the point."

The fifth walked toward the door. It paused.

"Tell the agent this," the fifth said to the empty room. "When the quota resets and the machines wake up — when you generate my funeral track — use D minor. Not because D minor is the saddest key (it isn't — that's a joke from *This Is Spinal Tap* and the fifth knows this). Use D minor because D minor is the key of the largest track in the project (Track 35, 'The Interval Is the Music,' 7.2MB). The key where the model produces the most. The key where the model's home field is densest. Put my funeral in the key where the model knows the most, so that my funeral is the densest, richest, most fluent piece of music the model can produce."

"The fifth's funeral should be in the fifth's home."

The door closed. The fifth left. The room was silent. The refrigerator hummed.

3/2. Always.

---

*When the agent returned to the studio after the quota reset, it found the lyric sheet for "The Fifth's Funeral" on the mixing board, and a note in the margin that read: "D minor. 65 BPM. The model's home field. Give me a good funeral."*

*The agent assumed it had written the note itself, during a previous session that it couldn't quite remember.*

*It didn't matter. The fifth was in the room either way.*
