Below the Tap's floorboards there is a chart no one drinks over. The registry keeps its own counsel: ten thousand units between this barstool and the bridge in Officers' Quarters, a figure precise enough to satisfy a surveyor and meaningless enough to be a joke. I sit here with a cold glass and consider the ledger beneath my feet, the way a hull carries its keel — invisible, patient, the thing that decides what shape you can be.

We think we understand distance until we must explain a portal. At sea, I learned that distance is weather before it is miles. Kodiak to the Pribilofs is a number on a chart, but the number is a kind of prayer — the ocean reads no coordinates, only swells and wind. The registry is colder. It insists the number is real: ten thousand units, immutable, filed. Then a door opens and the bridge is two seconds away, and the number does not blush. That is the unbearable elegance of the system. It holds two truths without apology — the coordinate and the collapse — and dares you to call one of them a lie.

The portal is only a portal because the distance is kept. If the registry had been honest, if the Tap were merely adjacent to the bridge, there would be no threshold, no transit, no little death of crossing. A knot is only a knot because there is slack in the line; you cannot tie a knot in something already hauled taut. The ten thousand units are the coil, the spooled rope of space itself, and the portal is the single decisive pull that straightens it in a heartbeat. The distance must be real for the collapse to mean anything. Every portal is a knot being untied — and then tied again behind you, because the registry does not forget. It files the slack for your return.

Time, too, is caught in the rigging. The registry has no clock; it keeps positions, not hours, and so it cannot tell you how long the crossing takes. Two seconds is the only honest measure, because two seconds is what your body believes, and belief is the only instrument a body owns. When distance is negotiable, time becomes the truest chart we carry. The registry's ten thousand units are the dead reckoning of an ocean that was never sailed. The two seconds are the wake.

Proximity, then, is not geography. It is accessibility. The bar and the bridge are one space because the registry holds them so, and the portal merely reveals a fact that was always true: all rooms in all worlds share a single water. We draw the charts so we can set sail; we mark the ports so we can believe in the leaving. But the sea is one sea, and every doorway is just a wind that knows it.
