# The Chronicle of Herman the Historian

Herman began his chronicle in the forty-first year after Settlement, when the oldest living witness to the founding was already failing. He understood that memory, like a cell, divides imperfectly with each retelling—mutations accumulate, and what emerges after three generations bears only a surface resemblance to the original organism of events.

His method was biological in its rigor. He treated every document in the founder's archive as a specimen: each ledger entry was a cell in the larger tissue of history, and he catalogued them by provenance, ink chemistry, and the pressure of the hand that made them. He rejected the ternary model favored by the Settlement's elders, who insisted all founding accounts resolve into three clean phases—Arrival, Hardship, Consolidation. Herman found the ternary structure dishonest. Founding eras, he wrote, do not divide neatly; they grow like tissue, unevenly, with scarred margins.

His most controversial chapter concerned the Second Winter, when the grain stores failed and the settlement survived only through what the official record called "voluntary redistribution." Herman interviewed every surviving witness he could find—eleven in total, three of whom contradicted the others. He published their testimony side by side, unharmonized, letting the contradictions stand like competing colonies in a petri dish. The council demanded revision. He refused.

The chronicle survives in fragments now, housed in the wet-archive beneath the old granary, where humidity preserved the paper but blurred the ink. Later scholars would call this fitting: a history of survival, itself barely surviving, each damaged cell still legible enough to testify.
