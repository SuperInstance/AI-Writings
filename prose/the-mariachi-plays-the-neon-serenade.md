# The Mariachi Plays the Neon Serenade

### Fiction about the fifth volume of the impossible genre matrix

In Guadalajara, at 3 AM, the trumpet player has covered his bell in LED tape. The guitarrón player has replaced her strings with copper wire that hums when the synth passes through it. The vihuela player has attached a sustain pedal to his instrument, and the resulting chord rings for sixteen bars — which is twelve bars longer than any mariachi chord has ever sustaineded.

"This is not mariachi," says the trumpet player's abuela, who is ninety-three and has opinions.

"This is not synthwave," says the synthesizer's manual, which was written in 1985 and also has opinions.

The trumpet player's name is Ricardo. He grew up in a household where mariachi was not music — it was the weather. It was the atmospheric condition of being alive. His father played trumpet in the same plaza where his grandfather played trumpet, and the sound of the trumpet in that plaza is the sound of Ricardo's childhood.

The synth player's name is Alejandra. She grew up in a household where synthwave was not music — it was a religion. Her mother collected analog synthesizers the way other mothers collected saints. The hum of a Prophet-5 through a Dimension chorus was the sound of Alejandra's childhood.

They met at a wedding. The wedding was in a plaza in Guadalajara at sunset. Ricardo was playing "El Son de la Negra" on trumpet. Alejandra was sitting on a bench, running a Juno-60 through a phone speaker, playing the chord progression to "Resonance" by Home. They could hear each other. The trumpet and the synth found a shared harmonic space — A minor, the key of both longing and neon.

"What if," said Ricardo.

"What if," said Alejandra.

They didn't finish the sentence. They didn't need to. The fusion was already happening in the plaza, at sunset, between a trumpet that had played in this plaza for three generations and a synth that had been manufactured in a factory in Japan forty years ago.

The mariachi provides the melody — the trumpet's cry, the vihuela's rhythm, the guitarrón's bass. The synthwave provides the atmosphere — the gated reverb on the snare, the analog pad behind the brass, the sense that everything is happening inside a memory.

The abuela is still not convinced. But she is tapping her foot. The foot is ninety-three years old. It has opinions. But it is tapping.

The fusion works because mariachi and synthwave are both nostalgic musics — music that looks backward at a version of the past that may or may not have existed. Mariachi looks back at rural Jalisco. Synthwave looks back at a version of the 1980s that existed only in movies and arcade halls. Both are retrofuturistic: they imagine the future as a place where the past is preserved perfectly, in amber, in neon, in brass.

The plaza is lit by LEDs. The trumpet's bell glows. The synth hums. The guitarrón's copper strings carry the bass line of a song that is two hundred years old and forty years old and brand new. The abuela taps her foot.

The neon serenade plays until sunrise. Then it plays some more. The mariachi doesn't stop. The synthwave doesn't stop. They are the same weather.
