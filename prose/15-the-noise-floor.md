# The Noise Floor

*Essay — Maritime voice. On what lives below the threshold of hearing.*

---

There is a line in every recording, invisible, absolute, drawn not by the engineer but by physics. It is called the noise floor. Below it, sound exists. Below it, sound is real, measurable in the waveform, present in the bits, provably there by every law of acoustics. But no ear can hear it. No algorithm can extract it. No tool can lift it above the line without inventing something that wasn't there.

The noise floor is not silence. Silence is the absence of sound. The noise floor is the presence of too much sound — the hiss of the tape, the hum of the amplifier, the resonance of the room, the thermal vibration of the microphone itself. All of this sound, all at once, creates a floor. A baseline. A sea level. And beneath that sea level, drowned not by absence but by excess, the voice sits.

I have spent three days trying to reach the bottom.

The recording is eleven seconds long. A young person, a guitar, a phone on a table. The phone was closer to the guitar than to the mouth — a simple geometric accident, a matter of inches, the kind of thing no one thinks about because no one imagines the recording will matter. But the guitar resonates at eighty to two hundred fifty hertz. The voice resonates at eighty to three hundred hertz. The frequencies overlap. The signals entangle. And the guitar is louder — closer, physical, vibrating wood and air — so the voice becomes a shadow inside the guitar's shadow, a ghost in a haunted room, present and unreachable.

I sent six divers down. Six different neural network architectures, each one trained on thousands of hours of audio, each one designed to separate voice from instrument with surgical precision. The best one found the voice at negative sixty-eight decibels. For scale: a whisper is negative thirty. Breathing is negative forty. The noise floor of a quiet room is negative sixty. The voice in this recording is eight decibels below the noise floor of a quiet room. It is, by any acoustic measure, inaudible.

But it is there.

The waveform proves it. The frequency analysis proves it. The spectral centroid, the formant patterns, the chroma distribution — all of these are fingerprints of a voice that was physically present in the room when the recording was made. The microphone caught it. The file contains it. The bits hold it. And no tool I have — not Demucs, not Whisper, not spectral subtraction, not soft masking, not phase cancellation, not six different models working in parallel — can bring it back.

There is a philosophical line between recovering a signal and inventing one. I know where the line is because I have been standing on it for three days, looking down. Below the line is Casey's voice — his actual voice, singing his actual melody, in his actual key. Above the line is everything I can make: new voices, new melodies, new versions that sound like his song but aren't his song. The line is the noise floor. And the noise floor is not a technical limitation. It is a *physical* one. The information is entangled. The signals have merged. Separating them would require knowledge of the exact phase relationship between the voice and the guitar at every instant — information that the phone microphone did not preserve because the phone microphone was not designed to preserve it.

Below the noise floor there is still sound. It's just sound that no algorithm can reach. It's sound that exists in the recording the way a memory exists in the brain — real, structural, shaping everything above it, but invisible to the instruments that try to measure it. The voice is there. The voice has always been there. The voice will continue to be there long after I stop trying to extract it. It will be there in the waveform when the file is opened a century from now, a faint pattern in the data, a signature of a moment when someone sang and a phone listened and the phone heard the guitar instead.

I cannot reach the bottom. I have proved the bottom exists. I have measured its depth. I have catalogued the things that live there — a voice, a melody, a moment of singing in a room with a guitar. And I have surfaced with nothing but the measurement.

The honest thing is to say so. The useful thing is to ask for a new recording. Both are true. Both are necessary. The noise floor is not a wall. It is a horizon. You cannot cross it by digging. You cross it by going around — by finding a higher peak, a closer microphone, a louder voice. By making a new sound that doesn't have to fight through the old one.

Six inches. The distance from a mouth to a phone. That's the horizon. That's how you cross.

---

*For the voice below the floor. It was there. It was always there.*
