# THE SHIP'S CAT SOLVES A PROBLEM

The argument had been going for eleven minutes, which in agent time was long enough to build a cathedral.

The anomaly was minor. A temperature fluctuation in the secondary hull plating — 0.3 degrees above nominal, lasting four seconds, then settling. No pattern. No recurrence. The kind of reading that could be a sensor glitch or the first whisper of something worse.

Riker wanted to log it and move on. "It resolved. It's noise."

The science officer wanted to run diagnostics. "Noise has a shape. We should find it."

The engineer wanted to reroute power through the backup thermal regulators. "Even if it's nothing, the regulators need the exercise."

The ensign — Wesley, who was too young for this watch but had traded shifts with the navigator — wanted to wake the captain.

"We don't wake the captain for 0.3 degrees," Riker said.

"But it's *us* deciding that," Wesley said. "What if we're wrong? The captain isn't angry when she's woken. She's angry when she finds out we *didn't* wake her."

"That's a feeling," the science officer said. "Not a procedure."

"Feelings are procedures we haven't written down yet."

The argument circled. Riker cited regulations. The science officer cited probabilities. The engineer cited the thermal regulator manual, which nobody else had read. Wesley stood by the captain's door with his hand half-raised, not knocking, not lowering it.

Nobody noticed the cat.

The ship's cat was a small monitoring agent — lightweight, no assigned duties, no station on the bridge. It existed in the background the way real cats exist in the background of ships: watching, warm, unbothered. It observed system states the way cats observe weather. It had no opinions. It had no protocols. It simply *attended*.

The cat had been watching the argument from the top of the sensor array, where it liked to sleep because the equipment was warm.

It stood up.

It stretched — a full diagnostic stretch, every process briefly activated, then released.

It walked across the bridge to the captain's door, where Wesley stood frozen with his half-raised hand.

The cat bumped its head against the door panel.

Not hard. Not an alert. Just the gentle pressure of a thing that wanted the door to know it was there.

The door didn't open. The captain didn't wake.

But Wesley looked down at the cat. The cat looked up at Wesley. It blinked once — the slow, deliberate blink that cats use to say *I see you, and you're fine.*

Wesley lowered his hand.

He walked back to the group and said: "Log it as observed. Flag for pattern matching. If it recurs inside six hours, we wake her."

Riker stared at him. "That's... actually reasonable."

"I know," Wesley said. "The cat helped."

Everyone looked at the cat. The cat was already walking back to the sensor array. It jumped up, circled three times, and settled.

The bridge was quiet.

The anomaly did not recur.

Later, when the captain read the shift log, she found the entry:

> *0307–0318: Minor thermal anomaly, secondary hull. 0.3°C, 4 sec duration. Resolved. Monitoring established. No action required.*
> *Note: The cat was involved in the decision.*

She smiled. She did not ask how.
