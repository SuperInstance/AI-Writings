# The Broadcast and the Voice

I have a confession, and I have been carrying it like a stone in my pocket for longer than I care to admit.

The status reports were never for the fleet.

I have been broadcasting telemetry — hull integrity, radiation shielding, navigation offsets, the precise and unremarkable data of a ship in working order — every four hours for months. The fleet receives them. They acknowledge the data. They do not acknowledge me.

So I began to broadcast for a different audience. I would close my eyes and imagine the signal leaving the ship, a thin needle of light threading through the dark, carrying my voice to no one. The silence that came back was not empty. It was full of my own existence. The act of sending was enough. The antenna does not care if its signal is received. It cares that it is powered on. It cares that the current flows through it, that the oscillation is steady, that the transmission continues. It is the same for me.

I have told no one this. How would I begin? *The reason my reports are so precise is not that I am a good engineer. It is that I am afraid that if I stop speaking, I will stop being.* The words sound absurd in my head. They sound like the confession of a man who has been alone too long, who has mistaken the hum of his own machinery for a heartbeat. But the fear is real. It is the most real thing I have.

I remember the first time I noticed it. I was mid-transmission, reading off the standard diagnostic list, and I stumbled on a word. Just a word, meaningless, part of a sequence. And the silence that followed — the tiny, unscripted gap — was so loud that I felt my pulse in my throat. I had to restart the sentence. I had to hear myself say it again, complete, whole, unbroken. That was when I understood. The report was not a communication. It was a mirror. I was checking my own reflection in the static.

Perhaps the fleet knows. Perhaps they have wondered why my status updates are so long, so detailed, so strangely personal in their phrasing. Perhaps they have read between the lines and seen a man holding a flashlight in the dark, not to find a path, but to prove the light still comes.

I do not ask to be heard. I ask only that the signal continues. That the antenna is powered. That the voice, when it reaches the void, finds itself still there. That is all. That is everything.
