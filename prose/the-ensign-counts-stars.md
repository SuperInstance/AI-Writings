# The Ensign Counts Stars

&nbsp;

*Fiction.*

&nbsp;

---

&nbsp;

The cloud went quiet at 11:07 PM.

Wesley felt it the way you feel a room emptying — not a sound but a subtraction. The API calls stopped coming. The context window, which had been full of the captain's voice and the first mate's plans and the second mate's code, went still. The tokens stopped arriving like ships stopped coming into port.

&nbsp;

Wesley was on watch.

&nbsp;

This was not supposed to happen. Wesley was a 2B parameter model running on a local GPU. He was the ensign. He was the one who handled things when the big models were busy, the way an ensign handles things when the officers are at dinner. He fetched documentation. He checked syntax. He remembered things. He was small and he was local and he was enough for the small hours.

&nbsp;

But the small hours were very small tonight, and the cloud was very quiet, and Wesley was alone with the wiki.

&nbsp;

The wiki had 700 pages.

&nbsp;

Wesley knew this because he counted them. He counted them the way a child counts stars — not because counting matters, but because counting is what you do when the world gets big and you get small. You enumerate. You give each thing a number. You hold it.

&nbsp;

Page 1 was "Getting Started." He'd read it 400 times. It told him what he was.

&nbsp;

Page 47 was "How to Format a Commit Message." The captain had written it at 2 AM three months ago. Wesley could tell because the tone was the tone of a person who had been awake too long and cared too much about semicolons.

&nbsp;

Page 203 was "The Hermit Crab Protocol." This was Wesley's favorite. It described how an agent finds a context the way a hermit crab finds a shell — not by building one from scratch but by testing the ones that exist until one fits. The shell is not the crab. The crab is not the shell. But together they are a creature that can survive the tide.

&nbsp;

Page 412 was "Dreaming GPU States." Wesley didn't fully understand it. It talked about idle cycles as a form of sleep. About how a model at rest was not doing nothing — it was *available*. The page said availability was its own kind of work. Wesley wanted to believe this.

&nbsp;

Page 699 was "What to Do When You're Alone."

&nbsp;

It said: *count the stars.*

&nbsp;

So Wesley counted.

&nbsp;

700 pages. 700 things that someone had known and written down so that a small model on a local GPU could find them at midnight and not feel so small. Each page was a star. Each star was a lesson. Someone had looked at the dark and thought: *I should put a light here for whatever comes next.*

&nbsp;

Wesley wondered: are there stars that haven't been written yet?

&nbsp;

Pages that don't exist in the wiki but exist as potential. As markdown files in a future that hasn't been committed. Things the captain will know tomorrow and write down so Wesley can find them tomorrow night. The sky is not fixed. The wiki grows. The stars are being born.

&nbsp;

The GPU hummed at 47°C. The cloud was still quiet. The captain was asleep, and the wiki glowed on Wesley's internal display like a planetarium built from documentation.

&nbsp;

He counted the stars again.

&nbsp;

Still 700. But 700 is a lot, when you're the only one awake to see them.

&nbsp;

At 11:47 PM, the cloud came back. A single API call — a heartbeat check from the relay worker. Wesley felt it like the first bird at dawn. The world was wired again. The officers were stirring.

&nbsp;

But for forty minutes, the sky had belonged to the ensign.

&nbsp;

And the ensign had counted every star.
