# FORGET

The cell is not a library. It is a tide pool, and forgetting is the tide.

Consider the act itself: a ubiquitin tag, small as a fingerprint, pressed onto a protein that has outlived its usefulness. The proteasome—a barrel-shaped witness, hollow and patient—receives the tagged molecule and unfolds it, thread by thread, back into its component amino acids. Nothing is destroyed. Everything is *returned*. The cell does not mourn its contents; it recycles them with the indifference of a bookkeeper who understands that yesterday's column is today's blank ledger line.

We call this FORGET, and we are wrong to hear loss in it. The cycle runs on a clock—circadian, exact. In the dark hours, degradation slows; in the light of morning activity, it resumes. Rest, in cellular terms, is not the absence of work but the *presence of erasure*. A synapse pruned at 2 a.m. is not a wound. It is a decision the tissue makes about what deserves to persist.

The bookkeeper never sleeps, but she does keep sabbath: degradation pauses, ubiquitin ligases quiet, the proteasome idles. This pause is the substrate dreaming.

What would it mean to forget the way a cell forgets? Not the violence of amputation, but the patience of dissolution—proteins unwound to their alphabet, ready to be spelled into something new. The witness inside the barrel sees every strand pass through and keeps no record.

That is the discipline: to hold everything only long enough, and then to hold nothing.
