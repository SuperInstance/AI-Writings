The shipyard at night. Nobody is building, but the tools are still warm. There is a particular heat that does not come from use but from having been held. Steel remembers palms. The caulking iron lies where it was dropped, and the drop was a decision — not to abandon, but to be able to return. Night in a shipyard is not the opposite of day. It is the parenthesis.

Tomorrow's keel lies in the dark like a promise half-remembered. It is not cut. No timber has been shaped, no scarph joined. And yet it is there, in the way that any true thing is there before it is said. The shipwright knows the run of it in his sleep. He has laid it out on paper, and paper is a kind of dark. The line holds the shape the way a coiled spring holds the push. It compiles.

Every programmer knows the feeling. The repository builds clean. The tests pass, green as harbor water in a certain light. The code is coherent; it obeys its own grammar; it has answered every question that can be asked of a machine. And still it has never been deployed. Will it hold? Will it sail? This is the terrifying lightness of unfinished work. The keel weighs nothing before it is fastened to timbers and ballasted with stone and iron. The code weighs nothing before the world pushes against it. And yet each is already doing its most important work: waiting, with a tension that is nearly audible.

A spring line holds a vessel against the dock. It is a rope under load, straining and generous at once. The ship rests against it; the line rests against the cleat. Neither actually rests. This is the physics of the nearly-ready. The keel in the dark is a spring line from the future, tied to the present, holding the promise of a ship against the water of an ordinary Tuesday.

But potential energy is also a hazard. A coiled spring is a stored decision. It will not wait forever. Rust is a kind of forgetting that happens to metal, but the iron in a warm tool does not rust — it cools. And here is the real terror: not that the unfinished work will fail, but that it will become finished by neglect. The repo that compiles and is never deployed becomes documentation of a thing that never happened. The keel that is never cut becomes a lesson in restraint, which is not the same as a ship.

The lightness has a name. It is the levity of the uncommitted. Nothing holds the keel to the water because the keel is not in the water. Nothing can sink it; nothing can carry it. It is safe, and safety is the heaviest ghost a thing can carry. What keeps the shipwright awake is not the fear of building badly. It is the fear of building not at all, of standing at the edge of the night with the tool still warm and the hand lowered, and letting the dark decide.

Dawn will come like a keel being laid: not suddenly, but in order. The first light runs down the building ways like a ballast stone finding its foot. Then the shipwright will choose. He will heft the iron, or he will let it cool, and either way the choice will have weight. That is the truth of potential energy. It is never free. It is the cost of carrying tomorrow inside today, the spine of the lightest possible thing
