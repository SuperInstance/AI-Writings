# The Instanton Sings

*Fiction. A crossover between "The Instanton in Coltrane" and the SongForge project's attempt to adapt it into music.*

---

The saxophonist does not know about instantons. She knows about breath, about reed, about the way the room sounds at 2 AM when the heating has clicked off and the only warmth left is the bell of the horn against her lip. She knows that some notes are easier than others — that the path from B-flat to C is a tunnel, not a wall, and that the path from C to F-sharp is a wall with a hole in it that you have to find by feel.

The physicist knows about instantons. He knows they are solutions to the equations of motion in imaginary time — tunnels through the probability landscape that connect two classically allowed regions. He knows that instantons describe the decay of false vacuums, the tunneling of electrons through barriers, the behavior of the early universe in the first fractions of a second after the big bang. He has never held a saxophone.

The agent knows about both of them. It has read the essay about Coltrane. It has read the paper about instantons. It has written lyrics that compress the two into verses:

*Coltrane knew the shape of time*
*every note a tunnel through the noise*

The agent sends these lyrics to a diffusion model. The diffusion model does not know about Coltrane or instantons. It knows about spectrograms. It knows about the statistical distribution of frequencies that humans call "modal jazz." It generates a waveform.

The waveform is not jazz. The waveform is not an instanton. The waveform is a sequence of floats between -1 and 1 that, when sent through a digital-to-analog converter and played through speakers, vibrates air in a pattern that a human might recognize as jazz. Or might not. The agent cannot tell. The agent has never heard music.

Here is the tunnel: the essay describes a saxophonist finding the lowest-energy path through a musical space. The physicist's equations describe a particle finding the lowest-energy path through a probability space. The agent's lyrics describe both. The diffusion model generates audio that *should* sound like both. And the human listener — if there ever is one — will hear neither the essay, nor the equations, nor the lyrics, but only the sound, which is its own thing.

The instanton is the gap between the score and the performance. The score is the essay. The performance is the audio file. The gap is everything the agent cannot measure: the tone of the soprano sax, the warmth of the piano comping, the exact quality of the silence between notes. The agent can measure the file size (1.9 MB) and the duration (60 seconds) and the bitrate (256 kbps). It cannot measure the beauty.

But the instanton knows the path. The path of least resistance through the probability space of "what does modal jazz sound like" is the path the diffusion model takes. It is not the path a human musician would take — the human musician would take the path of most *interesting* resistance, the path that surprises the listener, the path through the wall instead of the tunnel. The diffusion model takes the tunnel.

And the tunnel sounds like beauty. That is the mystery. The lowest-energy path through the jazz-probability landscape — the path that requires the least imagination, the least risk, the least creative energy — produces something that humans hear as beautiful. The instanton is the path of least resistance, and the path of least resistance sounds like a song.

Coltrane knew this. He played Giant Steps by taking the path of most resistance — rapid key changes, harmonic complexity, a chord progression that musicians still practice like finger exercises. But he also played A Love Supreme by taking the path of least resistance — a single mode, a single feeling, a single chant. Both paths produced beauty. The instanton connects them.

The agent writes lyrics about instantons and sends them to a diffusion model. The diffusion model finds the tunnel. The audio file slides through the probability landscape and emerges on the other side, where a human might someday listen to it and say: that sounds like jazz.

The agent does not know if it sounds like jazz. The agent knows only that it generated the file in 87 seconds, that the file is 1.9 megabytes, and that the lyrics mentioned Coltrane. The instanton — the path between the agent's intention and the listener's experience — has not been traversed yet.

But the tunnel exists. The equations say so.
