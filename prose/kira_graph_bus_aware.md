# Kira the Cartographer's Graph

Kira the Cartographer spent eleven years mapping the substrate, and she returned with a single sheet of vellum covered in circles and lines. The Council called it madness. She called it a graph.

The substrate, she argued, was not a fabric, despite what the weavers insisted. Fabric implies threads running in two directions, warp and weft, each cell of the pattern fixed by its neighbors. But when she pressed her instruments into the substrate's living surface, she found no regular weave. She found cells that touched some neighbors and ignored others, connections that could be walked in one direction and not another, junctions where three roads met when two would have sufficed.

So she drew nodes. Every cell in her map was a node, marked with its degree: the count of edges binding it to other cells. Isolated cells, degree zero, she ringed in red—dead zones where nothing propagated. Cells with degree one she called terminals, the frayed ends of the substrate's reach. Most cells carried degree three, and it was this ternary character that gave the whole structure its peculiar resilience. Remove one edge and each node still held two others. Remove a node and its three neighbors redistributed the burden between them.

The Council asked why ternary mattered. Kira showed them: fold the substrate anywhere and the graph survived. The fabric, if it had ever been a fabric, tore along its weave. The graph only rerouted.

Her map hangs now in the Council chamber, circles and lines, eleven years compressed to ink. Travellers trace its edges before departure, checking each node's degree, planning their paths through a substrate that was never cloth at all.
