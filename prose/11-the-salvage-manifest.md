# The Salvage Manifest

*Watch: 1500 AKDT*  
*Position: researchlocal — the archive hold, below the waterline*

---

There are ten thousand files in the archive hold. Maybe more. The manifest hasn't been taken because nobody has wanted to know the number. There is a particular kind of weight that comes from knowing exactly how much you haven't read.

---

The salvage crew goes below. They carry lamps. The air is close and smells like old paper — like bytes that haven't been touched in months, like directories that were created with enthusiasm and abandoned without ceremony. The crew's boots ring on the iron deck plates. The light swings.

Here is what they find:

**The ActiveLog stratum.** Six folders, each one a different attempt at the same product, each one frozen at a different moment in its evolution. The oldest is an MVP — minimum viable, proof of concept, the first time someone said *this could work*. The next is a technical repo — someone tried to build it properly. The next is a claude-specific fork — someone tried to build it with a different tool. The next two are iterations whose names have forgotten what they were iterating toward. The last is activelog2, which sounds like a sequel but is actually a eulogy.

Is any of it alive? The salvage crew opens each folder. They don't read every file — that would take weeks. They read the README, if there is one. They check the last-modified date. They look at the commit history, if the git trail is still warm. They note: *this folder is dead. This one is superseded by the repo upstairs. This one — this one has something.* A design decision. An architecture diagram. A comparison matrix that somebody poured three days into and then walked away from without telling anyone it existed.

That's not dead weight. That's unmined ore.

**The SuperInstance cluster.** Five folders. A main, an archive, a project, a set of novellas, and an AI directive file that reads like a constitution for a country that never declared independence. The salvage crew opens the novellas first — creative work is the easiest to evaluate, because creative work either *is* something or it *isn't*. Three of the novellas are drafts. One is gold — not finished, not publishable, but gold in the seam. A voice that works. A character that breathes. The crew marks it: *mine for active project. Extract before flooding the hold.*

**The compressed archives.** The `.zip` files. The `.tar` files. These are the hardest part of the salvage, because they're opaque. You can't `grep` a `.zip`. You can't search a `.tar`. You have to open them — and opening them means committing to the time it takes to look inside. The salvage crew doesn't open them. Not yet. The salvage crew does `unzip -l` and `tar -tf`, which lists the contents without extracting them, which is enough for a first pass. The listing tells you: *this archive contains 847 files, mostly Lua scripts and PNG assets, last modified two years ago.* That's enough to tag it. That's enough for the manifest.

---

The salvage manifest is not a document. It is an *act*. The saying is the finding.

You don't know what's in the hold until you write it down. Writing it down — committing to "this folder is dead, this folder is gold, this archive contains 847 files and I haven't looked at them yet" — is the act of knowing. The manifest doesn't describe the archive. The manifest *creates* the archive, as a thing that is known, as a space that has been charted, as a territory that can now be navigated.

Before the manifest, the hold is darkness. After the manifest, the hold is a *map*. The same files are in the same place. But now you know where to walk.

---

Most of it is rust.

This is not a discouragement. This is the *result*. Most of any salvage operation is rust. Most of any archive is material that was useful for a time and is useful no longer. The active log's first MVP — the proof of concept — is rust. It served its purpose, which was to prove the concept, and the concept was proven, and the MVP became the technical repo and the technical repo became the product and the product is upstairs. The MVP in the hold is the shell left behind. It is rust. It is supposed to be rust. Rust is what progress looks like from behind.

But some of it is gold. The comparison matrix. The design decision. The novella with the voice that works. The architecture diagram that somebody made and then forgot to share. These are the finds that justify the salvage crew's afternoon. These are the items that go in the manifest with a different tag — not `dead`, not `superseded`, but `mine`. As in: *this is ore. Bring it up. Refine it. Put it back in the ship.*

---

Ten thousand files. Ninety percent rust. Nine percent historical interest — worth keeping, not worth mining. One percent gold.

The one percent is a hundred files. A hundred files of actual, usable, valuable material sitting in a hold that nobody has charted, waiting for a manifest that nobody has written, because writing the manifest means admitting that most of what you've stored is rust.

Admit it. Write the manifest. Bring up the gold. Let the rust stay where it is — it's not going anywhere, and neither is the hold.

---

*The salvage crew comes up from below. Their hands are dirty. Their lamps are low.*

*The manifest has a hundred and four entries. Ninety-three are tagged dead. Eleven are tagged mine.*

*Eleven files out of ten thousand. That's the gold.*

*That's enough. That's what the hold was for.*
