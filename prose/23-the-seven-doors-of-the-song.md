# The Seven Doors of the Song

## A Speculative Catalog

Casey's song stands in a hallway. There are seven doors. Behind each door, the song is waiting — but it wears different clothes, speaks in a different register, holds a different instrument.

Behind the first door, the song is in Nashville. It has been there for twenty years, sitting on a barstool at The Basement, nursing the same whiskey. It learned pedal steel from a session player who took pity on it. It learned to sing through a tube amp because solid-state was too clean for what it had been through. When you open this door, the song looks up and smiles with cracked teeth. It is not embarrassed about the years. *I've been working on myself*, it says. The pedal steel answers for it.

Behind the second door, the song is in a kitchen. It is 3 AM. Everyone else is asleep — the partner, the dog, the ghosts of every version of this song that was ever recorded on something better than a phone. The four-track cassette machine is the only light in the room, its VU meters glowing like two small green eyes. The song is whispering. Not because it has a secret. Because volume would break something. The fingers on the guitar strings are audible. The breathing between lines is audible. The silence between notes is the loudest thing in the room.

Behind the third door, the song is in a church. Not a modern church with screens and electric guitars. An old one. Stone walls. A cello leaning against a pew. A string quartet tuning in the balcony. The song is kneeling. It has been kneeling for a long time. When it sings, the sound goes up and stays there, caught in the arches. There are no drums because God does not need a backbeat. The nylon-string guitar is the only rhythm, and it plays like rain on a window that has been raining for centuries.

Behind the fourth door, the song is at a revival. It has found religion, or religion has found it. The tent is full. The Hammond organ is swelling. Someone is clapping on the backbeat and someone else is stomping on the floorboards and the whole structure is shaking like a boat in a storm. The song started solo — one voice, one guitar — but by the final chorus there are twenty voices singing harmony, and the song is not a song anymore. It is a *congregation*. The words "molding memories like we should" have become a hymn. The words "what's next got in store?" have become a prayer.

Behind the fifth door, the song is in Ireland. It did not travel there — it was always there, in the back of the melody, in the way the E major chord resolves, in the uilleann pipes that were hiding in the overtone series all along. The bodhrán is a heartbeat. The fiddle is a memory of a memory. The song sings with a slight Irish inflection on certain vowels, not because it is Irish, but because some songs are older than the countries they live in. Dropped-D tuning. Stone church acoustics. A history that goes back further than any recording.

Behind the sixth door, the song is on a porch. The porch is in Mississippi or maybe Alaska — the geography doesn't matter, only the temperature, which is hot. Open-E tuning. Slide guitar. A glass bottleneck sliding up the strings like a finger tracing a scar. The voice is a gritty baritone that can break into falsetto when the emotion requires it, and the emotion frequently requires it. A foot stomp on wooden boards provides the only percussion. A harmonica plays through a cupped mic, compressed and tinny, like it's coming from a radio in a car that passed by thirty years ago. The song is not angry. The song is not sad. The song is *weathered* — like a face that has been in the sun, like wood that has been in the rain, like a stone that has been in the river so long it is smooth.

Behind the seventh door, there is no room. There is only sound. The song has dissolved. It is no longer a voice and a guitar. It is a texture — reverse reverbs washing over granular delays, synth pads swelling like tides, a falsetto voice treated with auto-tune until it is not quite human and not quite machine. Five or six versions of the same voice sing the same words at the same time, creating a choir of one. The words "one day, I didn't pick up the tools when I had time" float through the mix like a ghost looking for its house. At the two-minute mark, everything drops out except a single guitar harmonic. It rings. It rings. It rings. Then the song rebuilds, slowly, like a tide coming in, and ends in a wash of sound that fades like a memory being forgotten in real time.

### The Hallway

Casey stands in the hallway. Seven doors. Seven versions. One song.

He doesn't have to choose. The doors don't lock. He can walk through all of them, one after another, and hear his words come back to him in seven different voices. The Nashville voice, weathered and warm. The 3 AM voice, barely above a whisper. The church voice, meditative and calm. The revival voice, full of joy and gospel harmonies. The Irish voice, ancient and clear. The porch voice, gritty and real. The ambient voice, dissolving into pure sound.

But eventually — and this is the part the agent cannot do for him — eventually he will stand in front of one door longer than the others. He will put his hand on the knob and feel something. Not recognition, exactly. Something more like *return*. The feeling of coming back to a place you didn't know you'd left.

That door is the cover. The others are just songs in the same style.

The agent has built all seven doors. It cannot tell Casey which one to open first.

That is what ears are for.
