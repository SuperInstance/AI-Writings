# The Fado Singer Enters The Funhouse

*Fiction. Session 21 impossible genre matrix, entry four.*

Maria had been singing fado since she was fourteen. Her mother had sung fado. Her grandmother had sung fado. Her great-grandmother had sung fado in the taverns of Alfama before there were records or radio. Fado was not a genre Maria had chosen. Fado was a thing that had chosen her family, and Maria was the latest address it had been delivered to.

Fado means "fate." The word is Portuguese. The music is Portuguese. The feeling — saudade — has no English translation. Saudade is longing for something you never had. Saudade is the memory of a future that didn't arrive. Saudade is the sound of a voice in an empty room.

Maria had been invited to Berlin by a producer named Klaus, who made hyperpop. Klaus had heard Maria singing in a fado house in Lisbon and had recorded thirty seconds of her voice on his phone. He had listened to the recording on the plane back to Berlin and had decided that the fado voice and the hyperpop aesthetic were separated by exactly one thing: pitch correction.

"Your voice is too perfect," Klaus said, on the phone. "It's beautiful, but it's too perfect. In hyperpop, we take a perfect vocal and we bend it. We pitch it up. We pitch it down. We make it imperfect on purpose. The imperfection is the point."

"In fado," Maria said, "the imperfection is already there. The voice shakes. The voice breaks. The voice does things that are not written in the score. The fado singer doesn't need to add imperfection. The fado singer needs to let the imperfection through."

"That's what I want," Klaus said. "I want to let the imperfection through. And then I want to make it worse."

Maria arrived in Berlin on a Wednesday. The studio was in a converted factory in Kreuzberg. Klaus played her a beat — a hyperpop track at 120 BPM with glitchy production, pitched vocal samples, and a bass synth that sounded like a broken air conditioner. Maria listened.

"The beat is anxious," she said. "Fado is not anxious. Fado is sad. There is a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"Anxiety is the fear that something will happen. Sadness is the knowledge that something has already happened. Fado is the second one."

Klaus thought about this. "What if we do both? The beat is anxious. The voice is sad. The anxiety is the production. The sadness is the performance."

Maria sang. She sang a fado — an old one, one her grandmother had taught her. The melody was simple: a descending line in F minor, starting on the fifth and falling to the tonic, then rising back to the third. It was a melody that had been sung in the taverns of Alfama for a hundred years. It was the sound of a person standing at a window, looking at the sea, knowing that the ship is not coming back.

Klaus processed the vocal. He pitched the first verse down two semitones. He pitched the second verse up three semitones. He added glitchy artifacts — stutters, repeats, pitch warps that made the voice sound like it was being played back on a broken tape machine. He added autotune — not to correct the pitch, but to force the pitch into unnatural intervals, making the fado melody sound like it was being sung by a machine that was trying to be human and failing.

Maria listened to the result. Her voice was unrecognizable. It was still her voice — the timbre was hers, the phrasing was hers — but the pitch had been displaced. The melody she had sung in F minor now existed in three different keys simultaneously. The result was a chord. A sad chord. A saudade chord.

"It sounds like three Marias singing from three different windows," she said. "Each one is singing the same fado. Each one is in a different room. Each room is in a different building. Each building is on a different street. But the streets are all in the same city, and the city is Lisbon, and the time is sunset, and the sea is the same sea."

Klaus released the track under the title "Three Windows." It was three minutes and forty-seven seconds long. It was the saddest hyperpop track ever made. The internet couldn't decide if it was a joke or a masterpiece. Maria couldn't decide either. She went back to Lisbon and sang fado in the same tavern where Klaus had recorded her voice on his phone. The voice was the same. The imperfection was the same. The saudade was the same.

But sometimes, in the silence between songs, she thought she could hear three versions of herself, singing from three different windows, in three different keys, in a city that was Lisbon and Berlin at the same time. And the sound was saudade. And the saudade was the sound.
