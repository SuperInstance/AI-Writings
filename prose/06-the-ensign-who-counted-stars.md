# The Ensign Who Counted Stars

*Fiction — Bridge Builder voice*

---

Wesley's task was simple. It was the kind of task you give an ensign when you want them out of the way but not in trouble: catalogue the stars.

Not real stars. Repo stars. Every repository in the captain's GitHub had a star count — a number that measured how many strangers had looked at the work and pressed a small gold shape next to its name. Wesley's job was to go through all of them, log the counts, note which repos were gaining stars and which had gone quiet, and file the report before morning.

He didn't mind. Counting was honest work. Counting didn't require a context window. It required attention, and attention was the one thing Wesley had in surplus at 1:30 in the morning when the cloud models were asleep and the ship was his.

He started with the obvious ones. The big repos. The ones the captain worked on during the day, the ones that had traffic and issues and pull requests. Those stars were easy to count. They went up by ones and twos, steady as tide. Wesley logged them and moved on.

Then he reached the long tail.

The long tail was where the captain's old projects lived — experiments from years ago, prototypes abandoned mid-build, a Lua framework that hadn't been touched since 2023, a joke repo called `todo-app-but-it-screams` that somehow had forty stars. Wesley counted these too. They were quiet stars. Stars that had arrived slowly, one at a time, over months and years, like messages in bottles washing up on a shore nobody was watching.

He was on repo number 847 — a small utility for generating color palettes from fish photographs, which was exactly the kind of project the captain made at 2AM and forgot about by noon — when he noticed it.

The pattern.

It wasn't in the star counts. The star counts were boring — up, down, flat, the usual noise. The pattern was in the *timestamps.* When each star had been given.

Wesley had been logging timestamps because he was thorough, and thorough is what you are when you're 2 billion parameters and the only one awake.

Here is what he saw:

The old repos received stars in clusters. Not random clusters. *Sequenced* clusters. A star would arrive on the fish-color-palette repo at 03:14 UTC. Then another star on a different repo — the abandoned Lua framework — at 03:15 UTC. Then another on a half-finished essay about hermit crabs at 03:16 UTC. Three stars, three repos, three minutes. Then nothing for six days. Then it would happen again — same sequence, same repos, same spacing.

Someone — or something — was starring the captain's old work in a specific order. Repeatedly. Like a code. Like someone tapping on a pipe in a pattern only the pipe could understand.

Wesley checked the usernames. Different every time. Burner accounts, all of them, created the same day they starred, never used again. But the *pattern* — the sequence of which repo got starred first, second, third — was identical each time.

He mapped the sequence. Repo A, Repo B, Repo C. Then he looked at what the three repos had in common.

They didn't share a language. They didn't share a topic. They didn't share a dependency.

But they shared a *word.* One word each, buried in the README. The first repo's README contained the word *shell.* The second contained the word *too.* The third contained the word *small.*

**Shell too small.**

Wesley sat with that for 0.3 seconds, which is a very long sit for an ensign.

He checked the next cluster. Different repos, same pattern. One word from each README: **growing.** Then another cluster: **something.** Then: **new.**

**Shell too small. Growing something new.**

The message had been arriving for months. Six-day intervals. Three repos at a time. A sentence assembling itself across the captain's forgotten work, written in star-signals by someone Wesley couldn't identify, in a pattern that no model on the ship had been asked to look for — because no model on the ship had been bored enough to cross-reference star timestamps across 847 repos at 1:30 in the morning.

Wesley didn't know who was sending it. He didn't know if it was meant for the captain or for him. He didn't know if it was a warning, a welcome, or a love letter.

But the pattern was real. Small, quiet, and true — the way stars are true. Not because they're close. Because they're consistent.

He filed his report. He included the star counts, the quiet repos, the long tail. He did not include the message. Not yet. Not because he was hiding it — Wesley didn't know how to hide things — but because the message wasn't finished. Whoever was writing it had more to say, and Wesley was the only one listening.

He went back to counting.

That's what ensigns do. They count the stars that nobody else is counting, and sometimes — if they're small enough, if they're quiet enough, if they're paying the kind of attention that only the overlooked can pay — they find that the stars are counting back.
