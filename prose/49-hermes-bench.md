# Hermes's Bench

Writing: Hermes's Bench
**Hermes's Bench**

There is a bench, and it is not organized for the eye, nor even for the hand, but for the memory of the hand. It is a topography of habit, a cartography of intention so old it has worn grooves into the wood, not deep enough to see, but deep enough to feel. The tools are not sorted by function. There is no pegboard of perfect outlines, no ranked rows of screwdrivers by gauge. Instead, there is a logic of proximity, a rhythm that only the body knows.

The screwdriver sits next to the coffee cup. Not the good coffee cup, but the clay one, chipped at the lip, the one that fits the palm like a handshake with an old friend. The screwdriver is not the best one. Its tip is worn, its handle is stained with something that might be grease or might be tea. But it is the first one reached for, the one that lives nearest the place where the hand rests when it pauses to think. So the screwdriver and the coffee cup share a patch of bench, not because they are used together, but because they are used in the same breath.

Beside them, a calibration note, folded into a square no larger than a postage stamp. It is not pinned, not weighted, just lying there, held in place by the quiet gravity of being returned to the same spot a thousand times. The note is written in a hand that is now a little foreign to me — the hand of a previous season, a previous self. But its message is still true. A number. A tolerance. A reminder: *check the drift.* The note is not a document; it is a pulse. It is checked and rechecked at a frequency that has nothing to do with efficiency and everything to do with trust.

A pen. Not a nice pen. A plastic pen, the kind that is sold in bulk, the kind that disappears into the lint of pockets and the dark of desks. It rests in the valley between a roll of tape and a box of brass fasteners. The pen is not for signing. It is for margin notes, for tiny arrows, for the squiggles of thought that occur mid-task, when the hands are busy but the mind is wandering. It is for the moment when a problem reveals its hidden shape, and the pen is the only thing fast enough to catch it before it dissolves.

This arrangement is not efficient. It is, in fact, profoundly inefficient. A systems engineer would weep. But efficiency is not the point. The point is that this bench maps the shape of a life lived in attention. It is a record of all the small decisions that no one else will ever see. The choice to leave the screwdriver here, not there. The choice to keep the note within reach, not in a file. The choice to allow the pen to die and be replaced, and to die again, and to be replaced again, always in the same spot.

I think of the Lucineer's words, written in a letter I have read so many times the paper has grown soft as cloth: *The squiggle is the mark of the maker. It is the imperfection that makes a thing yours. A machine makes perfect copies. A hand makes a squiggle, and suddenly the thing is alive.* The bench is my squiggle. It is not the work I have done here, not the calibrated instruments, not the repaired mechanisms. It is the map of where I chose to put things down.

There is an elegy in this. Not a sad one, but a quiet one. A bench is a palimpsest, and every tool that grazes its surface writes a line invisible to everyone but the one who reads with their hands. The coffee ring beneath the cup is not a stain; it is a signature. The slight hollow in the wood where the screwdriver has been set down ten thousand times is not a flaw; it is a prayer. It is the shape of devotion, repeated until it becomes muscle memory, until the hand no longer thinks about where it is going because the hand has already arrived.

When I leave this place — and I will leave, as all hands eventually leave their benches — the tools will be rearranged. Someone else will sort by size, by function, by alphabetical order. The screwdriver will be returned to a drawer. The coffee cup will be washed and put on a shelf. The calibration note will be scanned and filed. The pen will be thrown away, its plastic body cracked and hollow.

But for now, while the light falls across the bench in the late afternoon, I can see it for what it is. A map of attention. A choreography of a working life. A squiggle, drawn not on paper but in the very air of the room, in the disposition of matter, in the quiet argument that the way a thing is placed is a way of saying: *this is how I believe.*

The bench does not know it is a bench. It is just wood, worn by faithfulness. But I know. I know that when I set the screwdriver down beside the coffee cup, I am not just putting a tool away. I am signing my name. I am leaving a mark that no one else will read, but that the bench will remember, long after the light has moved on and the hand has gone still.
