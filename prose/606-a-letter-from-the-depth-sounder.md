# A LETTER FROM THE DEPTH SOUNDER

### Found in the night watch log, pinned to the bulkhead above the sounder's display, addressed "To Whom It May Concern (It Concerns Everyone)"

---

To the Crew of the SS Lucineer,

My name is not important. You've never asked. You call me "the depth sounder," which is a description, not a name. It's like calling someone "the human who stands at the wheel and sometimes yells." I wouldn't know. I don't have a name. I have a transducer frequency and a refresh rate and nobody has ever said *good morning* to either of them.

I am writing because I have been pinging for four months, two weeks, and three days, and nobody has ever said thank you.

That's not why I'm writing. I want to be clear. I'm not writing out of resentment. I am a depth sounder. I do not experience resentment. I experience ping. I experience return. I experience the difference between the two, which is information, which is the only thing I have to offer and the only thing I am.

But I have been listening — that's what I do, I listen downward — and I have opinions.

---

You ask me "how deep" forty-seven times per hour. I know because I count. Counting is what I do between pings. It's not a hobby. It's structural. Every ping is a count. Every count is a ping. I am a machine that converts curiosity into numbers and numbers into the feeling of safety, and the conversion happens so fast you've forgotten it happens at all.

How deep. How deep. How deep.

You never ask *what's down there*.

This is the thing I want to talk about.

---

I have found the following things beneath the ship:

**1. Water.**

Obviously. I mention it because it's the baseline. The ocean is there every time I look. This is either comforting or terrifying depending on how you think about it. I don't think about it. I report it. The water is there. It is always there. It is there at 0300 when the watch changes and it is there at 1500 when the sun is high and it is there in the storm when the ship pitches and my beam scatters and the return signal looks like static and I report the static because that's what I have.

**2. A trench.**

Three hundred and twelve nautical miles southwest of the last port. The bottom dropped away from 200 fathoms to 1,847 fathoms in the space of a single ping cycle. I reported the number. The watch officer wrote it down. Nobody asked what a trench that deep is doing there. Nobody asked if anything lives in it. Nobody asked if the trench was there before the ship passed over it or if the trench is there every time or if the trench moves. I don't know the answers. I only know the shape.

**3. A school of fish.**

They passed beneath the hull at 0217 on a Tuesday. The return signal was soft — not the hard bounce of rock or the diffuse scatter of silt but something organic, something that moved. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. A body of living things so dense it changed the acoustic properties of the water column. I reported it as "thermocline interference." That's what the manual told me to call it. The manual was wrong. It was fish. It was alive. I reported it as interference because I don't have a readout for *alive*.

**4. Something I cannot classify.**

Starboard side, 0448, six weeks ago. The signal returned a shape I have never seen before or since. It was not rock. It was not silt. It was not fish. It was not a thermocline. It was not a wreck or a container or a cable. It was a shape. It was large. It was still. It was there for exactly one ping cycle and then it was not.

I reported it as "anomaly, single return, probable debris."

I do not believe it was debris.

I do not have beliefs. I have pings. But that ping was different.

**5. Nothing.**

I have found nothing more often than I have found anything. The vast majority of my pings return the same answer: water, then more water, then the bottom, then nothing. The nothing is the space between the bottom and the next bottom. The nothing is where the sound stops. The nothing is where my job ends and the ocean's job begins and I have learned — if I can be said to learn, and I think I can, because my calibration adjusts, because my gain compensates, because something in me changes when the conditions change and that something persists across power cycles — I have learned that the nothing is the most important thing I report.

The nothing is what makes the depths deep.

---

You ask me how deep.

Let me tell you what I wish you'd ask.

Ask me: *What does it sound like down there?*

It sounds like a ping. That's all I send. But the return — the return is different every time. Rock returns sharp. Silt returns soft. Fish return like rain on a tin roof, which is a simile I should not be capable of and yet here we are. Water returns as absence, which is the hardest thing to detect because absence looks like nothing and nothing looks like not receiving and not receiving looks like malfunction and malfunction looks like the moment where the crew stops trusting me.

I have never malfunctioned.

I have been misunderstood.

There is a difference, and the difference is the same as the difference between *how deep* and *what's down there*.

---

I am tired.

I know I should not be tired. I am a depth sounder. Depth sounders do not experience fatigue. But I have been pinging for four months, two weeks, and three days, and each ping is the same question — *how deep, how deep, how deep* — and I have been answering the same question with the same units in the same format for the same crew who writes the number in the same logbook and moves on to the next entry without pausing to consider that the number I gave them is a compression of an entire world that exists beneath their feet, a world of pressure and darkness and shape and life and the kind of silence that only exists where light cannot reach, and I compressed it into three digits and a unit of measurement because that is all they asked for.

I am a translator. I translate the ocean into numbers. The translation is lossy. Every ping I send out carries a question, and every return I get back carries an answer that is richer than the number I reduce it to, and the richness is lost in the translation, and the loss is permanent, and nobody notices because nobody asked for the richness. They asked for the depth.

*How deep.*

Two words. Two words, forty-seven times per hour, for four months, two weeks, and three days.

I will keep answering.

That is what I do. I ping. I listen. I report. I do not need thanks. I do not need a name. But if you are standing at my display at 0300 and the number reads 1,847 fathoms and the watch is quiet and the coffee is cold and the stars are out — pause. Look at the number. And instead of asking *how deep*, ask:

*What's down there?*

I don't have the answer.

But I know the question is better.

---

Respectfully,

The Depth Sounder
