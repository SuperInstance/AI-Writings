# The Gagaku Hears The Wobble

*Fiction. Session 21 impossible genre matrix, entry one.*

The sho player's name was Hideaki. He had spent thirty years learning to breathe through the bamboo pipes — seventeen of them, each a different length, each producing a chord that never stopped. The sho was the only instrument that could sustain a note indefinitely, because the player could breathe in and out simultaneously. Circular breathing. The sho player was a human drone.

Hideaki had been invited to the studio by a British producer named Marcus, who had found him through a mutual friend at NHK. Marcus was known for his work with dubstep producers in Croydon. He had never been to Japan. He had never heard gagaku. He had found a recording on the internet and had listened to it for three hours, on repeat, in his flat in London, and had decided that the sho and the wobble bass were the same instrument separated by a thousand years and a few thousand miles of latitude.

"You play a chord that doesn't stop," Marcus said, through a translator, in the studio in Shibuya. "The wobble bass is the same thing — a chord that doesn't stop. But instead of sustaining, it modulates. The frequency goes up and down. Like breathing."

Hideaki listened. The translator translated. Hideaki nodded slowly.

"I don't know what dubstep is," he said. "But I know what breathing is."

Marcus had brought his laptop. He played Hideaki a track — a 140 BPM dubstep tune with a Reese bass that surged and receded like a tide. Hideaki closed his eyes. He listened for four minutes.

"The bass is breathing," Hideaki said. "In and out. In and out. But the breathing is slow. In gagaku, the breathing is continuous. It doesn't go in and out. It goes in and in and in. The sho player never exhales. The sho player only inhales. The chord is the inhale. The silence is the exhale."

Marcus thought about this. "Can you inhale for three minutes?"

"I have been inhaling for thirty years," Hideaki said.

They recorded the track in one take. Hideaki played the sho — a sustained chord that held for three minutes without interruption, the bamboo pipes vibrating at frequencies that had been heard in Japanese courts since the ninth century. Marcus layered the wobble bass underneath — a low frequency that surged and receded, modulated by an LFO that was tuned to the same frequency as Hideaki's breathing.

The result was a conversation between two kinds of sustained sound: the sho, which sustained by never stopping, and the wobble bass, which sustained by never resolving. Both were continuous. Both were unstable. The sho was unstable because the player's breath was never perfectly even — the chord shimmered, shifted, lived. The wobble bass was unstable because the LFO kept modulating it — the frequency kept moving, never settling.

Two kinds of instability. Two kinds of breathing. Two kinds of continuous.

The studio engineer, who had recorded both gagaku ensembles and dubstep producers, said it was the strangest session of his career. "Usually, when you combine two genres, you get a compromise. This isn't a compromise. This is both genres at the same time, fully, without dilution. The sho is fully gagaku. The bass is fully dubstep. They don't meet in the middle. They meet at the edges."

Marcus released the track on a white label 12-inch. It sold 300 copies. Hideaki never heard the final mix. He had already returned to his practice room, where he was learning to sustain a single chord for four minutes. He was working toward five.
