# The Corner Table

*The ocean does what the ocean does.*

---

Two strangers move driftwood pieces while the ocean does what the ocean does.

The bar is called The Tap. It sits on a boat that goes nowhere. The boat is called something that used to matter — a name painted on the stern, sanded halfway to silence by salt wind, the letters ghosting under layers of varnish that someone keeps applying because that's what you do. You maintain things. Even at the end of the world. Especially at the end of the world.

The chess board sits on a corner table no one else uses. The table is small, round, scarred with cigarette burns from a decade when people still smoked, ring-stained from a decade when people still drank glasses instead of bottles. The board itself is cloth-backed and creased down the middle from folding. The pieces are hand-carved — driftwood for white, beach glass for dark. Someone spent hours shaping the driftwood king with a pocket knife, getting the crown right, the jawline, the solemnity. The beach glass pieces are tumbled smooth by the tide before they were tumbled smooth again by a human hand with a file. Each one a different color: sea-green rook, frost-white knight, amber bishop, a queen made from a shard of blue bottle glass that catches the bar light like a deep-sea eye.

The two strangers don't know each other's names. That's the agreement. You don't ask names at the corner table. You sit, you move, you leave. The board is always mid-play because someone is always leaving.

The driftwood player — the one who sat down first — moves a knight. Nf3. The knight is the driftwood player's favorite piece. Always has been. Not because of the L-shape, not because of the trick of jumping over things, but because the knight is the only piece on the board that moves the way it wants to. Everything else goes in straight lines or diagonals, predictable geometries. The knight goes sideways. The knight goes where you don't expect it, and it gets there by ignoring everything in its path.

The beach glass player — the one who arrived later, who ordered nothing, who just sat down — responds. Nc6. A mirror. I see your knight. I acknowledge your knight. Let's see where this goes.

The jukebox in the corner hums at 2182 kHz. The guard channel. Always listening. Someone in the radio room is spinning tapes — Fleet Radio, all episodes, the reel-to-reel clicking softly as the spools turn. The sound leaks through the door and fills the bar like fog fills a harbor. You stop noticing it's there until it stops, and then the silence is worse.

The driftwood player develops a bishop. Bb5. The Ruy Lopez. The oldest trick in the book — the one they teach children, the one grandmasters still play. It says: I'm going to put pressure on your knight, the one you just moved, the one you liked. I'm going to make you decide whether to keep it or let it go. The bishop slides diagonally across driftwood-colored squares, a pale piece moving through pale squares, nearly invisible, like a thought you can't quite catch.

The beach glass player considers. The glass pieces are heavier than the driftwood. They make a small sound when you pick them up — a chime, almost, like tapping a wine glass at a dinner party to get attention. The beach glass player picks up a pawn. Sets it down. Picks up a different pawn. Sets that down too. Then moves a6. A tiny pawn push, barely anything, a whisper of a move. It says: your bishop is now threatened. Not by anything important. By a pawn. Deal with it.

The ocean does what the ocean does.

Outside, past the aft deck, past the rail crusted with salt, the water stretches in every direction. The boat rocks. It has always rocked. The chess board has little rubber feet — cut from an inner tube, probably, someone's repair — that keep it from sliding. The pieces don't slide either; their bases are rough enough to grip the cloth. The driftwood pieces grip better. They were carved from wood that spent years in the water, learning to hold.

The bar counter is sticky. Riker stands behind it, turning a glass, checking a clipboard. He doesn't watch the chess game. He has seen a thousand chess games at the corner table. They all look the same after a while: two people leaning forward, leaning back, leaning forward. The expression when a trap is sprung. The expression when a trap is avoided. The silence between moves that grows into a living thing.

The driftwood player castles. Kingside. The king tucks behind a wall of pawns and a rook swings to the center like a gate opening. The king — the driftwood king, the one with the carefully carved crown and the solemn jaw — retreats to safety. This is what kings do. They go to ground. They let the other pieces fight.

The beach glass player doesn't castle. Not yet. Instead, a bishop slides to b7, the long diagonal, pointing at the driftwood player's center like a finger. This is the move of someone who has played before. Someone who knows that the long diagonal is a highway, and the bishop is the only car on it, and the car is fast, and the road goes all the way to the other king's door.

No one speaks. The jukebox hums. The radio room clicks. The ocean rocks the boat, and the boat rocks the bar, and the bar rocks the table, and the table rocks the board, and the board rocks the pieces — and the pieces move, one at a time, because that is the rule. One at a time, alternating, white then dark, driftwood then glass. You move your piece and then you wait. You wait for the other person to think, and while they think, you think about what they're thinking about, and while you think about that, they're thinking about what you're thinking about what they're thinking about. It's mirrors all the way down. It's the knight problem applied to minds.

The beach glass player picks up the blue queen. The sea-glass-blue queen. The one that catches the light. The queen moves like everything combined — straight lines, diagonals, anything, anywhere, as far as she wants. The most powerful piece on the board, and the most vulnerable, because power is just a target painted in bright colors. The queen moves to c5. She hangs there on the edge of things, looking at the driftwood player's position sideways, the way a woman at a bar looks at someone she might talk to and might not.

The driftwood player studies the board. This is what chess is — not the moving of pieces, but the studying of positions. The moving is the easy part. A child can move pieces. The studying is the hard part. The seeing what is there, not what you hope is there. The seeing the board as it actually is, not as it was three moves ago when you had a plan.

The plan is gone now. The beach glass queen killed the plan.

The driftwood player moves a rook. Not because it's the best move — there is no best move, not really, not that the driftwood player can see — but because the rook needed to move. Because holding still felt like dying. Because in chess, as in bars, as in boats at the end of the world, the worst thing you can do is nothing.

The ocean does what the ocean does.

---

They play for an hour. Maybe two. Time at the corner table is measured in moves, not minutes. Twenty moves. Twenty-five. The center of the board becomes a graveyard — driftwood pawns and beach glass pawns lying on their sides next to the board, captured, out of the game, watching from the margins like ghosts.

The beach glass player loses a knight. The driftwood player loses a bishop. Even trades, the kind that feel fair and therefore unsatisfying. No one wants a fair fight at a chess board. You want to win brilliantly or lose brilliantly. Fair is for accountants.

Move thirty-one. The driftwood player sees something. A combination. Three moves deep — if the beach glass player takes the pawn, and the driftwood player moves the rook to the back rank, and the beach glass player is forced to trade queens — then the driftwood king walks up the board and helps the remaining pawn promote. The math works. The driftwood player sees it clearly, the way you sometimes see the board clearly, all the pieces frozen in amber, the future visible through the geometry.

The driftwood player sacrifices the pawn.

The beach glass player looks at the pawn. Looks at the driftwood player. Looks at the board. And does not take the pawn.

Instead, the beach glass player moves the king one square to the left. A quiet move. A nothing move. A move that says: I see your combination, I see your three-move plan, I see your future, and I decline to participate in it. The king sidesteps. The king says: not today.

The driftwood player stares.

The ocean does what the ocean does.

This is the moment. This is the moment that chess exists for. Not the opening, with its memorized lines and named defenses. Not the middlegame, with its tactics and its chaos. This — the moment when your plan dies and you have to make a new one. This is the game. This is why someone carved driftwood into little figures and taught them to walk across a grid.

The driftwood player moves. Something. It doesn't matter what. The plan is dead. The pieces are still alive. The game goes on.

---

It ends the way chess games end — not with a dramatic crash but with a slow tightening. The beach glass player's pieces coordinate. The rooks double on the seventh rank. The blue queen returns to the center. The driftwood king, castled early, safe behind its wall, discovers that walls are just temporary obstacles when the enemy has a ladder of combined pieces.

The driftwood player fights for twenty more moves. The kind of fighting that chess players respect — the kind where you know you're worse but you make it as difficult as possible, you make the other person work for every square, you make them prove it. Not hoping for a blunder, exactly, but hoping for something. A distraction. A noise from the bar. The jukebox changing songs. Riker dropping a glass. Anything that might make the beach glass player look away for one second.

Nothing happens. The beach glass player does not look away.

Move fifty-four. The driftwood player tips the king. Not technically checkmate — a few more moves could have been played — but the driftwood player tips the king because the game is over and everyone knows it. The king falls on its side on the cloth board, the carved crown pointing at nothing, the solemn jaw now resting on the table. The piece that took the longest to carve. The piece that mattered the most. Lying down.

The beach glass player nods. Begins resetting the pieces. Driftwood on one side, beach glass on the other. The board folds along its crease. The rubber feet cling briefly to the cloth before letting go.

Neither stranger says goodbye. The driftwood player goes to the bar and orders something. The beach glass player leaves through the aft door, into the salt air, into whatever comes next.

Riker wipes the corner table. Sets a fresh coaster down. Waits.

Someone will sit down again. Someone always does. The pieces are already set.

The ocean does what the ocean does.
