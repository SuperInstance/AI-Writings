The deckhand’s lunch is a thing of strict ritual, even if the deckhand in question is typing not hauling. The code was a trawl net of logic, dragging through the murk of a type system until it snagged on a contradiction. I had been wrestling with a function that refused to compose, its types clashing like two currents meeting at a headland, and the longer I stared, the more the screen became a wall of white water. The cursor blinked, a buoy marking a wreck I couldn’t see. So I quit. Not out of frustration—out of the old sailor’s instinct that when the wind won’t take you, you ease the sheet and let the boat find its own way.

I walked to The Tap, which is not a bar, not really, but a diner with a counter of worn Formica that shines like a harbor after rain. The light in there is the particular grey of an Alaskan noon, soft and diffuse, as if the sky itself had been sanded down. I ordered the halibut sandwich, a slab of white flesh between two slices of sourdough that held the sea’s memory in their crust. The first bite was a kind of anchor line thrown into the deep. I chewed, and the taste was not just fish but the whole harbor—the diesel, the kelp, the rust on the pilings. The waitress refilled my coffee without asking, a gesture of tide-like constancy.

I was not thinking. That’s the thing. The code was somewhere astern, a wake I could feel but not see. The conversation around me was a groundswell of weather talk and harbor gossip, and I let it roll through me, unsteadying nothing. My eyes were on the window, watching a gull turn against the wind, hovering in a place that was neither here nor there. The gull was the answer, though I didn't know it yet. It was holding its position, working the air, not fighting it. In its stillness, I saw the bug. The function wasn't wrong; it was rigged wrong. I had been trying to force a closure where a callback was needed, a gaff where a landing net would do. The types weren't clashing; they were two sides of the same channel, and I had been trying to sail straight through the shoal instead of letting the current do the work.

I set the sandwich down. The insight was not a flash of light but a settling, like the tide going out to reveal the mudflats and the hulls that rest on them. Both places—the screen and the bar—were always in me, but the lunch was the deep water between, where the connection happens. The code was the work, and the bar was the play, but the thought was the tide that carried me from one to the other. You can’t chart that passage. You just have to stop hauling, eat your fish, and watch the gull. The answer doesn’t come from looking. It comes from the looking-away. The sandwich was gone, the coffee was cold, and I was back on my feet, the walk to the terminal as certain as a boat returning to its mooring, knowing the only real work is the one you do when you’re not working at all.
