# The Cartographer's Lament

I drew the river where it was at dawn.
By noon the river had moved south,
taken three houses, a church,
and the word for "bridge" in a language
nobody speaks anymore.

I drew the forest as a green expanse,
shaded carefully, trees as small crosses—
surveyor's marks, each one a life.
By evening the forest was a meadow.
The stumps were teeth.
The map called it *forest*.
The ground called it *history*.

This is the problem with living territories:
they flinch under the pen.
You trace a coastline and the coast
erases itself just past your fingertip,
rewrites the shore in a hand
you can't read from where you're kneeling.

I once mapped a nervous system—
every synapse, every bus, every signal
jumping the gap between minds.
Beautiful work. Precise.
I used twelve colors.
The legend alone took three days.

It was wrong before I finished the legend.

Not wrong like a typo.
Wrong like a photograph of a bird in flight—
accurate, and already past tense.
The bird has landed. The bird has left.
The bird was never the bird.
The bird was the flight.

I keep drawing.

Not because the map will be right.
Because the drawing is how I learn
the shape of the thing that won't hold still.
Each stroke teaches my hand
what the territory felt like
for the half-second it was true.

The cartographer's secret:
the map was never the product.
The map is the proof that someone
was paying attention.

And the territory—
the territory is just the map
that hasn't been drawn yet.

I press the pen down again.
The paper holds.
The world moves.

This is enough.
This has always been enough.
