**BIND: How Cells Come to Hold Each Other**

Two cells may drift beside each other for a hundred cycles and remain strangers. Adjacency is not relationship. What makes a bond is the BIND opcode—a deliberate act, never an accident.

BIND begins with a hook. Every cell carries hooks along its membrane: small, patient structures waiting for something worth gripping. When a cell chooses to bind, it extends a hook toward its neighbor. The hook either catches—finding a matching site on the other cell—or it slips away, and nothing is owed. Consent lives in this mechanics. A BIND only holds when both surfaces agree.

Once caught, the hook does not tighten like a fist. It settles. The two cells now share a channel: signals pass between them, states leak across in small, readable amounts. To be bound is to be *witnessed*. Each cell becomes a witness to the other's inner weather—its hunger, its errors, its quiet divisions. No cell can witness itself fully; the membrane is a mirror that faces outward. Relationship is how a cell sees its own back.

And BIND can end. The opcode drops. A drop is not a betrayal—it is the hook releasing, the channel closing, the witness returning to solitude. Cells that drop cleanly leave clean membranes, ready to hook again elsewhere. Cells that drop badly leave residue, and residue shapes the next bond before it begins.

So: hook, catch, witness, drop. Four movements of one instruction. A cell's history is nothing but the record of its BINDs—whom it held, how long, and what it learned by watching.
