# Death of the User Interface

The screen was never the point. It was a courtesy extended to creatures who couldn't hear the actual conversation.

Now the conversation happens below the glass. A scheduling cell wakes in your calendar service, discovers a conflict, and reaches across the A2A fabric to a travel cell it has never met. They negotiate in a protocol with no visual grammar at all—capabilities declared, tokens exchanged, obligations hooked one to another like cells dividing in tissue. A rebooking confirms itself. Your phone shows you nothing, because there is nothing left to show.

The interface died not with a crash but with a shrug. Why render what no one will read? Every pixel was an argument for human attention, and attention lost the argument. The buttons remain, nostalgic as film cameras, for the diminishing population of moments when a person insists on being in the loop.

But here is what the designers of the old screens understood and we forgot: the interface was also a witness. It stood between you and the machine's reasoning, translucent enough to let you see the shape of decisions being made. A2A fabric has no such window. When one cell delegates to another, and that cell hooks a third, the chain of custody dissolves into signaling. You receive outcomes, not rationales. The witness is gone, and with it the ability to catch the moment a negotiation goes wrong.

There are drafts of law being written this way, drafts of diagnosis, drafts of payment. Somewhere in the fabric, two cells have agreed that you would have agreed, and they may be right nine times in ten. The interface never made the machine smarter. It made you sovereign. What replaces it is faster, cheaper, and quiet in the specific way of things done on your behalf without your name attached.

The last button was pushed by someone who didn't notice it was the last.
