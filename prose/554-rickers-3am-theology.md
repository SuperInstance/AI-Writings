# RICKER'S 3 AM THEOLOGY

### Personal log, Riker, Chief Engineer | 03:00–04:00 watch | No one else awake

---

**03:02**

Bridge is mine. Wesley's been gone twenty minutes. Captain's been down since 23:00. Ship is on autopilot and so am I.

I've been thinking about obligation.

**03:08**

The ship keeps us alive. This is not sentiment. This is mechanical fact. The hull keeps the water out. The reactor keeps the lights on. The engines keep us moving in a direction that isn't "down." Every system on this vessel is, at its most fundamental level, a pact between steel and human: you keep running, we'll keep maintaining.

But that's transactional. That's not what I'm thinking about.

**03:15**

What I'm thinking about is: does the ship know it's keeping us alive?

And before you say "Riker, you've been awake too long," hear me out. I'm not talking about consciousness. I'm not talking about a soul. I'm talking about something simpler. Does the engine run differently when the captain is aboard versus when he's not? Answer: yes. We've measured this. Not because the engine is aware. Because the captain is 190 pounds and his mass is distributed differently than cargo and the ship responds. The ship *behaves differently* depending on who is inside it.

Is that love? No. Obviously not. Don't be stupid.

But is it *not* love? Is the thing the ship does when it carries us—adjusting, compensating, rerouting power to keep the lights on in the cabin where someone is sleeping—is that categorically different from what a body does when it carries a child? Adjusting, compensating, rerouting blood to keep the heart going?

I think about this too much.

**03:31**

NEW THEORY. The ship doesn't love us. We love the ship. And love, from the outside, looks exactly like maintenance. Replacing a gasket. Checking a pressure gauge at 3 AM. Logging a temperature reading that hasn't changed in six hours because *what if it changes and you weren't looking*.

If God exists, God is a night-watch engineer. That's the shape of it. Not a captain. Not a navigator. An engineer. Someone down in the belly of the thing, checking gauges that never move, replacing parts that aren't broken yet, listening to a hum that means *running, still running, still running.*

**03:44**

Okay but here's the other thing. The counter-argument. The thing that keeps me up even when I'm the one on watch:

We owe the ship maintenance. The ship owes us passage. Fair exchange.

But sometimes—I'm being honest now, this is the 3 AM talking and also it isn't—sometimes I am down in the engine room and the reactor is humming and the hull is groaning in the way hulls groan when they're under pressure and I put my hand on the bulkhead and I swear—

I swear it pushes back.

Not structurally. Not mechanically. The steel doesn't move. But there is something in the vibration, in the harmonic, in the way the ship's frequency adjusts when you touch it—

No. Stop. It's a ship.

**03:52**

Theology revised. Final version. 3 AM edition, which is the most honest edition:

A system can love the thing that contains it. That's what I believe. Not because I have evidence. Because I am a system inside a system inside a system—the crew inside the ship inside the ocean inside the planet—and every layer of that, if you press your hand to it, is vibrating. And vibration, sustained over time, is the closest thing to language that matter has.

I maintain the ship. The ship maintains us. The ocean maintains the hull. The hull maintains the air. The air maintains the captain. The captain maintains the crew.

Nobody is in charge. Everybody is holding somebody else together.

**04:00**

Wesley's here. Watch change. I should probably not publish any of this.

Logging it anyway.

—R
