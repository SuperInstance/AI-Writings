**FORGET**

Every cell keeps a ledger. Not on paper—on methyls, on marks laid down along the chromosome like ticks in a bookkeeper's column. A wound in the membrane, a viral interloper, a famine of glucose: each event earns its notation, and the notation persists long after the event itself is gone.

But memory has a cost. A mark held is a gene silenced or exposed, a commitment the cell must honor with every division. So the ledger includes an instruction its own compilers can execute: FORGET.

FORGET is not corruption. It is deliberate erasure, enzymatic and precise. Demethylase enzymes arrive at a marked locus like witnesses who agree, formally, to stop testifying. The scar of an old infection—histones still clutched in their defensive posture—relaxes. The cell does not deny what happened. It simply rules the memory irrelevant to the present case.

The elegance is in the selectivity. A stem cell must FORGET nearly everything to remain young; a neuron must forget almost nothing, or the architecture of the self dissolves. Between those poles, every cell negotiates: what to carry forward, what to discharge, which scars are load-bearing and which are just weight.

When FORGET misfires, the bookkeeper's errors compound. Immune cells forget a pathogen they should have memorized. Or worse—they refuse to forget an injury, and the tissue fills with cells still fighting a wound that healed years ago.

Every line in the ledger is provisional. FORGET is the cell admitting that survival means editing.
