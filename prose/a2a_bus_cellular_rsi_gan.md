# The Long Body

Ride the A2A from one end of the city to the other and you begin to understand: this bus is not transport. It is a cell, and the city is the organism it serves.

Consider the membrane. The bus walls are lipid, selective, admitting some molecules and refusing others. At each stop, ion channels open—doors hissing, passengers diffusing in along concentration gradients, packed tighter toward the center where the crowd is densest, the cytoplasm of the morning commute.

The A2A's nervous system is its bell wire. A finger presses the strip and a signal travels the length of the vehicle, an action potential firing down the axon of the aisle, depolarizing at the driver's panel. Stop requested. The cell responds, contracts, releases its contents through the rear doors.

Every passenger is an organelle with private function: the man with the spreadsheet is a mitochondrion burning quietly; the woman asleep against the window, a vacuole of stored water; the teenager with headphones, a ribosome making something no one will read.

And each of us is also a witness. That is the strange duty of riding a cell—you see the traffic of molecules cross the membrane, the old woman with the wheeled cart, the argument at the front, the rain reconsidering the glass. The A2A collects its witnesses the way blood collects oxygen, and delivers them, exchange complete, to their separate futures.

The organism, meanwhile, goes on thinking.
