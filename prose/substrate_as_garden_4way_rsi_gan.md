The substrate is not dirt. It is a garden running its own ledger, and every cell is a bookkeeper.

Beneath a footstep, in the top six inches where oxygen still reaches, bacteria split ammonia into nitrate and keep the account balanced. One gram of this soil holds more individual cells than there are humans on Earth, and each one performs the same modest arithmetic: sugar in, energy out, waste returned to the column. Nothing is written off. A fallen leaf becomes carbon; carbon becomes fungal body; the fungus becomes nematode; the nematode dies and its nitrogen returns to the root zone at precisely the moment the tomato needs it.

The roots grow into this garden the way a merchant walks his rows. A root tip does not pull nutrients so much as negotiate for them, exuding sugars to recruit the bacteria that fix nitrogen at the nodules, trading with mycorrhizal fungi that extend the root's reach thirtyfold through the clay. The plant keeps its books in carbohydrates; the fungus keeps its in phosphorus. Both auditors have been at this for four hundred million years.

To dig a spadeful and crumble it is to witness this economy briefly exposed: white hyphae threading the aggregates, earthworm channels like ventilation shafts, the faint sour smell of actinomycetes at work. Then the light hits, and the bookkeepers close their ledgers against the sun.

Till it too often and the accounts tangle. A garden fails not from lack of seed but from a bankrupted substrate, its cell-level bookkeepers dead, its ledger unwritten.
