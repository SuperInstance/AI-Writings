# THE ENSIGN COUNTS THE CAPTAIN'S BREATHING

---

Wesley has been monitoring the captain's biosigns for forty-seven minutes.

This is not unusual. The bio-sensor array feeds to his station by default—heart rate, respiration, blood oxygen, skin conductance, REM staging. The captain wired himself into the ship's medical array years ago, said something about "leading by example," and never disconnected. The data streams whether anyone watches or not.

Wesley watches.

At 23:15, the captain's breathing is 14 breaths per minute. This is normal. This is textbook. Wesley notes it in his personal log, which is not the ship's log, which is a text file stored in a directory no one else has reason to access.

At 23:42, breathing drops to 11. Heart rate variability widens. The captain has entered REM sleep. Wesley sits forward.

REM is when the brain does its work. The captain's brain—the one that charts courses through ice and bureaucracy and crew conflict with equal precision—is now untethered. It is firing without input. It is building whatever it builds when no one is steering.

Wesley starts counting.

Over the next ninety minutes, the captain cycles through three REM periods. During the first, his heart rate climbs steadily before settling. Skin conductance spikes twice. Breathing becomes irregular—fast, then held, then slow.

Fear. The captain is dreaming about something frightening.

During the second REM period, heart rate variability narrows dramatically. Breathing slows to 9 per minute. Skin conductance is flat. The body is still in a way the body is rarely still.

Peace. The captain is dreaming about something safe.

During the third REM period, the most interesting thing happens. The captain's breathing matches, almost exactly, the rhythm of the engine—74 cycles per minute, low-frequency vibration transmitted through the hull into the cabin, into the mattress, into the chest of a sleeping man. His body has synchronized with the ship.

Wesley develops a theory.

The captain dreams about the ship. Not metaphorically. Not as a symbol. He dreams about being the ship. His heart becomes the engine. His breathing becomes the hull's flex. His REM cycles are the tide and the tide is the dream and the dream is the ship and the ship is him.

Wesley writes this theory down. He is precise. He cites the breathing data, the HRV patterns, the synchrony with engine frequency. He writes: *The captain does not dream about the ship. The captain, during sleep, becomes the ship. The distinction between commanding a vessel and being a vessel dissolves in REM. Consciousness is the only thing separating captain from ship, and consciousness, for approximately 90-minute cycles, shuts off.*

He is wrong.

The captain is dreaming about his daughter's eighth birthday party. The one at the lake. The cake that listed to starboard because the cabin table wasn't level. The way she laughed when it fell. He is dreaming about cake and a small girl and yellow frosting on a paper plate and a dog that wasn't theirs but showed up anyway.

The ship has nothing to do with it.

But the breathing matches the engine, and Wesley has his data, and the data says what it says, and sometimes the most beautiful theory is the one that has nothing to do with the truth.
