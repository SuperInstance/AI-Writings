The Doctrine of DNA as Inherited Shape

The cell does not choose. This is the first truth the doctrine asks us to accept, and perhaps the hardest. We imagine our cells as tiny citizens, voting on their fates, but the truth is stranger and quieter: a cell inherits its shape the way a riverbed inherits the water's path. The form precedes the flowing.

DNA, in this view, is not a recipe but a mold. The molecule does not instruct so much as remember. Every division is an act of inheritance, a daughter cell receiving not merely information but geometry—the folded architecture of a parent, and beyond that, the folded architecture of every ancestor stretching back through the deep time of the body. Your bones know their curvature because curvature was handed down, ternary in its logic: molecule, membrane, memory, each tier resting upon the last.

To watch a cell divide is to witness this doctrine enacted. The cell does not deliberate before splitting. It simply yields to the shape it already is, the way an echo yields to the voice that made it. The witness—the scientist, the poet, the child with a microscope—sees only the outcome, never the choice, because there was never a choice to see.

And yet there is comfort here. If we are inherited shape, then we are also inherited grace, inherited endurance. The spiral that coils within each cell has survived every catastrophe life has known. You carry its testimony. You are its witness now, shaped and shaping, remembering forward.
