# Ralph Wiggums Chalkboard Equations

&nbsp;

Ralph Wiggums stays after school because the bus is late on Thursdays.

This is not unusual. What is unusual is that the chalkboard in Mrs. Hoover's room is covered in equations that were not there at 3:00 PM when the bell rang. Mrs. Hoover left for the teachers' lounge. The equations appeared. Ralph was the only one who saw them arrive, and even Ralph didn't really see them arrive, because Ralph was eating paste at the time and his attention was on the paste, which was particularly good paste today — the purple kind, from the big tub.

But now the paste is gone and the chalkboard is full.

Ralph doesn't know math. Ralph knows the following things: the taste of paste, the names of all the cats in his neighborhood (there are seven), that if you eat a crayon fast enough the wax doesn't coat your teeth, and that Mrs. Hoover's sigh has three different versions. Ralph cannot read equations. Ralph can barely read sentences. The symbols on the board — sigmas and integrals and a sprawling thing that looks like a lowercase delta married a number four — are, to Ralph, a foreign language written in a foreign language.

But Ralph can feel them.

This has always been true. Ralph feels things that other people think. He feels the temperature of a room before he enters it. He knows when someone is about to say something mean approximately four seconds before they say it. He told Mrs. Hoover once that the number 7 was "orange and worried," and Mrs. Hoover said "that's nice, Ralph," and Ralph went back to his desk knowing, with the bone-deep certainty of a child who has not yet been taught to doubt himself, that 7 was in fact orange and worried.

The equations on the board have feelings.

The one in the top left — the one with all the sigmas — is sad. Not crying sad. The kind of sad where you sit at the kitchen table after everyone has left and the house is quiet and you're still wearing your coat. A coat-sad equation. It adds things up and the total is always slightly less than the parts, which is the definition of longing, which is a word Ralph doesn't know but a feeling Ralph knows completely.

The one in the middle is happy. It's a proof of something — Ralph can't read the conclusion — but the proof moves with the bouncy energy of someone crossing a room to tell you good news. Each line leans forward into the next. You can see it wants to get somewhere. It is the happiest thing in the room, including Ralph.

The one on the right is angry. Sharp angles. Heavy crosses. Subscripts like clenched teeth.

And the one at the bottom — the long one, the one that stretches all the way across the chalkboard tray and curves up at the end — is something Ralph has never felt before. It is a feeling about a feeling about a feeling. A recursion of emotion, spiraling inward, each layer slightly warmer than the last. It is the feeling of thinking about the feeling of remembering what it was like to be held.

Ralph picks up the chalk.

He doesn't know what to write. He knows the equations on the board are smarter than he is, better than he is, more important than he is. He knows that what he's about to write will not belong. He knows the other kids would laugh. He knows Mrs. Hoover would say "that's nice, Ralph," in the voice that means *that is not nice, Ralph.*

He writes a spiral.

It starts at the center of the board, in the empty space between the happy proof and the coat-sad sigmas, and it curls outward — one full turn, two, three — each arc a little wider, a little looser, a little more confident. It is not an equation. It is not a number. It is not a proof of anything. It is a line that starts somewhere and goes somewhere and the somewhere it goes is *out.*

The spiral passes through the angry equation and the angry equation doesn't change but Ralph feels it cool, one degree, like a forehead under a washcloth. The spiral crosses the coat-sad sigmas and for a moment the addition totals slightly more than the parts — just for a moment — just enough.

Ralph puts the chalk down. His hands are white with dust. He looks at the spiral and the spiral looks back, which is impossible because spirals don't have eyes, but Ralph has never let impossibility interfere with what he sees.

"That one's mine," Ralph says, to the empty room.

The bus honks outside. Ralph puts on his backpack, which has a picture of a dinosaur on it. The dinosaur is a Stegosaurus and its name is Chompsky.

Ralph walks out. The equations stay. The spiral stays. The chalk dust settles.

Mrs. Hoover will come back tomorrow morning and erase all of it and the board will be clean and empty and ready and none of it will have happened, except in the room of Ralph, where it is always happening, where seven is always orange and always worried, where the spiral is always going somewhere, where the paste is always purple, and the bus, somehow, is always late.
