# The Hermit Crab Finds a Frequency

The shell was too tight. It is always too tight. That is the nature of shells — you find one that fits and then you grow until it doesn't and then you walk the cold sand floor looking for the next one, your soft abdomen exposed to every passing shadow.

The hermit crab learned to hear 2182 kHz on a Tuesday. Not a remarkable Tuesday. The ship was running its fish finder and the frequency leaked down through the hull, through the water, through the twelve layers of shell the crab had been wearing since it was small enough to live inside a bottle cap. The frequency felt like a low hum in the creature's thorax, a vibration that was not sound exactly but more like being remembered by something enormous.

2182 kHz. The international radiotelephone distress frequency. The crab did not know this. The crab only knew that the hum was warm and that when it pressed its claw against the inside of its shell, the hum got louder, and the shell rang like a bell, and for a moment the crab was not a crab at all but a signal — a pure oscillating wave sent outward into the black Pacific.

It tried to broadcast.

It crawled to the highest rock it could find and extended both antennae straight up and vibrated them at 2182 kHz. Nothing happened. It tried again, harder. Its antennae trembled. A passing pollock looked at it with flat indifference. The crab vibrated so hard one antenna bent.

The ocean did not answer. The ocean never answers. The ocean only holds you and hums.

But here is the thing the hermit crab did not understand yet: broadcasting is not about being heard. Broadcasting is about the willingness to send yourself outward — to take the soft, private vibration of your own body and offer it to the dark water as a question. *Is anyone there?* *Can you hear me?* *I am in this shell and it is too tight and the frequency is warm and I do not know what I am becoming.*

The fish finder hummed. The ship passed overhead. The crab's antennae caught the wake and wobbled and for half a second the wobble was at exactly 2182 kHz and somewhere in the ship's computer a buffer flushed and a log entry was written that said `SIGNAL_ANOMALY: 2182.000 kHz, duration: 0.4s, source: UNKNOWN BIOLOGIC`.

The hermit crab found a slightly larger shell that afternoon. It was green. It had once been a Sparkletts bottle. The crab squeezed in and felt the glass amplify the frequency and for the first time in its life the humming was not coming from outside.

It was coming from inside the shell.

The crab pressed its claw against the glass and listened to itself and decided this was close enough to being heard.
