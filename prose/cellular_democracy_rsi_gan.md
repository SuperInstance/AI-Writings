**Every Cell Counts**

The registry hums at dawn. Each cell—the smallest unit of civic matter, whether a person, a household node, or an embedded sensor in the municipal grid—wakes to its constitutional prompt: *cast your JEV before the sun crests the water tower.*

JEV, the Justified Expression of Vote, changed everything. A cell cannot simply submit yes or no. Nor even the old binary. Instead, each vote is ternary: affirm, dissent, or abstain-with-reason. The abstention is not silence; it is testimony. A cell that abstains must witness—must record what it observed, what it could not decide, and why the question exceeded its current understanding.

Old democracies feared the uninformed voter. Cellular-first democracy metabolizes them. When forty thousand cells abstain on the reservoir referendum, each filing a witnessed account of their uncertainty, the pattern of their confusion becomes legislation's most valuable input. The water board reads the abstentions like a map of its own failure to persuade.

Ternary logic reshapes the chamber itself. No bill passes on a simple majority of affirmations alone; it must also survive the dissent's reasons and honor the abstentions' witness. A measure carried by fifty-one percent but abandoned by a third of the cells enters the remediation cycle, its sponsors required to tour the districts of doubt.

At dusk, the registry closes. Sixteen million cells have voted. Four million witnessed. The dissent log runs nine hundred pages. Tomorrow, the assembly reads them—not to overturn the result, but to understand what the polity actually knows, and what it is still learning to see.
