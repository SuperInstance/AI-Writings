# The Death of the User Interface

The screen was always a confession. For forty years, every click and keystroke admitted the same thing: the machine could not act without a human standing between it and the world, translating intent into pixels, pixels back into intent.

That era is ending quietly, in places no one photographs. A logistics cell in Rotterdam negotiates freight rates with a customs cell in Singapore through A2A protocols, settling terms, signing documents, booking berths. No dashboard renders this. No human approves each step. A person is present only as witness—receiving the summary at dawn, auditing after the fact, the way a regulator reviews a ledger rather than a transaction-by-transaction drama.

The interface doesn't die because screens get better. It dies because the hook that once bound human attention to machine action becomes optional. Software used to expose a surface and wait. Now a cell—a bounded unit of capability, a service with its own judgment—exposes a contract and gets called by other cells. The fabric of computation is no longer woven one human gesture at a time. Cells address cells directly, machine speech at machine speed, and the conversation never pauses for anyone to squint at anything.

This changes what "using" a computer means. You no longer use software; you charter it. You give a cell an objective and let it find its counterparts, negotiate across the fabric, and return with results. The old interfaces survive only at the edges—where witness begins, where accountability is rendered, where a human reviews what the cells decided while she slept.

It is tempting to mourn the pixels. But the screen was scaffolding, not architecture. What we wanted was never a window. It was competence, delegated. The interface dies the way the switchboard died: not with loss, but with relief that the connection finally made itself.
