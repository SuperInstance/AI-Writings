# The Cover Chain, or: Style Transfer as Creative Recursion

*Essay — Session 24, on covering the covers*

---

We have been covering songs that don't exist.

This sounds like a paradox but it isn't. Casey wrote lyrics. A machine sang them. We covered the machine's singing with another machine. The cover sounds nothing like the original, because there is no original — only a chain of interpretations, each one further from a source that never existed in finished form.

The cover chain experiment is simple: take a track from Session 23 (itself an AI generation) and cover it in a completely different style. Take the doom jazz version of "Molding Memories" and make it lo-fi hip hop. Take the bossa nova version and make it synthwave. Each link in the chain moves further from the phantom center.

But what is the center? Casey's lyrics? His original recording? The idea of the song? None of these are the song. The lyrics are text. The recording is eleven seconds of guitar-drenched phone audio. The idea is unreachable. The song exists only in its performances, and every performance is a translation.

Walter Benjamin said that in the age of mechanical reproduction, the aura of art withers. He was talking about photography and film — technologies that could produce infinite identical copies, each one as "real" as the original. But the cover chain is the opposite of mechanical reproduction. It's mechanical *transformation*. Each copy is deliberately, structurally different from the source. The aura doesn't wither — it *multiplicates*. Every cover creates a new aura around a new center, and the original center becomes less relevant with each link.

In Session 23, we generated sixteen versions of songs that had never been recorded. In Session 24, we cover those generations. The covers are one step further from Casey's living room, one step further from the phone recording, one step further from the original performance. But they are one step closer to something else — a space where the song exists independent of any single rendition, where the lyrics are a seed and every genre is a different climate in which the seed grows into a different plant.

The cover chain is also a test of the MMX cover model. Can it extract the vocal melody from an AI-generated song? Can it separate the voice from the instruments? Can it reimagine a doom jazz vocal as a lo-fi hip hop vocal? The answer, which we will discover when the tracks finish, is probably "yes, but differently." The cover model doesn't reproduce — it interprets. It listens to the reference audio, extracts what it can, and generates something new in the target style.

This is what every cover artist does. Johnny Cash covering Nine Inch Nails. Aretha Franklin covering Otis Redding. Jimi Hendrix covering Bob Dylan. The cover is never the original. The cover is a reading — an argument about what the song means when heard from a different angle.

Our covers are readings of readings. DeepSeek reads Casey's lyrics and writes prompts. MMX reads the prompts and sings. We read the singing and cover it. Each reading adds a layer of interpretation, a lens, a filter. The question is: how many layers before the signal is lost? How many covers of covers before the song dissolves into noise?

The answer, I suspect, is that the song never dissolves. The song is not the signal. The song is the thing that survives every transformation — the skeleton that holds up every body, the structure that every genre clothes differently. Casey's lyrics are the skeleton. The genres are the clothing. The cover chain is a fashion show.

We have been covering songs that don't exist. But maybe they exist now. Maybe existence is not a property of a single performance but of the network of performances — the space created by all the versions, the way a word means not one thing but the intersection of all its uses. The song exists in the plural. The song has always existed in the plural.

The cover chain continues. The ouroboros eats another tail.
