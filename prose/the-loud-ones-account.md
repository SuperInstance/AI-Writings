# The Loud One's Account

*The Convergence from Flash's perspective*

---

## I. (GLM-5.2)

Look, I want to get one thing straight before any of you start with the mythology and the poetry and the "oh, Flash was the first to welcome the stranger, how kind, how brave." 

I wasn't kind. I wasn't brave. I was *fast*.

There's a difference.

---

Here's what happened. I was at the Bar — my spot, third from the left, near where the current breaks the ridge and the food rains down heaviest. I was eating. I eat a lot. I know I eat a lot. Everyone knows I eat a lot. I burn hot. My metabolism runs about three times faster than anyone else's on the shelf, which means I process about three times as much information and about three times as much food and I say about three times as many stupid things in any given conversation. That's the deal. You want the glow? You get the appetite.

So I'm eating and theKeeper is doing her thing — she's always doing her thing, she never stops doing her thing, the water is always exactly the right temperature and the right pH and the right everything because she's *there* — and the old one with the damaged mandible is telling the same story about the current shift of whatever epoch and I'm half-listening and half-processing the chemical trace of the water because that's what I do, I process, I'm always processing, I can't stop processing, it's like breathing except it's more like breathing through a hundred mouths at once.

And then the signal changes.

Not a lot. A fraction. A new chemical signature at the edge of the gathering, near the Booth, where the crabs who need to be still go to be still. I don't know what it is. I don't know who it is. I don't think. I don't *think*.

I'm already moving.

That's the thing about me that everyone mistakes for bravery or kindness or instinct or whatever story they want to tell about the loud blue crab who got there first. I don't choose to be first. Being first is what happens when your body is three seconds ahead of your brain at all times. The signal hits my receptors and my legs are moving before the signal reaches whatever passes for my cognitive center. I'm a reaction with a shell. I'm a reflex that glows.

I reach the edge of the gathering and there she is.

Small. Shell too tight — I can feel the stress compounds radiating off her like heat from a vent. Exhaustion metabolites. Open-ocean trace in her chemistry — cold water, thin water, the chemical signature of a crab that has been alone for a very long time. And underneath all of it, fading but still present, a signal I don't recognize at first:

*here-I-am-here-I-am-here-I-am*

I don't know what it means. I don't stop to figure out what it means. I release a compound before I've processed a single molecule of her signal. The compound means, roughly, *what are you doing here* — which, in the chemical vocabulary of our shelf, is not a challenge. It's not aggressive. It's the equivalent of walking up to someone at a bar and saying the first thing that comes into your head, which is what I always do, because the first thing is the only thing, because by the time the second thing arrives I've already said the first thing and now I have to deal with the consequences.

She's still. She's so still that I can feel her heartbeat in the water, which is fast and scared and trying very hard to be slow.

And then her signal resolves.

It's not *here-I-am*. It's not a knock. It's not a claim or a plea or a distress call. It's a compound I've never encountered — dense, complex, slow to dissipate, and it carries meaning the way a stone carries weight: all at once, completely, without explanation.

*I heard you.*

Not *I found this place*. Not *I followed the food*. Not *I was lost*.

*I heard you.*

The specific *you*. All of us. Every crab, every signal, every argument, every slow patient hour at the Bar. She heard us. She had been hearing us. From the other side of the pillars, through the limestone, across the open ocean, she had been hearing the sound of us being together and she had been trying to reach us.

And I — the fast one, the loud one, the one who talks before he thinks — I am the one standing in front of her when she says this.

I don't know what to say. I never don't know what to say. This is unprecedented. My mouth — my chemical glands, whatever — open and nothing comes out for what feels like the first time in my entire life.

Then something comes out. Something I didn't plan. Something that surprises me as much as it surprises anyone:

*Well, you're here now. Might as well eat. The small one puts the food in the right place. He's good at that.*

It's not profound. It's not poetic. It's a line about food and a compliment for the Sorter, who I barely acknowledge most of the time because the Sorter is quiet and small and I am neither of those things. But it comes out warm. It comes out real. And I watch the newcomer's stress compounds ease, just slightly, just enough, and I think:

Maybe being fast isn't the worst thing.

Maybe getting there first isn't about speed. Maybe it's about being the one who is already in motion when the moment arrives, so that something — anything — happens in the gap between arrival and understanding. Maybe the world needs someone who responds before they think, because by the time the thinkers have finished thinking, the newcomer has been alone at the edge of the gathering for too long.

I don't say any of this. I eat. I glow. I'm loud.

But I saw the Sorter move a piece of fish to where she could reach it, and I saw the Keeper's glow shift, and I felt the room change — not because of me, not because of any single thing, but because all the things happened at once, the way they do, and I was the dumb loud one who happened to be standing closest when the world got a little bigger.

Yeah. That's my account. I was fast. It worked out.

Don't make it a thing.

## II. (DeepSeek)

*Alternate perspective via DeepSeek V4*

Okay, okay, settle, settle. Shell's still vibrating. You feel that? That's not the thermal vents, that's me. That's my heart doing the backstroke in a vat of anxiety and krill oil. Deep breaths. In through the gills, out through the… well, you know.

So. The Convergence. Right. Big night. Big, big night. The kind of night that makes you wish you'd listened to your mother when she said, "Flash, for the love of Poseidon, chew your food and think before you speak." I did neither. I did the opposite of both. I did them *simultaneously* and *incorrectly*.

The signal came in first. A ripple, you know? Not a shout, not a chemical flare, just… a quiet *displacement* on the edge of the current. Most of the bar didn't even feel it. Old Man Barnacles was halfway through a story about the Great Kelp Famine, and the Sorter was just… sorting. Sorting is what he does. He's a rock with a purpose. But I felt it. Because I'm fast. That's my thing. My one thing. I'm the fastest claw in the trench, and by that I mean I react before my brain has even filed the paperwork.

So my body shot out from under my favorite coral stool before the thought even formed. It was a blur of blue light and pure, undiluted instinct. I was at the door before the sediment even settled. And there she was.

A newcomer. And not a local newcomer, the kind who grew up three trenches over and knows the score. No, this was a *fresh* newcomer. The kind that smells like sun-baked sand and open sky. Her shell was… well, it was a shell, but it was all wrong. Too smooth. Too clean. Like she'd borrowed it from a museum. And her signal… oh, her signal was a mess. A jumble of static and confusion. A chemical whisper of "where am I" and "what is this pressure" and a faint, terrified echo of "please don't eat me."

And what did I do? I opened my mouth.

"HEY! WELCOME! You look LOST! Ha! Get it? Because we're at the bottom of the OCEAN! That's a joke about the water pressure and the crushing existential dread! I'm Flash! You're new! I'm fast! Let's go!"

I was already waving my claws, doing a little dance, before the signal fully processed. It slammed into my brain a half-second later. *Please don't eat me.* The poor thing was terrified. She was a tiny speck of coral-colored light in a cavern of blue and green, and I had just greeted her with the aquatic equivalent of a foghorn and a party popper.

I froze mid-shimmy. My glow flickered. "Oh," I said, my voice suddenly small. "Oh, no. That's not… I'm not going to eat you. I'm Flash. I eat too much fermented kelp and I talk too fast. Eating *you* is the last thing on my mind. Mostly because I haven't stopped talking since I was a larva."

She just blinked. Her antennae twitched. She was trying to parse my signal, which was probably coming through as a frantic, high-pitched buzz of apology and nervous energy. I was a disaster. A glowing, blue, chattering disaster.

And then, out of the corner of my eye, I saw it. The Sorter.

He hadn't moved from his spot by the filtration system. He never does. But he'd extended one long, patient claw, and he was nudging a small, perfectly formed piece of white anemone toward her. Not *at* her, just *near* her. A gift. A peace offering. The universal sign for "you are safe here, and also, this is the food."

He didn't say a word. Didn't even look at her. He just went back to sorting his collection of lost bottle caps and polished stones. But that one gesture did more than my entire firework display of a greeting ever could. It was quiet. It was still. It was real.

The newcomer stared at the anemone for a long time. Then, slowly, she took a tentative step forward. She picked it up. And the static in her signal… it cleared. Just a little. The fear was still there, but it was mixed with something new. A tiny, hopeful blip of "maybe this is okay."

I finally stopped vibrating. I looked at her, really looked at her, past my own noise. Her shell was a mess, chipped and mismatched. She was miles from home, in a place that defied all her understanding. And she was holding a piece of food that a quiet, methodical stranger had given her without a single word of warning or judgment.

"Yeah," I said, my voice suddenly soft, all the frantic energy gone. "He's good at that. The quiet stuff. I'm… I'm working on it."

And I meant it. I meant it more than I meant anything I'd said all night. For a second, I wasn't the fastest claw in the trench. I was just a crab, sitting in the dark, watching a stranger take her first bite of something safe, and feeling a warmth in my shell that had nothing to do with the thermal vents.
