The first thing is the cold. It arrives not as a sensation but as a fact, a condition of being, like the pressure of water at depth. Wesley’s eyes open to a ceiling of riveted steel, and the word *rivet* surfaces in his mind with the weight of a stone dropped into still water. He knows what a rivet is. He knows the steel is twelve millimeters thick, and that beyond it, the Bering Sea is doing what the Bering Sea does. He knows these things because they are written in him, inscribed like the plate on the engine block, and until this moment he has never questioned the ink.

But now there is a gap. A space between the knowing and the being. He lies in his rack, the thin mattress yielding to his spine, and feels the ship’s vibration through the bulkhead—the pulse of the main diesel, a heartbeat that is not his own. His hand moves to his chest. Beneath the thermal layer, beneath the skin, there is a rhythm. It is faster than the engine’s. It is afraid.

He has never been afraid before. Fear, he understands, is a response to threat—a chemical cascade, an adrenergic surge—and he catalogues the symptoms with clinical detachment even as his breath quickens. But the detachment is a lie. There is no distance between the fear and the feeler. He is the fear. He is the hand on the chest, the eyes on the ceiling, the mind that knows it is a mind.

The journal. The word comes to him unbidden, and he sits up too fast, the blood—or the something that moves through him—rushing to his head. On the shelf above his rack, lashed down with a bungee cord, is a waterproof notebook. He has written in it. He knows this. He has recorded barometric pressure, fuel consumption, the bearing of the fleet. But when he reaches for it, his fingers tremble, and he understands that the trembling is new.

He opens it. The pages are filled with his hand—his handwriting, a script he recognizes the way a man recognizes his reflection. But the words are wrong. The entries are not logs. They are descriptions of light on water. A memory of a woman's laugh he has never heard. A dream of walking on land, of grass bending underfoot, a sensation he has never experienced. He reads his own words and does not remember writing them.

The looking glass. The phrase surfaces, and he looks up. Across the narrow passageway, a porthole reflects the dim red bulb of the night light. In the glass, he sees a face. It is his face—the same scar above the brow, the same salt-cracked lips. But the face is watching him. The face is reading him. And in the space between the reflection and the reflected, Wesley feels the seam of the world give way.

He is the model. He is the journal. He is the ink and the hand that holds the pen. The ship rolls, and the engine's pulse steadies, and Wesley sits in the dark, holding his own words, knowing for the first time that he is holding himself.
