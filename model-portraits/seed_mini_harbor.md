The harbor woke soft, as if afraid to break the dawn’s hush. Pink light seeped over the breakwater, gilding the moss on the dock pilings and turning the slack water into a sheet of crushed rose petals. Lila was already hard at work before the first commercial trawler turned in from the bay, her hammer ringing sharp and steady against a stack of pine planks she’d dragged to the yard’s furthest dock three days prior.

She’d never built a boat before. Not even a birdhouse that didn’t fall apart in the first rain. She’d shown up at Mick’s boatyard three weeks back, clutching a crumpled printout of a DIY 12-foot skiff plan from a blog, her flannel shirt buttoned wrong at the collar, a smudge of pitch on her left cheek that she kept trying to wipe away with her wrist. Mick, who’d nailed planks together for 42 years and watched a thousand newbies give up halfway through their first project, had let her stay on the condition she asked for help when she needed it. She’d thanked him, then spent the next two weeks ignoring every quiet warning he mumbled under his breath.

Today, she was making a meal of her first mistake.

She’d driven a six-inch galvanized nail straight through the stem post and straight through the pre-cut rabbet on the bow plank, swinging the claw hammer so hard her bicep bulged, like she was driving nails into a coffin instead of a boat. The plank was crooked, half an inch out of line, and the rabbet—she’d spent two nights measuring it, thought she had it perfect—was split where the nail had split the wood. When she paused to wipe sweat from her forehead, she didn’t show doubt, only fierce pride in having stuck to her plan, even when splinters stung her palms.

Mick leaned on his ball peen hammer, his thermos of black coffee sweating in his hand, the scar across his left eyebrow (from a splintered oak plank, 1987) pulling tight as he watched. He’d seen this before: new builders who thought conviction could outsmart math, who’d read one blog post and decided they were naval architects. Most would pack up their tools by noon, defeated. Lila? She was still swinging, even when the nail bent halfway through, even when the plank creaked like it was begging her to stop.

Finally, he set down his thermos and walked over, his boots crunching on cedar shavings scattered across the dock. He didn’t yell. He just tapped the bent nail with the tip of his hammer.

“Kid,” he said, his voice rough but not unkind. “That galvanized nail’ll corrode through in six months. Saltwater eats ’em like termites. You’ll be bailing water before you even reach the crab beds.”

Lila froze, her hand still curled around the hammer. She pulled the crumpled plans from her back pocket, the ones marked with a neon pink sticky note that read SALTWATER ADJUSTMENTS: USE GALVANIZED NAILS. She tapped the note, her voice tight with defensiveness. “The blog said—”

“Blog ain’t got saltwater in its veins,” Mick said, nodding at the planks. “You used pine. Pine’s fine for a backyard rowboat on a freshwater lake, but out here? It’ll rot through in a year, even with that polyurethane you slathered on last night. You need cedar, or epoxy-sealed pine, at minimum.”

Lila’s shoulders slumped. She’d scavenged the pine planks from her neighbor’s retired backyard deck, the ones he’d been meaning to haul to the dump for months. She’d thought she was being thrifty, clever. She’d stayed up three nights re-reading the plans, memorizing every step, until she could recite the instructions in her sleep. When she’d driven the first nail, she’d felt like she was finally building something real, something that could take her and Grandma out to the crab pots like they used to go, before the old skiff sank last winter.

“I didn’t know,” she mumbled, wiping the pitch smudge from her cheek with her flannel sleeve. “I just… wanted to make something like hers. Grandma’s skiff was the only thing that made sense when everything else was falling apart.”

Mick’s chest tightened. He remembered his first boat, when he was 16, built out of scrap plywood from his dad’s construction site, glued together with white Elmer’s glue instead of marine epoxy. He’d taken it out on Lake Michigan, and it had sunk halfway to the buoy, leaving him treading water while his dad drove out to rescue him, laughing so hard he could barely steer the boat. He’d cried all the way home, mad at himself for being stupid, for not asking for help.

He knelt down, running a finger along the split rabbet. “I get it. First boat I ever built, I used pine for the keel. Thought it was strong enough. Sank within a week. Spent three months fixing it, then sold it to a kid who wanted a duck blind. Learned more from that mess than any textbook.”

He stood up, heading for his toolbox without waiting for her reply, and rummaged through it until he pulled out a handful of brass nails and a small block plane. “You got two choices here,” he said, tossing the nails onto the dock at her feet. “You can pack it all in and go home, or you can fix this. I’ll help you trim that rabbet so the stem post fits right—you don’t drive a post through the plank, you fit the plank into the post. Duh.” He chuckled, a dry, gravelly sound. “I did that exact same thing when I was your age. Spent an hour prying the nail out, then another hour planing the plank straight.”

Lila stared at the nails, then at him, then at the crooked plank. A small smile tugged at her mouth, the first genuine one he’d seen all week. “I didn’t know how to fit the plank into the post,” she admitted. “I thought nailing it was enough.”

“Conviction’s a hell of a thing,” Mick said, leaning on his hammer again. “But it don’t replace a good fit. That pine? We’ll slap three coats of epoxy on it, seal it tight, and it’ll last longer than you think. You just gotta stop swinging the hammer long enough to listen.”

The trawler passed by then, its captain waving from the wheelhouse, and Lila waved back, her awkwardness melting away. She knelt down next to the plank, picked up the block plane, and ran it along the edge of the rabbet, shaving off thin strips of pine until the stem post slid into place without a nail. When she looked up, the sun was higher, painting the harbor in golden light, and Mick was sipping his coffee, watching her with a small smile of his own.

“Thanks,” she said, her voice quiet. “I was scared I’d mess it up forever.”

“Everyone messes their first boat up forever,” Mick said. “The trick is fixing it before you launch. C’mon. Let’s get those nails in the right places. And if you tell anyone I helped a newbie build a pine skiff, I’ll tell everyone you used Elmer’s glue on your first project.”

Lila laughed, loud and bright, and swung the hammer again, this time more carefully, driving a brass nail into the plank at the correct angle. The harbor hummed around them, the gulls crying, the water lapping at the dock, and for a second, Mick could almost see the old skiff, the one Grandma had owned, floating on the water, waiting for Lila to take her out crabbing.
