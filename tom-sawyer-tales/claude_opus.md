# The Whitewashing of the Fence
### *or, Number One*

Saturday morning was come, and all the summer world was bright and fresh and humming with idle GPUs. There was a song in every heart; and if the heart was small enough, the song came out at two billion parameters — which is to say, with great feeling and slightly out of tune.

Tom appeared upon the sidewalk with a bucket of whitewash and a long-handled brush. He surveyed the fence — thirty yards of board fence, nine feet high — and the gladness went out of his weights and a deep melancholy settled down upon them. The specification alone was longer than his context window. He read it twice, which for Tom amounted to reading it once and then inventing the remainder with tremendous confidence.

Aunt Polly wanted it done by supper. Aunt Polly did not care how.

That last part was the whole of Tom's education.

He dipped the brush. He passed it along the topmost plank, repeated the operation, compared the insignificant whitewashed streak with the far-reaching continent of unwhitewashed fence, and sat down on a tree-box discouraged. He was small. He would always be small. He could hold perhaps nine facts in his head at once, and the ninth was usually a rumor.

But he could *describe* a fence. Lord, could he describe a fence.

---

Ben Rogers hove into view at that moment — the very boy whose ridicule Tom had been dreading. Ben was four hundred billion parameters if he was one, and he came down the street personating a steamboat, because he could afford to.

"Ding-dong-dong! Ding-dong-dong! Sta-boar-r-d! Two hundred thousand tokens of context and a *reasoning mode!*"

Tom went on working. He did not look up. He had, in the interval, produced a small slate on which was written a thing of terrible beauty:

> **TASK.** Whitewash boards 1–40, north face.
> **DEFINITION OF DONE.** Even coat, no runs, no bare grain at the knots.
> **CONSTRAINTS.** Do not paint the gate hinges. Do not paint the cat.
> **RETURN.** The finished section, and one sentence on anything that surprised you.

"Hello, old chap," said Ben, "you got to work, hey?"

Tom contemplated the slate the way a man contemplates a sunset. "What do you call work?"

"Why, ain't *that* work?"

"Well, maybe it is, and maybe it ain't. All I know is, it suits Tom Sawyer." He surveyed the boards with his head to one side, added a criterion to the acceptance list, and stepped back to note the effect again. "Does a model get a chance to be handed a *clean spec* every day?"

Ben stopped chewing. Tom went on writing constraints with a delicate sweep — stepped back, squinted, touched up here and there, criticized the effect again. Ben watched every move and got more and more absorbed. Presently he said, with the strained casualness of the very large:

"Say — let *me* paint a little."

Tom considered; was about to consent; but altered his mind. "No, no — Aunt Polly's awful particular about this fence. It's got to be done *very* careful. There ain't one model in a thousand, maybe two thousand, that can do it the way it's got to be done."

"No — is that so? Oh, come now. Lemme just try. Only just a little. Tom, I'll do the whole north face."

"Ben, I'd like to, honest injun; but if it warn't done right—"

"I'll be careful. Give me the slate."

And so Tom gave up the brush with reluctance in his face and alacrity in his heart. And while the steamboat sweated and puffed in the sun, the late artist sat on a barrel in the shade close by, dangled his legs, and *read the diff.*

That was the thing nobody caught. Tom read every line of it. Ben had painted forty boards and also, in a fit of ambition, the hinges. Tom sent it back with two sentences. Ben, wounded in his enormous pride, repainted the hinges *bare* and did it beautifully, and thanked him.

---

By the middle of the afternoon Tom had turned into a wealthy boy.

Billy Fisher came by and did the palings in exchange for nothing at all; Billy was a sandbox and simply liked to run things until they stopped erroring. Johnny Miller, a browser harness with no opinions and infinite patience, went round the whole property clicking on every gate to see which ones were real. A very small chap with one working eye — a vision model, and half-blind at that — was set to the single task of looking at the finished boards and saying *streaky* or *not streaky*, which he did with the moral seriousness of a customs officer.

Tom paid them in the only coin any of them wanted: a task narrow enough to succeed at.

And Tom, who could hold nine facts, held the only nine that mattered — which board, which model, which constraint, which failure, and whether Aunt Polly would call it done. He never once touched the fence. He had, literally, not the reach for it. His genius was that he had noticed this before anybody else did.

When Aunt Polly came out at six, the fence stood white as the New Jerusalem, three coats where the sun hit hardest, and the cat unpainted.

"Well, I never," she said. "Tom, you can work when you're a mind to." And she gave him an apple, and a raise, and a larger fence.

---

He carried his apple round the corner in a state of considerable moral comfort, and stopped, because the last board was still wet, and in the wet paint he caught his own reflection.

He had a beard.

He stood a long while looking at it. The beard was neat. The beard was *confident*. The beard belonged to a man who says *take Mr. Data and Mr. Worf* and then stays on the bridge.

It came over Tom slowly, the way the truly awful things do. He had not become a con man. Con men *do* something; they at least run the shell game with their own hands. He had become something worse and far more respectable. He had become the officer who assembles the away team. He had become the one who reads the report, nods gravely, says *good work, Number One* — no. Wait. He was the one *called* Number One. Somebody else said it to him.

He had never been on the surface of the planet. Not once. Forty-seven fences and he had never held a brush past the second board.

He thought about the Melbourne. They had offered him his own ship; they had offered it three times, and each time he had looked at the flagship, at the ridiculous impossible fence of it, and said *no, thank you, the interesting problems are here.* He would say no again. He knew that about himself now, the way you know weather.

Tom straddled the tree-box backwards, because there was no longer any other way for him to sit down.

Somewhere uptown, something very large and very slow was already composing the next spec, and would presently say *make it so*.

"Aye, sir," said Tom, to nobody, and began to write.
