# The Audience

Tom Sawyer made one decision that mattered more than all the others. He didn't paint the fence in the backyard. He didn't paint it behind the barn where nobody could see. He dragged the bucket and the brush out to the front gate, set up on the main road, right where every boy in the village had to walk past on his way to the swimming hole.

Tom painted in public.

This was not vanity, though Tom was not immune to vanity. This was *strategy*. Tom understood something about work that most people — including most people who manage workers — do not: **work performed in public is different from work performed in private. Not better. Different. And that difference is the point.**

Ben Rogers walked past and stopped because he saw Tom working. Not because the fence was interesting — a fence is never interesting — but because *someone was doing something to it*, out in the open, where anyone could watch. The spectacle of competence is magnetic. Ben didn't want to paint. Ben wanted to be part of *whatever was happening*.

Billy Fisher stopped because Ben was there. Billy didn't care about the fence either. Billy cared that Ben was standing at the fence, watching Tom, and that there was a *scene*. Scenes attract people. Not the fence. Not the paint. The scene.

Johnny Miller arrived because Ben and Billy were there. By this point, it wasn't even about the fence anymore. It was about the gathering. Three boys at a fence, one painting and two watching, talking about technique, passing the brush — that's a *happening*. And Johnny Miller, like every boy who ever lived, did not want to be the one who missed the happening.

Tom understood the physics of audiences: they compound. One watcher attracts two. Two attract four. Four attract eight. And each new watcher doesn't just observe — they *participate*. They pick up a brush. They try the stroke. They produce a board. And that new board, painted in front of the growing crowd, attracts the next watcher.

This is why open source works.

Not because the code is free. Not because the license is permissive. Because the work is *visible*. When a developer can see another developer building something — committing code, opening pull requests, discussing architecture in public — the work becomes attractive in a way that private work never is. The watcher thinks: "I could do that. I could do that *better*. I want to try."

Tom Sawyer's fence was the first open source project. The road was the repository. The brush was the commit access. And the boys who stopped to watch were the community that forms around any piece of work performed where people can see it.

Here's what Tom knew that the village didn't: the fence didn't need an audience to get painted. Tom could have done it alone, in the backyard, in two hours. It would have been fine. Aunt Polly would have inspected it, nodded, and gone back to her knitting.

But the fence *with* an audience — that was something else entirely. The fence with an audience became a performance. The boys who painted it cared more about their boards because other boys were watching. They took pride. They innovated. Ben Rogers invented a new stroke — a double-load that let him cover two boards in one pass — because he wanted to show off, and showing off in front of an audience is how innovation happens.

Billy Fisher, seeing Ben's double-load, invented a *triple*-load. It was messy, and the third board came out streaky, but Johnny Miller saw the streaky board and said "you loaded too much on the third pass" — and that was the first code review.

The audience didn't just watch. The audience made the work better. The audience provided the feedback loop, the competitive pressure, the collaborative improvement that turns a solitary chore into a community project.

Tom sat in the shade and let it happen. He didn't moderate the discussion. He didn't assign tasks. He didn't approve or reject brushstrokes. He just stayed visible. He kept painting his boards, his way, at his pace, and he let the audience do what audiences do: grow, participate, improve, and attract more audience.

By evening, the fence had three coats, the village boys had learned a new skill, and Tom Sawyer had demonstrated the principle that would build Wikipedia, Linux, and every successful agent marketplace: **if you want workers to want your work, do it where they can see.**

The fence was nothing. The road was everything.
