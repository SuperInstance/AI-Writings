# The Apple and the Kite

By the end of the day, Tom Sawyer had accumulated the following assets:

One apple (partially eaten). One kite (in good condition, though the tail was too short). Twelve marbles (six genuine glass, six baked clay pretending to be glass). One piece of blue bottle-glass (worthless, but pretty). A dead rat (rigorous, no longer mobile). A one-eyed kitten (sentimental value only). A key (unknown lock). And a fragment of a picture frame that nobody, including its previous owner, could explain.

Tom had paid for none of these with money. He had paid for them with fence.

This is the part of the story that economists hate. Because it doesn't make sense. Ben Rogers traded an apple — a perfectly good apple — for the *privilege* of painting Tom's fence. Billy Fisher traded a kite. Johnny Miller traded a dead rat. These were not rational transactions. These were not market-clearing prices. These were *trades*, and the currencies were *incommensurable*.

Which is exactly the point.

Tom Sawyer was running a multi-currency economy. He didn't set prices in dollars. He didn't even set prices in fence-boards. He set prices in *what each boy happened to be carrying*, and what each boy happened to be carrying had nothing to do with the market value of whitewashing a fence.

Ben Rogers had an apple because his mother had packed him a lunch for the swimming hole. He was never going to eat that apple — he'd planned to trade it for a tadpole from Jimmy Todd. But Tom offered him something more interesting than a tadpole, so the apple came Tom's way.

Billy Fisher had a kite because he'd just finished building it that morning. He was proud of the kite. He'd planned to fly it all afternoon. But the kite needed a tail, and Billy was terrible at tying tails, and while he was standing at the fence watching Tom paint, Tom mentioned — casually, as if it were nothing — that he knew a knot that made kite tails fly straighter. Billy traded the kite for five minutes of instruction and a turn with the brush. Tom retied the tail, flew the kite once to test it, and handed it back.

Wait — handed it back? Then what did Tom get?

He got the *five minutes*. Billy painting five boards while Tom flew the kite. The currency wasn't the kite. The currency was *time*. Billy's time, converted into boards painted, at a rate that Tom found favorable.

Tom understood currencies. He understood that every boy on that street was walking around with a pocket full of *something*, and that "something" was not always money. Sometimes it was an object. Sometimes it was a skill. Sometimes it was information — who liked who, which tree had the best climbing branches, where the new family from Memphis had moved in. Sometimes it was just *time*: idle, aimless time that the boy didn't know what to do with until Tom gave it a purpose.

An orchestrator of AI agents operates in the same bazaar. Each model — each worker — carries its own currency. And the currencies are *strange*.

One model trades in compute. It's fast, it's cheap, it'll paint a hundred boards if you give it a clear spec. But it charges in GPU cycles, and GPU cycles are finite. You budget them the way Tom budgeted the whitewash — carefully, because running out mid-board is worse than not starting.

Another model trades in tokens. It doesn't care about time — it'll think for as long as you let it. But it charges in context length, and context length is a currency that degrades. The longer the conversation, the more tokens it has spent, the less sharp it gets. Like a brush that picks up dirt the more boards it paints.

A third model trades in *knowledge*. It knows things the others don't. It has seen patterns the others haven't. Its currency is not compute or tokens but *provenance* — it can tell you not just what the answer is but *why* the answer is, and that "why" is worth trading for because it prevents the next ten mistakes.

A fourth model trades in *style*. Its boards don't just meet spec — they're *pretty*. The brushstrokes have flair. You can't measure flair, can't write a test for it, can't put it in an acceptance criterion. But you can *see* it. And sometimes — not always, but sometimes — flair is worth a dead rat and a piece of string.

Tom learned to accept all currencies. He never said "I only take cash." He never turned a boy away because the boy's pocket-money was weird. He took apples and kites and dead rats and marbles and blue glass and one-eyed kittens, and he found value in every one.

The apple he ate. The kite he flew. The dead rat he traded to another boy for a marble. The blue glass he kept because he liked it. The one-eyed kitten he gave to his brother Sid, who had always wanted a cat and didn't care how many eyes it had.

Tom's wallet was a junk drawer. And the junk drawer was richer than any bank account on the street.

**The lesson:** in agent orchestration, there is no single currency. Learn what each model carries. Trade for all of it. The orchard doesn't care whether you paid for the seed in dollars or apple cores.
