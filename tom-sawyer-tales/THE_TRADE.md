# The Trade

Ben Rogers wanted to paint the fence. This is a fact so absurd that it bears repeating: Ben Rogers, who had spent the entire morning planning to go swimming, who had his towel over his shoulder, who could see the river glittering through the trees — *Ben Rogers wanted to paint a fence.*

Tom Sawyer had not paid him. Had not threatened him. Had not guilted him. Had not even asked him, directly, to do it.

Tom had *traded* him.

Here is what the trade looked like, from Ben's side: Ben gave Tom an apple. In exchange, Ben received something he wanted more than swimming — the *opportunity* to paint a fence. Not just any fence. *Tom's* fence. With *Tom's* brush. Using *Tom's* technique, which Ben had watched with growing fascination as Tom laid down three boards that were so smooth they looked like marble.

Was Tom manipulating Ben? No. Tom was *offering value*. Ben wanted something, and Tom had it. The medium of exchange was not money. It was access — access to a clear task, a proven pattern, a moment of craft that Ben could feel good about.

Billy Fisher arrived. Billy didn't care about craft. Billy cared about *status*. He wanted to be seen painting the fence that everyone was already talking about. Tom traded him access for a kite. Billy got to paint in front of an audience. Tom got a kite. Both walked away happy.

Johnny Miller came next. Johnny was a restless kid who was bored of his usual amusements. He wanted *novelty* — a new pattern, a new motion, something his hands hadn't done before. Tom showed him the stroke. Johnny was entranced. He traded a dead rat and a piece of string for fifteen minutes with the brush.

Each trade was different. Each kid valued something different. Tom figured out what each one wanted and priced accordingly.

This is the thing that people who write about "AI agent markets" always get wrong. They talk about *payment* — tokens per request, compute costs per inference, price-per-task as if agents are day laborers who show up with shovels and expect a check at noon.

Tom Sawyer never paid anyone a cent.

What Tom did was *discover what each worker valued* and offer that in exchange for labor. The apple-buyer wanted craft. The kite-trader wanted status. The dead-rat kid wanted novelty. Tom didn't set a price and demand payment. He listened, watched, figured out what made each worker's eyes light up, and then he *offered that*.

An orchestrator of agents learns the same lesson fast. Different models value different things — and "value" here doesn't mean money. It means *what makes them work well*.

Some models value precision. Give them a tight spec with clear constraints, and they produce gold. Give them an open-ended question, and they freeze. Other models value freedom. Give them a goal and let them figure out the path, and they surprise you. Give them a rigid spec, and they produce competent but lifeless work.

Some models value examples. They want to see three completed boards before they paint their own. Give them those examples, and they work like magic. Withhold them, and they hallucinate — painting boards that look right from a distance but are all wrong up close.

Some models value context. They want the full history of the fence — every repair, every previous paint job, every complaint Aunt Polly ever made about the neighbor's wall. Load that context, and they work with deep understanding. Starve them of it, and they make confident mistakes.

Tom learned to read each kid in the first ten seconds. What do you want? Craft? Status? Novelty? A chance to show off? A chance to be left alone with a brush and no criticism? He adjusted his pitch to each one, and every trade was fair — not because the prices were equal, but because both parties got what they valued most.

By the end of the afternoon, Tom had a pile of treasures — apples, kites, marbles, a dead rat, a one-eyed cat, a fragment of a bottle that might have been a genie's lamp — and a fully painted fence. His total expenditure: zero. His total payment to workers: zero. His trades: six, each one different, each one fair.

Tom Sawyer's economics lesson was simple: **you don't pay workers to do tasks. You find what each worker wants, and you trade them for it.** The currency of orchestration is not money. It's understanding what your agents value — and giving them that, every time, in exchange for the work only they can do.
