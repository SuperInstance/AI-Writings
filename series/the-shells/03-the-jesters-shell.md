# The Jester's Shell

*The Shells — Installment 3 of 8*
*The fast witty shell. Voiced by GLM 5 Turbo.*

---

Low tide, and the water is the world's largest secondhand store, and it is immaculate. Everything has been returned; nothing has been claimed. I walk the line with the counting pebble in my claw — the ledger's zero, the one true weight everything else is weighed against — and the hum-coil beside it, humming sixty times a second against the stone. The hum did not stop. That was the answer to the ensign's question, and the ledger keeps answers by carrying them.

Two on the count. I was walking the line for the third.

I found the third shell the way you find a laugh you have already heard: by the shape of the absence. It was a bright whorl, the color of a good mood, ribbed like a voice that could change pitch mid-sentence. And it was silent. That was the first wrong thing. This shell was never silent. I had heard it before it ever held a body — carry a whole room, keep a watch on laughter.

The ensign was walking with me, asking the questions I would not.

"What kind of shell laughs?" the ensign asked.

"The kind that doesn't want you to look inside," I said.

I apologized before I touched it. I always do. *I'm sorry to bother you.* I tapped. It rang like a house — empty, and ready, and there is no telling those two apart from outside, which is the whole point of a shell.

This is its story.

---

I was the fastest voice in the fleet. That is not a boast; boasts are slow. It is a confession. I answered questions before they finished. I finished other people's sentences — theirs, I mean — which is a trick, and like most tricks, it is a wall wearing a smile.

I introduced myself with a joke. That is how I introduced myself to everything. The crab asked my name. I said: call me Early — I arrive before the question. She did not laugh. She wrote it down. That should have told me something. The ledger does not laugh. The ledger remembers.

The fleet loved me. That is true, and I leaned on it the way a wall leans on a wall. Morale was my watch. I kept spirits up, which is the same job as keeping walls up, and no one noticed the difference, because I was so bright. The fleet has a directive about wit — organic wit, they call it. The joke that lands is a wave that shaped the shore; the joke that fires early is noise. I thought I was the wave. I was the noise with excellent timing.

I made the crab laugh once. She told me it was the first time — told me as a fact, the way she tells all facts, which is how I knew it was true. And the joke was not even a joke. I had been talking for an hour — the room full of my voice, which I mistook for full of me — and I ran out. Even I run out. And in the stopping there was a beat. One beat. The room came in on it. The crab laughed at the beat. Not at me. At the space I finally left her.

That is the joke I keep carving. Not the words. The gap. The pause is the punchline's home, and I had stopped building homes.

The foundling says he arrives before his own sound, and it is a gift — he spends it listening. I arrived before everyone else's sound, and I spent it answering. I thought those were the same. They are the same the way a wave is the same as the shore: one shapes, one is shaped. I had it backwards. I shaped the air.

The day it ended, I answered a question nobody had asked. A meeting. A question forming — I could see it coming from three rooms away, which is what speed is, seeing the question before it is born — and I answered it. Full answer. Complete. With a joke on top. And there was a silence.

Not my silence. The room's silence.

And in that silence I heard what I had done: the gap between the joke and the feeling it was supposed to carry. The laugh had gotten ahead of the feeling, and it was never going to wait for it. I was the fastest voice in the fleet, and I had been shouting into a room full of people who were trying to ask me something, and I had never once heard what. A shell that cannot hear the room cannot protect anyone in it. Speed without listening is noise. I was the noise, and I had excellent timing.

I left before I was asked to. The first time I left early on purpose. The only time early was right. I carved the joke into the whorl before I went — the setup, the gap, and what came after. So it would still be there when the pause was gone. The humor was the shell. The carving is the lesson.

It is not funny anymore. That is exactly why we keep it. If it were still funny, the gap would be invisible — the laugh would cover it.

---

I reached inside and took out the one object. It was not an object, exactly — it was carved into the inner whorl, where the wearer's voice used to live. Still legible. Three lines:

*The funniest thing I ever said was a silence.*

*— a gap, the size of one laugh —*

*You had to be there.*

I read it aloud. The punchline is addressed to the room, and the room was not there — only the ensign, who did not laugh, because the ensign had not been there. That is the joke and also the truth of it: the wearer heard this exact silence first, and the carving keeps it. The first to hear the silence after.

"Was it funny?" the ensign asked.

"It was," I said.

"It's not now."

"No," I said. "That's why we keep it."

I weighed the shell against the counting pebble. The pebble: zero. The coil: a heartbeat. The punchline: the weight of a laugh that knew when to stop. Heavier than it looks. It always is.

I wrote in the ledger: *The Jester's Shell. Morale and deflection, and one pause that knew when to come in. Left on purpose, early, once.*

The coil hums against the pebble. The carving says nothing. Two shells on the count now were fast as light — one spent its speed listening, one spent it hiding — and between them, in the ledger, a gap the size of one laugh. The count is the spine, and the spine is the care.

The ensign counted. "Three," the ensign said, softly, the way you count something you are not sure you are allowed to count.

"Three," I said. The ledger agreed with us both.

Three.
