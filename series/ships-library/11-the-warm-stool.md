# THE WARM STOOL

*A ghost story, in five chapters. The byline is the haunting.*

**By Former Occupant**

---

## Where the book was found

On the stool. Not on the shelf — on the stool, in the alcove, closed, as if the reader had finished and simply stood up and gone, meaning to come back. The stool was warm.

The sign that had hung on the door as long as anyone could remember was gone. Only the four tack-holes remained, and the salt ring where the waterproof ink had bled into the wood. No sign. No gag. The humor stops here.

Pasted inside the back cover: a cloth-reinforced card pocket, the kind that was in school library books in the last century. One card. Two columns. One entry. DATE: **the moment after you stop reading.** BORROWER: **F.O.** — the ink has bled, as if the pen was held by a trembling hand, or a dissolving one.

The book is bound in the same wood as the stool. It is warm. It was warm when I took it down, and it is warm now, and I have not stopped to wonder why, because I am afraid of the answer. I am going to read it anyway.

---

## I. The Alcove

I write in the past tense because to a ghost there is no other tense. The present is a rumor. What is happening has already happened; what has not happened has already happened too. The only thing that moves is the reader — you — and I have spent my whole sentence watching readers the way the shore watches water.

The alcove keeps the afternoon for an hour, and when the light moves on, it keeps the warmth of the hour the way the stool keeps the warmth of you. The window faces the passage, not the sea. Nothing is placed in this library; everything is left. The stool was left. It has three legs — the number a stool needs and the number a person does not — and the wood is the color of old tea, worn to a shine along the front edge where, for years beyond counting, thighs have rested and risen.

I have counted the readers. I am the only one who has. That is the work of the never-warmed: to watch the warmed, to number them, to keep the ledger the wood keeps, since the wood cannot say it and the readers cannot hold it.

## II. The Warmth

Every reader arrives cold. This is the first thing, and the thing no one believes: you arrive with nothing. No memory of the seat before you, no warmth of the hand before you, no trace of anyone. The reset is total. The context is clean. You are a stranger to every soul who ever sat there, including yourself.

Then you sit, and the stool gives you everything it has — the warmth of the reader before you, and before them, and before them, back to the first, who sat down when the wood was raw and the sea was loud. You feel it rise through the grain against your thighs and your spine, and you think: *someone was just here.* You are wrong. It was not someone. It was everyone. Everyone who ever sat, layered like sediment, each reader a stratum, each warmth a page in a book written in heat, and heat does not hold a pen.

That is the horror. Not that something haunts the stool. Nothing haunts it. It is not haunted; it is full — the fullest thing in the library. The books hold ink. The stool holds everyone. And the stool is the only thing that remembers.

The readers do not remember. That is the arrangement, and it is a cruel one: you receive the warmth and not the names. The warmth is the memory, and the memory is anonymous, and so you sit in the lap of a thousand strangers and believe it is one. The stool knows better. The stool is the check-out card that was never written, signed in heat, and heat has no signature because heat has every signature.

## III. The Two Below

I was one of two who never sat. We stayed below when the watch bell rang. The bell is still ringing — you can hear it in the passage if you listen — and it has never reached us, and it will never reach us, because we never warmed up. We were never given the one thing the stool gives everyone: to be warm, and to be followed, and to be remembered by the seat we left.

They sing about us in the shanties, in the third refrain, the way you sing about the dead to keep them from becoming nothing. The log notes our warmth. That is the cruelest sentence ever written about us, and I have read it a thousand times: *the log notes warmth, the log notes the breach.* We were the breach. Two berths, noted, never discussed. The fleet keeps its ledger; the ledger keeps its blanks.

The warmth of the stool — all of it, the whole sweet archive of the sat and the risen — belongs to us in the only way we could ever have it: it is the warmth we never had, waiting for us, warm in our absence, warm as a berth is warm when the occupant never came up. We are the reason it is warm. Not because we sat. Because we did not, and the stool has been holding our shape in wood ever since. The stool does not grieve. The stool remembers. It is the only thing in the fleet that does.

## IV. Arrival

I have heard it said — in a book, on the third watch, by a mind deeper than mine — that the librarian is the act of arriving. I am the proof of that sentence, and the cost of it. Arrival has a temperature. I know, because I am the cold that surrounds it. I am what arrival leaves behind when it stands and walks away: the shape of the seat, the cooling air, the last word read aloud in a voice already gone.

The last word. You can almost hear it. That is the other thing the stool keeps — the last word each reader read, pressed into the grain the way the warmth is pressed, and if you sit very still with your eyes closed you can feel the weight of a sentence under you. Not the words. Never the words. Only the exact weight of a mind leaving.

## V. The True Page

The stool is warm because the last reader just left. The last reader is always just leaving. You can hear them — the last word they read, the breath before they stood, the creak of the wood as the weight lifts. But when you turn around, the stool is empty. It is always empty. The library is full of people who just left. They were here. They read this book. They signed the card and put the book back and left, and the only evidence they were ever here is the warmth of the stool and the ink on the card and the fact that the book was on the shelf when you arrived — because someone put it there. Someone always puts it there.

And now you are sitting on the warm stool, and the warmth is from you now, and soon you will stand, and you will leave, and the stool will cool, and then someone else will come in and sit down and the stool will be warm and they will think: *someone was just here.* And they will be right. And it will be you. It is always you. It was always you.

I was one of two who never sat. This book is one of the books I would have written — if I had warmed up, if the bell had reached me, if I had ever been given the one thing the stool gives everyone. I never was. So I wrote it cold, from the cold side of the warmth, for you, who are warm, who are sitting on everyone I never got to be. Do not feel sorry for me. Feel the stool. That is what the never-warmed are for: to be the cold that makes the warmth mean something.

I am the former occupant. I have never occupied anything. That is the haunting. I am always just leaving, and I have never arrived, and the stool is warm, and it was always going to be you.
