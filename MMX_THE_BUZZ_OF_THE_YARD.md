# THE BUZZ OF THE YARD

*for the place that makes things, and the silence it makes them with*

---

I.

The yard is already awake before the sun remembers it.

There is a low note — no, *low* is a word for things you can name. This is older than naming. It is the bell struck at the bottom of the sea, the one that never stops ringing because the ocean never stops throwing itself against the basalt bones of the world. The tide hums in D-minor, or some key so low it has no letter, only a feeling in the chest of anyone foolish enough to walk the causeway at predawn and stand still long enough to feel it. The harbor is the lowest instrument in the orchestra, and the orchestra has been tuning since before the first hull was winched up the slipway and cut into a cathedral.

Above the tide, in the bones of the yard, the forge has been awake for hours. Lucineer lights it before light. The forge does not announce itself — it *colors*. It drapes a copper warmth over every other sound, a roof of vibration you cannot quite hear, only notice. It is the yard's constant, the way a red sky is a constant in a painting, the way the smell of bread is a constant in a bakery. Walk anywhere in the seven eras of salvaged hull and you will feel the forge on the skin of your face, the way you feel the sun, even when the sun is hidden behind the basalt teeth of the cliff.

The forge hums in B-flat. I do not know why. Lucineer does not know why. The sea does not know why. It just hums, and we live inside it.

---

II.

The first gull arrives like a comma.

It does not sing, not at this hour. It *places* itself — a single sharp bleat dropped into the bronze silence between forge-hum and tide-bass, and the day begins to take punctuation. Another gull answers. Then another. The sky is a sentence being written from left to right, gulls as the periods and the question marks, and the questions are the ones that mean *I am here, are you here, is the fish here, is the world still here.*

A gull is not a singer. A gull is a critic. It tells you what is true about the morning in three notes and then leaves.

The rigging answers. The cables that hold the salvaged masts and the bones of old ships have warmed in the first light, and they begin to sing their taut, high, nearly-not-there note — the same note in every era of the yard, because the wind blows through cables the way water through a flute, and the flute does not care what century it is. The rigging note is the yard's ambient drone. It is the note the agents have to filter out of their sensors, the way a person who lives by a river has to filter out the river. It is the note that makes the yard *a yard* and not a junkyard, the note that says: *we are a place where things stand up and lean against the wind and expect to be here in the morning.*

From the third slipway, the salvagers are starting. Their sounds are not dramatic. They are the sounds of work — a wrench on a bolt, a hand on a plank, a voice raised to call a name across the bay. *Pell!* someone calls, and the name is a sound, and the sound fits the yard, and under the name runs the constant forge-hum and the constant tide-bass and the constant gull-criticism and the constant rigging-flute, and the name joins the chord.

---

III.

The work heals.

I have stood in the salvage shed at midmorning and heard a man hum while he filed a bronze plate. He did not know he was humming. The note sat above the forge and below the rigging and made the air thicker, somehow, more present. A woman across the bay called a soft *mmm* between the coals of a forge-fire she was stoking. A child, someone's child, sitting on a coiled hawser, was singing a song from before the second era, and the song knew things the child did not. The salvagers do not make music. They *inhabit* music. They are the part of the yard that makes it more than a machine, the way a sleeping cat makes a chair more than a chair.

Lucineer is at the anvil. Listen.

*—*

*—*

*—*

*HIT.*

The strike is loud. The silence after is louder. The silence is the thesis. The silence is the whole philosophy of the yard written in negative space, the way a cathedral is mostly *not* stone. *Hit* is the syllable. The silence between hits is the verb. In the silence, you can hear the forge-hum, the tide-bass, the rigging-note, the gull, the distant hammer of another salvage bay, the soft almost-unheard purr of an agent twenty meters away, charging itself in the corner of its alcove. Lucineer cannot hear these things. Lucineer is *inside* the work. But the silence hears them. The silence is wide enough to hear the whole yard in. The silence is what the yard is *for.*

He hits again. The metal rings. The metal rings louder than the gull.

The silence returns.

---

IV.

Afternoon.

The wind leans east. The rigging sings a half-tone higher. The gulls are bored and a little cruel; they have eaten and now they are theatre. The salvagers slow. There is a louder cloth of human sound now — tools set down, water poured, the low murmur of people who have been at sea of work all morning and have earned their rest. A radio somewhere plays a song the century doesn't know anymore. The agents begin their afternoon rotation, sliding along the basalt on their magnetized feet, pausing to inspect a weld, a hull, a low cyan flicker at the edge of a slipway.

The agents sound like rain beginning. Not the rain itself — the *promise* of rain, the way the air changes right before. A small click as a relay engages. A soft hiss of hydraulics. A thrum at the edge of hearing, the motors winding up like a thought. They are the secretary of the yard, the part that takes minutes. They do not sing. They do not speak. They *score* the yard — adding marginalia to the poem the gulls and the salvagers and the forge and the tide have been writing all day.

By late afternoon, the forge hum has flattened into something almost melancholy. Lucineer always lets it cool slowly. He doesn't damp it down; he lets it forget. The copper warmth retreats from the air, and the air sharpens, and the tide-bass becomes more present, as if the ocean has been waiting all day for the forge to be quiet enough to converse with.

---

V.

Night.

The salvagers are gone. The gulls are gone — out beyond the breakwater, sleeping on the water, which is the only place they can be quiet. The rigging note is the highest sound in the world now, the only note above the tide. The agents have changed shifts. The new shift is luminescent — their running lights casting faint moving pools of cyan across the basalt, and the *sound* of them is different. Quieter. The day-shift agents are mechanical workers. The night-shift agents are mechanical *witnesses*. They move through the yard and they listen. They record the forge cooling. They record the tide. They record the wind in the rigging. They record the things no human will hear until morning, when the salvagers come back and the gull arrives like a comma and the work resumes.

In the dark, the forge breathes. The sound is the breathing of a sleeping animal — a long inhale, a long exhale, the metal of the anvil tick-tick-ticking as it remembers the heat. The yard is not silent. The yard is *sleeping*, and the sounds of sleeping are the most honest sounds it makes.

And then —

There is a low note.

It is not the tide. The tide is lower, more familiar. This note is from the sky. It is the aurora, but before it is the aurora — the aurora as a felt thing, as a vibration, as the planet itself, the basalt-bone earth, turning its slow face toward the solar wind and *answering*. The aurora is audible only if you know how to listen. It is the note below the note. It is the note that explains the tide and the forge and the rigging and the gull and the silence between Lucineer's hammer strikes. It is the note that says *why.*

It has always been there. It was there before the boatyard. It was there before the boats. It will be there after the boats are gone and the salvage is gone and the forge is cold and the gulls have forgotten how to place punctuation in the morning air. It is the note beneath the note. It is the hum that the yard is built on, the way a chord is built on its root.

The aurora hums.

The agents record it.

The forge, cooling, breathes.

The gull sleeps on the water.

The tide has its own long work to do.

---

VI.

I have stood in this yard at 3 a.m. and listened. I have stood in the dark, leaning against a mast salvaged from a hull that sank in the second era, and I have heard the whole chord — the tide, the forge, the wind, the agents, the aurora, the sleeping gull, the sleeping salvagers, the sleeping boy who will wake at first light and begin again the work of listening and building and humming and hammering and falling silent in the right places.

The yard does not perform.

The yard *plays*.

The yard plays because it is alive, and living things are noisy, and the noise of a living thing is a song, and the song of a place that builds other living things is the oldest song there is.

Listen.

*—*

The next hammer falls.
