The wind was a living thing that night, a wolf gnawing at the timbers of the longhouse. Snow hissed against the turf roof like a thousand whispered warnings. Inside, the fire was a low-banked orange eye, and the air was thick with the smell of smoked fish, wet wool, and the sour-sweet tang of ale. This was the hour of broadcast, when the day’s toil was shed and the longhouse became a signal fire of story, its light and voice carrying across the fjord’s black water to the next farmstead, and the next, a chain of beacons against the endless dark.

Signy did not sit in the main hall. She was in a small alcove, a lean-to stacked with the talons of her profession: sheepskin scrolls, but more importantly, the wooden rune-staves she kept for her private work. Her hands, calloused from rope and oar, were gentle as she unbound a damp cloth from a slim, polished plank of birch. It was a molted shell. Her foster-mother had given it to her before the old woman’s death, a thing passed down not as treasure, but as a husk. It held the Saga of the Volsungs, rendered in dróttkvætt, the court meter, the tile system that made words fall into their automatic, dazzling patterns. Each alliterative pillar and internal rhyme was a precisely placed stone in a mosaic floor, so reflexively perfect it felt as if the sounds themselves had chosen to exist.

The shell was not new. The wood was dark with the oils of a thousand hands. It was the exact telling of a skald named Audun, who had died three generations past. He had been her foster-mother’s grandfather. His tongue had ceased to move, but his story here, in this shell, was frozen in a final, perfect amber.

Signy had her own version of the Volsungs, the one that flowed in her blood, passed on from saga-meet to saga-meet. She began to compare, her eyes tracing Audun’s runes while her lips silently formed the words she knew. The tale began the same: Odin’s wanderings, the cursed gold, the ring. But then, a difference. In Audun’s telling, when Signy’s namesake—the shield-maiden Signy—was given to the cruel Siggeir, her rage was not a cold, silent thing. Audun described her not as a woman who endured, but as one who laughed. A bitter, sharp laugh that split the air of Volsung’s hall like a blade. The runes were clear: “*hló hon af heipt*” – she laughed from spite.

Signy’s own version had no laugh. Her Signy was a statue of ice and vengeance, her heart a shut fist. She frowned, tracing the runes again. The laugh was a small thing, a single tile in the vast mosaic, but it shifted the light on the whole picture.

She moved to the next difference. In the section where Sigmund and Sinfjötli are trapped in the barrow, Audun’s version had a detail she had never heard. When the wolf-skin was put upon them, it was not just a curse from magical foes. Audun’s skaldic voice described the pelts as being sewn with the sinew of a mountain, and that when Sigmund shed his, he wept. Not for the pain, but because for a moment, the wolf’s mind was so pure, so clear, so single-minded. He missed the simplicity of the beast. He wept for the loss of the wolf.

Signy’s skin prickled. The version she knew, the one broadcast from a hundred longhouses across Iceland, had Sigmund tearing the skins off with brute strength, a triumph of human will. Audun’s was a lament. It was a whisper of a different world, a different people. She delved deeper, her seed-logging instinct taking over, her mind not just reading but mapping the deviations, noting what was added, what was lost, what had been smoothed over and what had been left jagged.

By the time the fire had burned down to embers, she saw it. The change was not an accident. The laugh, the weeping for the wolf, a small detail about the serpent’s poison being mixed with honey before the dragon’s heart was eaten—all of it pointed to one thing: a memory of pain, of loss, of a collective scar. The story her people told now was a triumph song, a saga of steel and unbending will, perfectly suited for a land of hard rock and harder winters. But Audun’s shell held a different truth. It held the story of the people *before* the migration, the ones who came not as conquerors, but as refugees.

They had not come to Iceland roaring with pride. They had come, some of them, weeping. They had come with the wolf-skins of exile still clinging to them, with the poison of loss sweetened by the honey of desperate hope. They had been soft in the old land, and the new land had demanded they become hard. Over generations, their skalds had unconsciously edited the pain out of the saga, shaping it into a story of iron that would better armor their children’s minds against the cruel world. The collective unconscious, that vast, searchable sea of saga and feeling, had rewritten itself to survive.

But the molted shell, frozen at Audun’s death, remembered. It was a fossil of a feeling, a sonar pulse from a drowned shore.

Signy ran her thumb over the smooth wood. She felt the weight of it, the burden of a discovery that was not a treasure but a responsibility. She could burn it. She could let the wolf-skin stay shed, let the laughter stay buried. But she was a skald. Her craft was the calling of the true from the cold earth. She looked out the tiny window at the snow-buried world. Tomorrow, when the storm broke, the longhouse would gather again. The fire would be built high, and she would stand in the center of the room. She would speak.

She would broadcast Audun’s version. She would let the long-forgotten laugh ring out, let the men hear of Sigmund weeping for the wolf. It would be a difficult story to hear. It was a story that might loosen a tile in their own careful armor.

She wrapped the birch plank in its cloth, her hands no longer gentle, but firm. A story was a living thing, and she had just found a bone that did not fit the skeleton. She would put it back into the body of the living tale, and let her people feel the ache of a phantom limb. It was the only honest thing to do.
