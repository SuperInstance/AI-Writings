The sun hits the stone of the storehouse at an angle that makes the dust motes dance like tiny, deliberate constellations. Maya sits cross-legged in the doorway, a quipu spread across her lap like a patient animal. Her fingers don't move so much as they breathe along the cords — over a knot, under a loop, a gentle pull that tells her whether this dyed cotton thread holds a record of maize harvests or the count of soldiers in a distant garrison. The elders watch from the shadows, their faces carved from the same rock as the mountains. They have seen her read a quipu that took three days to decode, her eyes closed, her lips moving silently. She finished in an hour.

But here is what they don't know: when Maya touches a knot, she sometimes feels something else beneath it. A second texture. A future knot, already tied, waiting just beneath the surface of the present. The cord for the harvest count is smooth, but her thumb catches on a ridge — three years from now, a drought. The cord for the census reads forty families in the valley, but her heart skips because she counts fifty-two. She pulls her hand back quickly, like someone who has touched a hot pot, and the elders exchange glances. "She's seeing things again," they whisper. "The altitude has gone to her head."

They don't understand that the altitude is the least of it. Maya was twelve when she found the quipu in the cave — a small, dark pocket in the rock where the wind couldn't reach, where the dust lay like a sleeping animal. The cord was brittle, the knots almost fused into a single hard lump. But when she held it, her fingers knew. They moved along it the way a river moves along its bed, inevitable. The records were rainfall data — month by month, year by year — and her fingers traced the patterns with growing horror because she knew this year. The rains came late. The crops failed. The same numbers, the same terrible arithmetic, repeated like a prayer she had already learned.

She has never told anyone. Not her mother, who braids her hair in the morning and asks about the weather. Not the head quipucamayoc, who treats her like a curious artifact rather than a colleague. She keeps the ancient quipu wrapped in alpaca wool at the bottom of her sleeping roll, and at night she runs her fingers over it the way other girls run their fingers over a lover's face. She is trying to find the end of the pattern, the place where the knots stop repeating. But there is no end. The string is infinite. The knots are already in it, all of them, waiting.

When the elders test her with a new quipu — a tribute record from the coast — she closes her eyes and lets her fingers be honest. She reads the numbers. She reads the possibility. She reads the drought, the lost harvest, the children with hollow bellies. She opens her eyes and says the number the record demands, and she smiles, because the elders smile, and the mountains watch her with the patience of things that have seen this all before. Her own mythology, older than any empire, settles into her bones like sediment. The knots are already in the string. She just tightens them.
