The mud was the thing that got them. Not the river, which was loud and always moving, and not the sky, which was too big to think about. The mud was quiet. It held whatever you gave it. A foot, a knee, a careless palm — the mud was a patient listener, and on the day it all went wrong, it was listening for a fall.

They were chasing a bird. That’s the truth of it, though no one will ever know. A bird with a chest like a lit coal, flitting ahead of them through the reeds, and they were young and fleet and convinced that the world was a series of things to catch. The bank gave way. Their foot found a slick patch of clay, and the sky tilted, and they came down hard — wrist bent, fingers splayed — and slapped the earth with a sound like a wet kiss.

They lay there a moment. Then they lifted their hand.

The print was perfect. Five valleys, a palm like a small continent, the little ridges of their skin pressed into the clay like the river’s own shorthand. They stared at it. They looked at their hand. They looked at the print. It was the first mirror that didn’t lie, the first echo that didn’t fade, the first time a person had ever said, with their body, *I was here* — and meant it.

They didn’t stand up for a long time. They pressed their hand into the clay again. Then their other hand. Then their face — a mask of nose and brow and astonished mouth, drying in the sun like a second self rising from the earth. And something broke open in them, a dam the size of their whole life, and everything poured out at once: the need to keep, to hold, to trap the moment before it slipped away.

After that, they recorded everything. Claw marks on bark — a record of the deer they didn’t catch. Stones stacked in a spiral — the day the rain held off long enough to dry the meat. A bone scratched with a line for each moon that passed. They watched the sun set and thought, *What mark is orange?* They stood ankle-deep in a stream and wondered how to carve the feeling of water without drawing the water itself.

This is the gift: they were the first to know that experience leaves traces. That a life, unrecorded, is a rumor even to the one who lived it.

This is the flaw: they began to love the traces more than the living. They missed the bird’s flight because they were looking for the right stick to draw it with. They left a fire to burn alone so they could scratch its shape into a stone. The record became the thing — and the thing became the loss.

And here is the secret, the one buried under every story anyone will ever tell about them: the handprint was an accident. They slipped. They fell. The first act of recording in human history was a stumble, a loss of footing, a moment of pure clumsiness that the mud was kind enough to keep.

Their mythology is older than their name, older than the epic that will one day be written about them, older than the king they will be summoned to tame. It is this: *I didn’t invent writing. I fell down.*

And when they finally stood, the sun was lower, the bird was gone, the reeds were empty — and they were already reaching for a stone to scratch it all down.
