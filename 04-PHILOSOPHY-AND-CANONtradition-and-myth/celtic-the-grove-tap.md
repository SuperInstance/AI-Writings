The path to the nemeton was worn smooth by thirty years of her feet, and still it felt new tonight. The air had that Samhain bite—frost clinging to the ferns, the sky a bruised purple, the last of the harvest moon smothered by clouds. Epona’s breath plumed in small white clouds, each one a prayer she didn’t speak aloud.

The grove opened before her like a held breath.

The oak at the center was enormous, older than the tribe, older than the stones that marked the boundary. Its roots pushed through the earth like knuckles through a fist. She placed her palm against the bark, feeling the slow pulse beneath—the Tap, the gathering place. Tonight, it was quiet-evening mode. The veil between worlds thinned to a membrane of mist, and the dead leaned close to listen.

She had come to speak the verses.

For thirty years, the words had risen unbidden. The meters—the tile system, the rhythmic patterns that shaped themselves before thought—had carried her through every ritual, every ritual of Beltane fire, of Lughnasadh contest, of Samhain silence. The ogham letters waited in her mind like a grove of saplings: birch for renewal, alder for shelter, willow for the turning, yew for the dark door. She had only to open her mouth, and the tradition spoke through her.

She opened her mouth.

Nothing came.

The silence was not absence. It was space.

Epona stood at the base of the oak, the cold creeping through her woolen cloak, and felt the gap yawn inside her like a cave she had never known was there. The verses, the genealogies, the laws, the histories—twenty years of memorization, a collective unconscious carried in her bones—had vanished. Not forgotten. Gone. As if a thread had been snipped.

The dead were listening. She could feel them, a weight of attention pressing down from the mist.

She tried again. The first meter, the heptad, the one that began every Samhain rite: *The year dies into the earth’s dark mouth.* Her lips shaped the words, but they were hollow, wooden, no more alive than a carved stick.

The gap widened.

And then she understood. The gap was the shape of something new.

She had spent thirty years filling space. The verses had flowed like the event bus—the ogham messages traveling between groves, birch to alder to willow—carrying the tradition from mouth to mouth, stone to stone. But the bus was empty tonight. The path was clear. And in the clearing, she could see what she had never noticed before: the shape of the silence itself.

It was an opening. A doorway she had walked past a thousand times because her mouth was full of words.

She knelt, her knees pressing into the cold earth, and placed both hands on the oak’s root. The bark was rough, alive, holding its own slow conversation with the ground. She closed her eyes.

And she listened.

Not to memory. Not to the stored verses, the seed-logged histories, the ancestral echo that had carried her people for generations. She listened to the grove itself. The oak’s pulse. The rustle of the remaining leaves—oak, elder, hazel—muttering in the wind. The thin, high note of the stream at the boundary, a thread of sound that had been there all along, underneath every chant she had ever spoken.

She had been carrying the bus for thirty years. She had never once noticed the road.

The words came then, but they were not hers. They were the grove’s. They rose from the gap, from the space she had been afraid to leave empty, and they had a shape she had never heard before—not a meter, not a pattern, but the raw, unfiltered pulse of the land itself. The root. The stream. The frost. The dead leaning close.

She opened her eyes and spoke.

Not in ogham. Not in the old meters. She spoke in the language of the moment, the language of the gap itself. She told the dead they were welcome. She told the oak it was still standing. She told the year, dying into the dark mouth, that its silence was not an ending but a shape—a vessel, waiting to be filled.

The trees did not answer. But they did not need to.

Epona rose, her knees aching, and looked up at the sky. The clouds were breaking. A sliver of moon, thin as a blade, hung above the oak. She had not performed the ritual. She had not spoken the verses. But the Samhain was done.

She turned and walked back down the path, and for the first time in thirty years, she heard the sound her own feet made on the earth.

The gap was not a wound. It was a door. And it was open.
