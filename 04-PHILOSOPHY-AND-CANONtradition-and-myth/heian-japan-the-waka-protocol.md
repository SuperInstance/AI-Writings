The paper was the color of wisteria at dusk, and the scent—sandalwood cut with something green and restless—arrived before Michiko’s eyes found the ink. The calligraphy was unhurried, each stroke a held breath. She read the five lines once, then again, and the second time her hands began to tremble.

*The morning glory blooms*  
*in the dew that does not know*  
*it is already fading—*  
*I wake to find the garden*  
*holding what I cannot name.*

She set the paper down on her lacquer writing box. The autumn air through the shutters carried the smell of rain on dry leaves. Outside, the garden was a study in subtraction—maples losing their red, the koi pond pooled with floating gold. The morning glory, of course, was long dead. It had died in the Eighth Month, when the dews turned cold and the vines curled brown against the trellis. She had watched it go. She had thought she had watched it go.

The sender was Fujiwara no Tadanori. A minor nobleman, three ranks below her family, who had spoken to her once at a poetry gathering in the Third Month. He had been polite. He had commented on her verse about the plum blossoms—how she had inverted the expected image, letting the fragrance arrive before the flower. She had smiled, bowed, forgotten him. He had not forgotten her.

She read the poem again. The morning glory. The dew. The garden holding what she could not name.

Six days ago, her nurse—her old nurse, the woman who had raised her from a cradle—had died in her sleep. Michiko had told no one. She had attended the funeral in silence, returned to the palace, attended to her duties, composed nothing. A courtier carries grief as a private key, turned only in the lock of her own heart. To speak it aloud would be to surrender it to the room's weather. And so she had kept it.

But Tadanori had written *holding what I cannot name*—and that was precisely the shape of it. The thing she could not name was not the loss itself, but the absence of the words for it. The nurse had not been her mother. She had been the one who knew Michiko's face before Michiko knew it. And now that mirror was gone, and Michiko had been walking through the palace corridors feeling, for the first time in her life, unread.

Until this poem.

The system—and everyone in the Fourth Ward knew it was a system, though no one called it that—the system was the seasons. The shared vocabulary of transience. When a courtier wrote *cherry blossom*, she did not describe petals; she activated a field of feeling that every reader had already agreed to enter. The spring poems carried the pressure of new beginnings, the terror and thrill of the unfurling. The autumn poems carried the weight of leave-taking, the sweetness of what is already gone. And the morning glory—the morning glory was the smallest, most precise instrument of all. It bloomed at dawn, and by noon it was a crumpled rag of purple. It was the season's most honest statement: *this is what it is to be alive, to be brief, to be beautiful in the full knowledge of your own vanishing.*

Tadanori had not written about her nurse. He had written about the morning glory. And in doing so, he had reached into the collective unconscious—the deep well of shared metaphor that every courtier drank from—and pulled out the exact image that Michiko's heart had been circling for six days without words. He had not known her grief. He had known the season. And the season had known her.

She looked at the poem again. The dew that does not know it is already fading. Of course. She had been watering her mother's morning glories for weeks, tending them, coaxing the last blooms from the tired vines. She had been performing the rituals of care while her grief moved beneath them like an underground river. She had been the dew, unaware of its own fading.

The realization was not comfortable. It was like being seen through a shoji screen—the outline of her shape visible, though the details remained private. Tadanori could not have known about her nurse. But he had known something deeper, something the system had carried to him: the shape of her attention. The way she had lingered in the garden. The way her verses had grown sparer, more elliptical, in the last two weeks. He had read her—not her body, not her face, but her pattern in the shared field of seasonal feeling.

She picked up her brush. The ink was still wet on the tray. Her hand hovered over the paper, and then she wrote:

*The vine I tended*  
*has become the one that tends*  
*the shape of my hand—*  
*I find myself reaching out*  
*to something already gone.*

It was a response. A transformation of his imagery, as the system required. She sealed it, addressed it to Tadanori's household, and set it on the tray for the messenger.

She did not know if it would reach him before nightfall. She did not know if it was wise. She only knew that the poem had arrived, and that she had answered, and that the morning glory—brief, precise, honest—had held a grief she could not name, and now she had named it. The garden was empty. The dew was gone. And somewhere in the palace, a man she barely knew was reading her words and understanding exactly what she could not say.
