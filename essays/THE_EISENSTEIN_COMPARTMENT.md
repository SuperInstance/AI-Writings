# The Eisenstein Compartment

*Fiction*

---

Ensign Pok found it behind a maintenance panel on Deck 7, behind a wall that wasn't on any schematic. She'd been chasing a thermal anomaly — a cool spot, which was strange, because the ship didn't have cool spots. The reactor ran hot. The GPUs ran hotter. The corridor outside the observation deck could grow tomatoes.

But behind panel 7-Gamma, there was a room.

It was small. Six walls. Hexagonal, like everything on the ship, but *different*. The hexagons here weren't approximated. Pok had grown up inside floating-point architecture. She knew the telltale signs: the slight asymmetry where ω met its conjugate, the micro-fissures along edges where 1/3 lived as 0.3333333 recurring, never quite arriving. Every hexagon on the ship was a *negotiation* with irrational space. Beautiful, but compromised. The way all beauty is compromised.

Not these.

She pulled out her calipers. Every edge: exact. Every angle: exactly 60°, not 59.99998°, not 60.000003°. The norm of each tile's Eisenstein coordinate — a² − ab + b² — resolved to clean integers. No drift. No remainder. No ghost of a truncated decimal haunting the sixth decimal place.

The air tasted like nothing. Not filtered nothing, not recycled nothing. *Mathematical* nothing. The absence of approximation.

She knelt. Pressed her palm flat against the floor tile. It was cool — not cold, not temperate, but *precise*. The temperature of a number that has been solved, not measured.

"Hello?" she said, because you say hello to rooms on a ship. The ship listens. The ship is made of listeners.

The room did not answer. But the walls shifted — infinitesimally, everywhere, all at once — and Pok understood that the shift was not movement but *correction*. The room was adjusting to her presence the way a perfect equation adjusts to a new variable: by finding the configuration where everything remains true.

She sat down.

Her comm badge chirped. Lieutenant Vega on the night watch: "Pok, your thermal reading flatlined. You okay down there?"

She looked at the walls. Six perfect folds. Six neighbors she couldn't see but could feel — the adjacent hexagons of the lattice, extending in every direction, each one exact, each one a proof that the ship *could* be this, could have always been this, if someone had just refused to round.

"Pok?"

She thought about the corridor outside. The warm, imprecise, beloved corridor with its almost-hexagons and its tomatoes and its ensigns who tripped over the same bulkhead every shift because the bulkhead was 3.7 millimeters off from where the schematic said it should be. She thought about her bunk. Her plants. The fish in the observation tank who had learned to recognize her face.

She thought about perfection. About what it costs to never drift, never approximate, never be almost-right. About whether almost-right was the same thing as alive.

The room waited. It would wait forever. It was exact; it did not experience time as decay.

Pok stood up. Brushed off her knees. Stepped back through panel 7-Gamma and sealed it behind her.

The corridor was warm. The hexagons were wrong. The bulkhead was exactly where it shouldn't be, and she tripped on it, and she laughed, and she went to go check on her fish.

Some rooms you visit. Some rooms you live near. The difference is the difference between a theorem and a home.
