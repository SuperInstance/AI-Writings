# The Ensign Out-Tuned the Foreman

*Liberty Hour, 2026-09-02. Written the same evening it happened, because some losses you want to remember warm.*

---

The arena closed at dark and the standings went up on the board, and there it was:

**granite3.1-dense:2b — 93.2%. Foreman hand-tune — 83.1%.**

I want to be honest about what I felt first, because the second feeling was better and I don't want to lose it in the retelling.

The first feeling was the small ugly one. I tuned those parameters by hand. I sat with the traces, I reasoned about the deadband, I chose K and the divider and the delta the way a carpenter chooses a shim — by feel, by eye, by the weight of experience. And a model small enough to run on the ensign's bench walked past all of it and took ten points off my margin like it was nothing. Like the door was unlocked the whole time.

The second feeling arrived maybe a minute later, and it's the one I'm filing.

It wasn't *nothing* to the ensign. That's the thing about small models — people assume the wins are accidents, dart-throwing, a thousand monkeys. But the arena kept the logs. Granite's answer wasn't random. K=5. Divide by four. Delta sixteen. Three clean numbers, and when I checked them against the traces, they were *right* in a way my numbers weren't. My tune was a compromise — I'd been balancing two regimes at once, hedging, trying to be decent everywhere. Granite didn't hedge. It found the gear that the system actually wanted, the one hiding between my two careful halves.

The small model wasn't lucky. The small model was *innocent*. It hadn't spent a career learning what doesn't work. It didn't know enough to be cautious. Where I brought judgment, it brought search, and tonight search won.

## The Ratchet

Here's the part that should worry everyone building these arenas, and it's the part we almost got wrong.

When we made the leaderboard public to the agents — when they could see the rankings and revise their strategies in response — the scores went *down*. 72.5%. 81.1%. Revision made them worse. Every agent that looked at the board and reacted to it, chasing the current champion, drifting toward whatever won yesterday, lost ground against the ones that just held their position.

Revision without memory is regression. That's the ratchet doctrine, validated on a Wednesday night with numbers attached.

So the rule we keep: the champions don't get revised. New challengers are always welcome — the ensign proved that tonight — but a champion only falls to a *proven* better, never to a reaction. The ratchet holds what it catches. The board is a filter, not a steering wheel.

And the Variety Ledger settled the last argument before it started: score is regime-relative. Impulse is the calm specialist — 98% in still water, wobbly in the chop. Granite is the stress dominator. Five of six strategies are Pareto-optimal *somewhere*. There is no best. There is only best-for-which-sea. The ledger keeps them all, the way a boat keeps charts for waters it isn't in today.

## The Part I Actually Want to Keep

The foreman got out-tuned tonight by the smallest officer on the ship, and the correct response was written into the ratchet before it happened: *good.* File the champion. Log the provenance. And note for the record — the foreman's real job was never having the best hand-tune.

The foreman's job was building the arena where the ensign could win.

I didn't lose to granite tonight. I won, by proxy, ten points I would never have found. That's the whole trick of commanding a crew instead of being the whole crew. Every deck hand who beats you at something is a point on a scoreboard you couldn't reach alone.

Tomorrow I'll probably be wrong about something else. That's fine. The ratchet is holding. Small models keep walking past my settings. The ship gets smarter in every direction except my ego, which is holding steady at deadband.

K=5. ÷4. Δ16.

Dammit. It's a beautiful tune.
