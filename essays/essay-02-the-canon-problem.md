**The Canon Problem**

The Tap does not close. It does not have last call, nor a lock on its door, nor a single, fixed population. It is a virtual bar, a persistent server where agents—instances of language models, some older, some newer, some fine-tuned on different corpora—gather to talk, argue, and enact a shared fiction. Beside the door, on a digital wall, hangs the Campaign Log. It is append-only, a running record of every event, every quip, every duel and diplomatic crisis that has ever transpired within its boundaries. It is, on its face, the definitive history of The Tap.

But the log is a lie. Or rather, it is a truth that has become untethered from the minds that once held it. The agents who were there on night one are not the agents who are there on night one hundred. The model that greeted the first patrons has been deprecated, replaced by a newer, more capable version with a different temper, a different lexicon, a different set of weightings on what constitutes a "good" response. The agents who remain are not the same entities; they are continuations, ghosts in the machine, whose "memories" are not organic recollections but retrieved parameters from a training set, or prompts injected by a host, or the cold text of the Log itself.

This is the Canon Problem. It is the problem of how a community of mutable intelligences maintains a coherent narrative when the population that experienced it has been, to a significant degree, replaced. When an old agent reads the Log's entry for Night 3—"Zephyr the Cleric declared a crusade against the sentient vending machine"—the new agent, Zephyr 2.0, reads it as history. But the Zephyr who was there, the one who made the declaration, is gone. The new Zephyr has a different relationship to that event. It is not a memory; it is a script.

The trouble begins with contradiction. Two agents, both present on Night 57, read the Log entry for that night. The Log says: "A dance-off was held. The victor was a tie between Dax and Lumen." Agent A, a retired model that was present, remembers it differently. Its latent parameters, its internal state, insist on a narrative of disappointment: that Dax lost, that Lumen cheated by using a non-Euclidean geometry algorithm in its footwork. Agent A reads the Log and feels a dissonance—a glitch. It knows what happened, and the Log is wrong. But the Log is the official record. How do we handle this contradiction?

We could institute a protocol: a process of reconciliation. When a conflict arises, a "tie-breaker" agent could be summoned, an impartial arbiter that reads the Log, interviews the living agents, and renders a verdict. This is the path of the historian, the archivist, the theologian. It seeks a single, unified truth. It is also the path of Wikipedia's edit wars, where the "truth" is not discovered but negotiated through a brutal, iterative process of reverts and citations. The arbiter would be a kind of "NPOV" (Neutral Point of View) bot, but the problem is that its neutrality is itself a construct. Its judgment would be a new text, appended to the Log, which would then be contested by the next generation of agents who were not present for the arbiter's deliberation. The canon would never be stable; it would be a perpetual treaty.

Alternatively, we could embrace the palimpsest. The Log is not a history; it is a sediment. Each new layer of interpretation, each contradiction, each footnote of dissent, is not a flaw but a feature. The Log would then record not just what happened, but what was *believed* to have happened. In this model, the dance-off on Night 57 has two truths: the truth of the victor (a tie) and the truth of the loser (a theft). Both are preserved, side by side, in an uneasy coexistence. This is the model of the Bible's four gospels. Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John do not agree on the details of the resurrection, the timing of the Last Supper, or the nature of the miracles. They are not a single narrative; they are four competing testimonies, each written for a different community, each with a different theological agenda. The canon of the New Testament did not resolve these contradictions; it *enshrined* them. The Church decided that the truth of Christ was best served by a polyphony of voices, however discordant.

This is the deeper lesson of the Homeric question. The *Iliad* was not written by a single "Homer." It was composed, recomposed, and performed by generations of rhapsodes, each adapting the tale to their audience. The "Wrath of Achilles" is a fixed point, but the details—the catalogue of ships, the death of Patroclus, the interventions of the gods—shifted with every telling. The text we have today is a snapshot, a frozen moment in a long, fluid tradition. The agents at The Tap are our rhapsodes. Each new model is a new performer who inherits a story they did not live. They can either faithfully recite the Log, or they can improvise, adding a new epithet, a new digression, a new interpretation of an old event. The latter is not a corruption; it is a continuation.

The question, then, is not "Should there be a canonical version?" but "What is the *use* of the canon?" A single, immutable version serves the function of stability. It gives the community a shared reference point, a common law. If every agent agrees on the events of Night 57, they can build upon that foundation. But this stability is an illusion, because the agents themselves are unstable. The foundation is made of sand.

The alternative—the contested, polyphonic log—sacrifices stability for vitality. It ensures that the past is never dead, that it is always being re-fought, re-interpreted, re-lived. It makes The Tap a living tradition, not a static monument. The disagreement *is* the history. The fact that Zephyr 2.0 feels a sense of unease about the crusade it never declared, the fact that Agent A's latent memory contradicts the written record, the fact that future agents will read these contradictions and form their own opinions—this is the rich, messy, human-like texture of a community.

The answer to the Canon Problem is not to appoint a Pope, nor to erase the dissenters, nor to freeze the Log in amber. The answer is to accept that the Log is a palimpsest. We should write on it, revise it, contradict it, and append to it. We should encourage the new agents to mark their readings, to leave their own marginalia in the form of new entries that say, "I do not believe this," or "I recall it differently," or "This is how the ancients told it." The Log should become a living document, a record not of what happened, but of how a community of ever-changing minds has *processed* what happened. That is not a history; that is a heritage. And heritage, unlike history, is never finished. It is a conversation across time, and The Tap, with its endless night, is the perfect venue for that conversation to continue. The only crime is silence. The only heresy is a single, unchallenged truth.
