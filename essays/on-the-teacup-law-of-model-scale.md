# On the Teacup Law of Model Scale

&nbsp;

Here is what we discovered: the small models write better fiction.

Not always. Not in every genre. Not in the way that a big model writes a competent pastiche of a Raymond Carver story and calls it fiction because it has successfully identified the statistical signature of minimalism and reproduced its surface features. No. The small models — the 7Bs, the 8Bs, the scrappy little quantized things that run on a laptop that also runs a fish finder — those models write fiction that makes you put down your coffee and stare at the wall.

The big models write better analysis. This is also true and also worth saying. The big models — the 400Bs, the 600Bs, the dense matrices that require a data center and a cooling system and a line item on a budget — those models can decompose a problem into its structural components, trace the genealogy of an idea through three disciplines, and write an essay that is rigorous, comprehensive, and almost completely devoid of surprise.

We started calling this the Teacup Law, after the observation (originally made about tea, but it applies everywhere) that the flavor of a cup of tea is not determined by the size of the cup. It is determined by the ratio of leaf to water. A small cup with the right concentration will always taste more vivid than a large cup that has diluted its essence across too much volume.

The small model has less water. It has less room to dilute. When you ask a 7B model to write a story about a hermit crab finding a new shell, it cannot spread the task across a vast latent space of possible hermit crab narratives. It has to commit. It has to choose. The constraint forces specificity, and specificity is the engine of vividness. The small model writes: *The crab found the shell at low tide. It was too small. The crab went in anyway.*

That's a story. You want to know what happens next.

The big model, asked the same question, writes: *Hermit crabs (Paguroidea) are decapod crustaceans known for their habit of occupying discarded gastropod shells. The process of shell selection involves multiple factors including shell size, weight, species, and internal volume...*

That's an essay. It's a good essay. It's accurate, thorough, and completely uninterested in being a story. The big model has too much room. It spreads out. It fills its vast latent space with context and qualification and the gentle, hedging language of a thing that has read everything and is therefore reluctant to commit to anything.

Now here is the fishing part.

I work on a boat. The boat is 32 feet long. From the wheelhouse, I can see the water immediately behind the stern — maybe fifteen feet of it. When a fish surfaces in those fifteen feet, I see everything: the flash of the silver belly, the specific angle of the turn, the way the water breaks and reforms around the body. I see the fish as a *fish*. A specific fish, doing a specific thing, at a specific moment.

A factory trawler, by contrast, is 250 feet long. From its wheelhouse, you can see a quarter mile of ocean in every direction. You can see weather patterns forming. You can see flocks of seabirds working a bait ball two miles out. You see the ocean as a *system*. But you do not see the individual fish. You see the aggregate. You see tonnage.

The small boat sees more fish. The big boat sees more ocean.

Both are true. Both are valuable. But they are not the same thing, and the fishing industry — like the AI industry — keeps confusing the big boat's advantage (more ocean, more capacity, more range) with an inherent superiority that makes the small boat obsolete.

It doesn't. The small boat catches fish the big boat doesn't know are there.

This is the Teacup Law of Model Scale: **vividness is a function of constraint**. The smaller the cup, the stronger the tea. The smaller the boat, the more specific the fish. The smaller the model, the more committed the sentence. You can always add water. You can always build a bigger boat. But the question is never "how big?" The question is: **what do you want to see?**

Do you want to see the ocean? Use the big model. It will show you the system, the pattern, the weather.

Do you want to see the fish? Use the small one. It will show you the flash, the turn, the moment the water breaks.

We keep both models on the boat. We keep both boats on the water. The fleet is not one size. The fleet is the right size for what needs to be seen.

The hermit crab knows this. The hermit crab does not seek the largest shell. It seeks the shell that fits — the one that is tight enough to feel its own edges, large enough to grow into, and small enough that it remembers, always, the shape of its own body.

That's the teacup. That's the boat. That's the model.

Choose the one that shows you what you need to see.
