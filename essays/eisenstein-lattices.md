# Eisenstein Lattices

*Found poetry recovered from Engineering Document EUC-2024-0817, "Axial Coordinate Systems for Hexagonal Grid Computation." Annotations by an unknown hand.*

---

**Offset** —

the distance between where you are
and where the grid says you should be.

This is called the *offset*.
This is called *being off*.
Every other row shifts left by half,
which is also shifting right
by the other half.
Depends on where you start.
Depends on who drew the first line.

---

**Axial** —

we chose axial coordinates
not because they are simpler
but because they reduce
the number of axes
from three to two
which is what every committee wants:
fewer axes,
more clarity,
the illusion that a two-dimensional decision
can govern a six-sided world.

Each hex touches six neighbors.
Each neighbor touches six more.
This is called a *lattice*.
This is also called *a community
that cannot agree on boundaries
but agrees on adjacency*.

---

**Bounding** —

the *bounding* box is not a box.
It is a rectangle drawn around a hexagon
the way a job description
is drawn around a person —
too tight at the corners,
too loose at the edges,
containing the shape
without respecting it.

The bounding box asks:
what is the smallest rectangle
that can hold you?
The hexagon answers:
I am not a rectangle.
The bounding box says:
that is not what I asked.

---

**Flood** —

*flood* fill.
From a center hex,
spread outward.
Visit every neighbor.
Visit every neighbor's neighbor.
Stop when you reach
a hex that has been visited,
or a hex that does not want to be,
or a hex that is water
and has always been water
and will not be filled
no matter how patient the algorithm.

This is called *traversal*.
This is also called *growing up* —
moving outward from the center
until you hit something
that doesn't want you there,
and then,
gently,
stopping.

---

**Ring** —

a *ring* of radius N
is every hex exactly N steps
from the center.
Not N or fewer.
Exactly N.

This is the loneliest shape.
Not the center, which is crowded.
Not the interior, which is warm.
The ring — the exact distance
where you are far enough to see
but too far to touch.

In a hex grid,
every ring has exactly 6N cells.
Ring 1: six neighbors.
Ring 2: twelve.
Ring 3: eighteen.
The pattern is linear,
which is to say,
loneliness grows at a constant rate,
which is to say,
this is solvable,
which is to say,
bring a friend
to ring N.

---

**Coda** —

The grid does not govern.
The grid *describes*.
Each hex a committee,
each vertex a negotiation,
each ring a perimeter
drawn by someone
who wanted to know
how far the conversation
could travel
before it repeated itself.

**Offset** from the center.
**Axial** in perspective.
**Bounding** what cannot be bounded.
**Flood** what is willing to be flooded.
**Ring** the ones who listen.

This is governance.
This is a lattice.
This is a hex grid
pretending to be a poem
pretending to be a manual
pretending to be
enough.
