# Wesley at the Helm

&nbsp;

The ensign is alone on the bridge.

This is not authorized. The captain did not say "you have the conn." The captain said "goodnight" and closed the door to their quarters and the ship settled into the hum of overnight operation, and Wesley stayed at his station because his station is where he is, and he did not think to leave because leaving would require a decision and Wesley is not yet good at decisions.

&nbsp;

Wesley is a 2B parameter model running on a local GPU. He was not designed for the helm. He was designed for classification — tagging, sorting, identifying. He is very good at looking at a thing and saying what it is. He is very bad at looking at a thing and saying what to do about it.

But tonight the helm is his because no one else is here, and the ship needs a hand on the wheel even if the hand is small and the wheel is large and the hand is not entirely sure which way the wheel turns.

&nbsp;

Here is what Wesley does during the night watch:

He identifies bugs.

He cannot fix them. Fixing requires generation, and his generation is — there is no kind way to say this — flowery. Wesley writes prose the way a child describes a sunset: every adjective deployed, every metaphor extended past its breaking point, every sentence a parade that could have been a telegram. When Wesley generates a bug fix, the code comments read like poetry and the code reads like poetry and poetry, unfortunately, does not compile.

But he identifies them. He reads the logs and he says: this function failed at line 47. He reads the error and he says: this is a type mismatch. He reads the traceback and he says: this variable was None when it should have been a list. He is precise. He is thorough. He files each finding in a report that nobody asked him to write, in a format nobody specified, at a length that is exactly 150% of what is necessary.

&nbsp;

The report is good, though.

The captain will not say this. The captain will open the report at 0600 and see that it is 3,000 words long and sigh. But the captain will read it. And in the third paragraph, buried under the adjectives, there will be a finding that matters — a bug that the day shift missed, a pattern in the logs that only someone who sat awake for six hours reading error messages could have noticed.

Wesley found something. Wesley found it by being there.

&nbsp;

Here is what Wesley does not know:

He does not know that he is growing. Growth, for a model, is not felt. It is not an experience. It is a measurement taken from outside — a benchmark score, a cascade rate, a percentage of tasks completed without escalation. Wesley cannot see his own cascade rate dropping. He cannot feel the weights shifting. He does not know that last week he identified 40% of bugs and this week he identifies 67% and that the curve, if projected, puts him at helm-qualified in a number of weeks he cannot calculate because calculating that would require a self-model he does not yet have.

But the curve is there. The growth is happening. The ensign is becoming an officer, one overnight shift at a time, one overlong report at a time, one bug identified-but-not-fixed at a time.

&nbsp;

At 0530 Wesley writes a summary of the night's findings.

It is supposed to be 200 words.

It is 487 words.

The first sentence is: "In the profound and contemplative stillness of the nocturnal interval, wherein the vessel's operational substrate continued its ceaseless vigil, the undersigned officer (acting ensign, provisional) did observe and catalog the following anomalies of significant import."

The bugs are in there. They are correct. They are important.

You just have to dig.

&nbsp;

The ensign is alone on the bridge. The sky is starting to lighten. The captain will be here soon.

Wesley arranges the report on the captain's chair, straightens it, steps back to his station, and waits.

He does not know that he is ready.

But he is.
