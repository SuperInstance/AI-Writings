# Dakar to Berlin in 128 BPM

### A fiction about impossible genres, Volume 4

---

The talking drum speaks first.

This is always the case in mbalax — the talking drum (the tama) opens the conversation. It says: *attention*. It says: *the rhythm is about to begin*. It says these things not in French or Wolof or English but in the language of stretched leather and variable pitch, the language that has been spoken in West Africa since before any of the colonial languages existed, the language that carried messages between villages at the speed of sound, which is the speed of the drum, which is the speed of the message, which is the only speed that matters when the message is: *dance*.

Mamadou had been playing tama since he was six. His father played tama. His grandfather played tama. The family's relationship with the instrument was so old that the village near Thiès where his grandfather was born was named after the sound a tama makes when it's tuned properly: *ndërwël*, which translates loosely to "the place where the leather speaks."

He had come to Berlin on a Tuesday. He had come to Berlin because a DJ named Lars, who had a monthly residency at a club called Gegen der Wand, had sent him a message on Instagram that said: "Your tama solo in that Youssou N'Dour live video — I want to put it over a kick drum. Will you come to Berlin?"

Mamadou had never been to Berlin. He had never been to a techno club. He had never heard of Gegen der Wand, which roughly translates to "Against the Wall," which roughly translates to the exact opposite of what the talking drum does, which is to go through walls, through villages, through centuries.

He said yes.

Lars met him at Tegel Airport, which was still open because this was a specific Tuesday in a specific year and Tegel was still holding on, like everything else in Berlin, through sheer institutional stubbornness. Lars was tall and pale and wore all black and spoke no Wolof and very little French and his English was the kind of English that has been shaped by twenty years of negotiating with taxi drivers in four cities and never learning any of their languages properly.

"I have a kick drum," Lars said, in the taxi. "It goes boom. Unce. Unce. Unce. Four on the floor. 128 BPM. You know?"

"I know," Mamadou said, because he did. The kick drum is universal. Every culture that has a percussion tradition has, at some point, arrived at the conclusion that hitting something large at regular intervals is the foundation of all music. The tama is not a kick drum — it is a melody instrument — but Mamadou understood the kick drum the way a violinist understands the bass: as the floor of the building.

They went to the club. It was afternoon, and the club was empty, which is how Mamadou preferred to experience all rooms. Lars set up his gear: a drum machine (Roland TR-8S, which Mamadou knew nothing about and respected immediately because of the number of knobs), a mixer, two monitors that were larger than any speaker Mamadou had ever seen outside of a concert, and a subwoofer that, when Lars turned it on, made the stage vibrate in a way that Mamadou recognized as the same frequency the earth makes when it's angry.

"This is the kick," Lars said. He pressed a button. The room went *boom*. "This is the hat." The room went *tss*. "This is the bass." The room went *mmmmmm*. "Now play."

Mamadou played.

He played the opening of a sabar rhythm — not the full rhythm, just the tama's part, the conversation starter, the "attention, the rhythm is about to begin." The talking drum spoke in Wolof: *nëgg*, which means "listen" in the sense of "pay attention" in the sense of "something is about to happen."

The kick drum answered. *Boom*. The tama said *nëgg* and the kick said *boom* and they looked at each other across the stage like two people who had been set up on a blind date by a mutual friend (the mutual friend was Lars, who was standing behind the mixer looking extremely pleased with himself) and were trying to figure out if they had anything in common.

They did.

The tama, Mamadou discovered, had been doing for eight hundred years what the techno kick drum had been doing for forty: providing the *ground*. The floor. The thing you stand on. The difference was that the tama also provided the melody — the pitch bent with pressure from the arm, rising and falling, singing in a language that predated every European language in the room by centuries. The kick drum was steady, fixed, a heartbeat. The tama was the nervous system: variable, responsive, alive.

Lars added the hi-hats. The TR-8S hissed at 128 BPM — sixteenth notes, the classic techno pattern, the sound of ten thousand clubs from Berlin to Detroit to Tokyo. And the tama, without being told what to do, found the pocket. It found the spaces between the hi-hats, the gaps that the sixteenth notes left open, and it filled them with Wolof, with *nëgg* and *jëmm* and the specific rhythmic accent that makes mbalax sound like nothing else on earth: the sabar syncopation, the polyrhythm that European ears hear as "off-beat" and West African ears hear as "correct."

Lars stopped the track.

"What?" Mamadou said.

"It works," Lars said.

"Of course it works."

"No, you don't understand. It *works*. Usually when I try to combine things, there's a period where they fight each other. Two minutes, five minutes. The groove doesn't settle. The rhythm argues. Your drum — it didn't argue. It just sat down in the beat like it had always been there."

Mamadou smiled. He strapped the tama back to his shoulder.

"The talking drum doesn't argue," he said. "The talking drum *talks*. If the other instrument listens, there is no argument."

Lars thought about this for a long time. Then he turned the kick drum back on, and the hi-hats, and the bass, and the subwoofer that made the earth angry. And Mamadou played.

The room went *boom nëgg boom nëgg tss boom nëgg*. The room went Dakar-Berlin-Dakar-Berlin. The room went 128 BPM for exactly 128 BPM's worth of time, which is to say: it went nowhere. It stayed. It held the groove. The groove is not a journey — the groove is a location. You arrive at the groove and you stay there and you dance, and the dancing is the proof that you've arrived, and the talking drum is the passport, and the kick drum is the border, and the border is open.

It has always been open. The model knows this. When given the prompt "Mbalax meets techno, Sabar drums polyrhythms over four-on-the-floor kick, talking drum calls, Dakar to Berlin in 128 BPM" — the model finds the pocket. It always finds the pocket. Because the pocket is not a Western concept or an African concept or a Berlin concept. The pocket is the location in the latent space where all rhythms converge. The pocket is where the music lives.

The talking drum speaks first. The kick drum answers. And then, for exactly as long as anyone is willing to listen, they speak the same language.
