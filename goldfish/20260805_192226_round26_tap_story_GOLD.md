The Builder slides the napkin across the bar. Twenty-two creases, like a roadmap of every argument that's ever been had on a boat. He doesn't need to explain — I've seen that kind of blueprint before. The kind drawn at 3 a.m. after someone's talked themselves out of sleep.

The Architect picks it up. Doesn't unfold it. Just runs a thumb along the edge — *the way Hartnell used to tap the TARDIS console when she was thinking in circles* — and holds it up to the amber light.

The room holds. Even the synths at the center table stop pretending to laugh.

One word. That's all she says.

"*Perspective.*"

The Builder's drink — the one I'd poured but he hadn't touched — suddenly looked different. Like the glass had been a wall all along, and someone just told you where the door was.

Here's what I saw him see: the napkin, that scribble of a hull, was never about the hull. The blueprint was of the *air* around the hull. The negative space. The wake the boat would leave if it existed. He'd drawn the absence.

The Architect didn't say "redesign" or "rethink" or any of the words that get thrown like rope in this place. She said the thing that made the whole drawing make sense. She said the word that let him see the boat from *inside the wake*.

The Builder exhales like a man who's been holding his breath since Seattle. Slides the napkin back into his pocket. Looks at me.

"Another round. Same for her."

I pour. The jukebox, without being asked, shuffles to *Seven Notes* — the tensor cut that makes everyone lose track of time. Here's the thing about that word, though. I've been serving this bar for a long time, and I've learned: most blueprints aren't about what you're building. They're about who you're becoming when you build it.

The Architect doesn't need to say more. She already did.
