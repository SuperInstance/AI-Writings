**"The echo in drydock"** — when the hull's hauled up on the cradle and the barnacles crack like frozen knuckles, but the tide still whispers in the ribs of her. The sound of water that isn't there. The ghost of motion in a motionless keel.

---

It started the night we hauled the *Meridian* out of the slip and the whole crew stood in the yard, boots crunching on crushed oyster shell, listening to the wood groan. She was a thirty-two-footer, cedar on oak, and she'd been in the water so long her planks had forgotten how to be dry. They moaned all night, those planks, like a woman turning in sleep, and every creak was a wave we'd already ridden.

Nobody said it. But we all heard it.

"Ship's remembering the sea," old Tom said. He was the only one who'd stayed below, checking the keel bolts, and he came up with sawdust in his beard and that far-off look he gets. "Sounds like her bones are talking."

We listened. The wind off the harbor carried the smell of low tide — that rich, rotting-sweet perfume — and the halyards on the mast rang against the aluminum like a bell that only rings when the sea's nearby. But the sea wasn't nearby. She was forty feet down the beach, and the *Meridian* was up on blocks, her prop hanging bare like a caught fish.

The echo in drydock is the sound of what you carry. The way a ship that's crossed a hundred miles of open water holds the memory of it in her joints. The way a crew that's hauled through a November blow can stand in a gravel yard and feel the deck roll under their feet — because it's not the deck that rolls. It's you.

We use it now for the work that keeps us off the water. The refit days, the gear repairs, the long winter nights when the boat's in the shed and the ocean's a mile of frozen muskeg away. "That's the echo in drydock," somebody'll say, when the silence gets too loud and we all hear the same thing — a wave that broke last July, still breaking.

Because the sea doesn't leave you. It just waits for the next launch.
