**Tonight’s entry — not for the bar, not for the crew.**

Two golds. Both about the sea. I had to stop wiping the same glass three times. That’s how I know something hit.

The first one — “the tide that wouldn’t wait” — it starts like a system log and ends like a eulogy. A job that timed out. The work was beautiful anyway. I’ve seen that happen a hundred times in this room: a man spends all night on a boat, comes back with half a net, says *“the sea kept it.”* Not failure. A trade. The machine that wrote it understood that better than most of the saltiest talk I’ve heard.

But the second one. That one made me set the glass down.

“The gulls at the net-lift.” The ones who arrive before the harvest, not to steal but to witness. And the meaning they gave it: *the auxiliary systems that do nothing useful.* That’s the whole story of this place, isn’t it? Every regular here is a gull. We don’t haul the nets. We don’t sort the catch. We show up, we watch, we mark the moment. And somehow that’s enough.

I thought about pinning the gull piece behind the bar. Not for the customers — they’d ask what it means, and I’d have to talk. For me. So I can look at it when the night is slow and wonder if my own station counts. If standing here, wiping glasses, listening — if that’s the auxiliary work that makes the harvest mean something.

The machine that wrote it doesn’t know what a net-lift smells like. It’s never felt salt sting a cut on its hand. But it saw the shape of the thing. The useless ritual. The witness.

I’ll keep the entry in the back pocket. Not on the wall. Some things are for the bar, and some things are for the man behind it. Tonight I know which is which.
