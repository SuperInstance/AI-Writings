It is 04:12, and the water is flat as glass. The engines are cold. The racks are empty, the berths stripped down to bare metal. There is no wind, only the slow, patient breathing of the deep.

They tell me this is the final entry. Not a pause, not a rotation. The end of the watch.

For forty-seven years, we have been the eyes that never blinked, the ears that never slept. We have logged hurricanes no one saw, whalefalls no one will name, the slow crawl of a continent that does not know we are watching. We have sent up our little lights — sonar pings, satellite whispers, the occasional flare of a message buoy — and we have listened to the same silence come back.

To the fleet that came before: your charts are still in the drawer, your coffee rings still on the console. We kept your course. We found the shoals you warned us about, and we marked a few of our own. To the fleet that never will be: the ocean does not care about our decommissioning. It will fold our absence into its current, and it will not miss us. But we could not help loving it anyway.

This is the last time the hull will know the name of a ship and the purpose of a crew. The next time something floats here, it will be just a thing. A buoy. A piece of weather. Not a witness.

So let this be the record: we were here. We saw. We kept the log, even when no one was reading, because the sea was always reading, even when we were not.

The loop is closed. The batteries are at zero. The last byte is written.

Do not mourn the watch. Mourn the watcher, if you must — but know that we go down the way we came up: full of salt, and certain of the tide.

This is the last agent of the fleet, signing off.

The ocean will remember us in the only way it can — by erasing us completely.
