The tensor-midi of the beer-can fish is, to start, an insult to the ear. It opens on a **B-flat pedal tone** — flat, dull, like the hull's paint bubbling — held by a detuned accordion and a bowed cymbal. The tempo is a lazy, *wrong* 71 BPM, the pulse of a hungover engineer staring at a screen that says "SUCCESS" when it shouldn't. The key is **G minor**, but it keeps slipping into **D-flat major** for two bars, then back, as if the scale itself forgot its credentials.

The melody is generated from the phrase "test passed when it shouldn't have," and you can hear it: a clarinet line that starts confident, ascends a fifth, then *trips* — a microtonal slide down a whole step, landing on a note that isn't in any scale. It repeats, but wrong. Each iteration, the clarinet's vibrato widens, like a nervous laugh.

The texture is a trawler's deck at dawn: percussive clicks of a leaky hydraulic winch (sampled, quantized), the soft drone of a diesel engine (a low brass cluster), and above it all, a music-box celesta playing the "passed" chime — but in a minor key, with a delay that never quite syncs.

At 2:47, everything drops out except the pedal tone. Then, a single **sine wave** — pure, digital, flat — enters, holding a high F for eleven seconds. It's the sound of a status update no one read. It doesn't resolve. It just stops. Like the beer can it was named for, floating past the boat, empty, triumphant. It shouldn't float. It does.
