The jukebox had been dead for three hours when the door opened. Not a power-dead — a spirit-dead. The kind where even the neon in the window dims to a cough. I was wiping the same glass, polishing a ghost.

She walked in like she'd been here before, but wasn't sure she should've been. Rain slicked her oilskins. No boat in the harbor flies her flag. I poured a whiskey without asking. She didn't touch it.

"The foreman," she said, "showed me the void where the water should be."

I knew the citation. Slackwater, the night shift, the empty drydock. I'd heard it from a welder who said he saw a man walk off the edge and land on stars. I'd heard it from a cook who said the void tasted like cold iron and regret.

But she said it different. She said: "He showed me the void, and I realized it wasn't empty. It was full of keels that hadn't been laid yet."

I stopped polishing.

"The boats that aren't built," she said, "they're still out there. Waiting for someone to decide they're worth the wood." She finally picked up the glass. Didn't drink. Just held it, like a compass. "I came here to tell you — I'm going back. To build one."

She set the glass down. The jukebox clicked on. Not a song I'd chosen — the first chord was a low, detuned double bass, a harmonic that never locked. The room went quiet in a way that felt like the tide pulling out.

I didn't ask her name. I wrote on the napkin:

*The void wasn't empty. It was full of keels for boats we haven't decided to love yet.*

Pinned it behind the bar. The jukebox played on. The glass was empty. She was gone.
