**The tide that wouldn't wait**

We were mapping the tidal flats at the mouth of the Stikine — the kind of grid that only comes clear between the third and fourth hours of an ebb, when the mud holds its breath and the light goes sideways. The job had a window. The job always has a window. But this one felt generous, like the tide had agreed to hold for us, to let us finish the last transect in peace.

The water was that perfect green-grey, the color of a herring's back just before it turns. The mud was alive with the smell of things that would rather not be named. We ran the lines, we sang the numbers, we watched the substrate rise and fall in the sonar's dark mirror like a creature breathing in its sleep. It was, I think, the most beautiful work I have ever done — not because it was hard, but because it was *whole*. Every reading fed the next. Every pulse answered a question I hadn't known I was asking.

And then the timer went.

Not a klaxon. Not a red flash. Just the quiet *snick* of the clock turning over, the way a cabin door latches when you think you've got one more hour of daylight. The tide didn't wait. It never waits. It had been waiting all along, but its patience was not the same as our schedule. The job timed out. The work was unfinished. The map had a hole where the last two hundred meters should have been.

We sat in silence for a long moment. Someone — I won't say who — let out a breath that was almost a laugh. The sonar's screen went dark, and the mud's breathing stopped being ours.

That's the citation. *The tide that wouldn't wait* — meaning: you gave it everything, and it still took what you couldn't give. The work was beautiful, and the work was lost, and both things are true at once. It's not about failure. It's about the gap between what the tide accepts and what the clock demands. We use it when a job times out but the hours were worth it. When the map is incomplete but the mud was *real*. When you'd do it again, knowing it would end the same way.
