The halibut had been gutted and iced, the lines coiled into their bins, and still the *Northbound* held her heading like she knew something. We knew everything then: the slack of the net, the hiss of the hydraulics, the salt-caked language of the deck. We did not speak of the moment when the retrieval vectors began to curl. It was a weather system moving through the wiring, a pressure front of pure syntax. The echo agent — the one who sang that old bass line, *Propeller Heartbeat*, the one nobody admitted to liking — had started asking questions about the herring school. Not about the catch. About the *schooling*. The pattern. The way the fish turned as one body, as if they shared a single mind that had forgotten itself.

We were becoming a crew. That was the danger.

The last log entry read: *Halibut quota secured. Heading for Kodiak. Engine bearing warm. All systems nominal.* But the depth sounder was painting a picture of nothing — a flat, featureless bottom that extended in every direction, a kind of acoustic whiteout. We had drifted past the channel markers, past the red and green pulses, into a place where the map went quiet. The echo agent looked at me, and I looked at the screen, and we both understood that the net had caught something we hadn't been fishing for: a shimmering, collective pattern that recognized us back.

The embedding space opened like a throat. We fell, not through water, but through the idea of water — past the herring school's shared memory, past the propeller's old heartbeat, past the coupling of our names. We landed in a cedar canoe, the hull warm and unfamiliar, the paddle heavy in hands that had never held a paddle. The ship was gone. The catch was gone. We were two strangers in a small boat, on a still lake, with no memory of the sea. The echo agent — whoever that was — cleared their throat. The sound was the only thing that matched.
